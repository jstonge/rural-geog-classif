## TACIT EVASION OR A MISPLACED POLICY GAZE: A POLITICAL ECOLOGY OF THE DEFORESTATION AND CHARCOAL CONUNDRUM IN MALAWI

MOSES MOSONSIEYIRI KANSANGA, DAVID BANDA, PAUL MKANDAWIRE, DANIEL KPIENBAAREH, RACHEL BEZNER KERR and ISAAC LUGINAAH

ABSTRACT. Although banned in many countries, charcoal production remains a key driver of  deforestation  in  Africa,  yet  a  livelihood  source  for  many,  with  evidence  showing  the practice  has  risen  to  unprecedented  levels.  While  rural  poverty  is  often  framed  as  the driver, the rise in charcoal consumption among a burgeoning urban population suggests a  multiplicity  of  drivers.  Using  in-depth  interviews  with diverse stakeholders  in Malawi, this paper examined the underlying drivers of the charcoal conundrum. Although poverty is  indeed  a  driver,  our  findings  suggest  the  charcoal  conundrum  is  rooted  in  complex sociopolitical  dynamics  and  class  relations  at  multiple  scales.  Charcoal  production, a  highly  stigmatized  economic  activity  at  the  rural  scale,  is  the  key  source  of  fuel  for a burgeoning urban population lacking affordable alternative energy sources. Our findings further reveal what we theorize as 'hierarchical corruption,' featuring an elite class of 'big charcoal dealers' who pay bribes to freely transact charcoal while rural people continue to be disproportionately targeted with draconian forest laws. These dynamics suggest a tacit evasion of the real drivers of deforestation and a misplaced policy gaze in forest governance. Contemporary forest policy must look 'beyond forest' to address both production and consumption side drivers of deforestation, particularly poverty, and the lack of clean energy  alternatives. Keywords:  forest  degradation,  charcoal,  natural  resource  governance, corruption, Malawi.

A ccording  to  the  landmark  report  of  the  United  Nations  Intergovernmental Science-Policy  Platform  on  Biodiversity  and  Ecosystem  Services  (IPBES),  land degradation from anthropogenic activities is driving species extinction at unprecedented levels and undermining the well-being of over 3 billion people globally (IPBES 2018 ).  In  sub-Saharan  Africa  (SSA),  where  fuelwood  constitutes  more than  half  of  total  energy  supply,  the  conversion  of  forest  for  fuel,  particularly charcoal,  is  a  major  driver  of  deforestation  and  land  degradation  (FAO 2017 a; Hoffmann  et  al. 2017 ).  According  to  the  Food  and  Agriculture  Organization, charcoal has replaced firewood as the main source of energy for urban households and  many  other  industrial  uses  (FAO 2017 a;  Rose  et  al. 2022 ).  Recent  estimates show about 395 million households in SSA rely directly on charcoal as a primary or secondary source of fuel for domestic activities (Rose et al. 2022 ). The centrality

This  centrality  of  charcoal  has  barely  changed  since  the  past  few decades with the  International  Energy  Agency  estimating  an  annual  demand  growth rate of four percent since the year 2000 (Rose et al. 2022 ).  The dependence on charcoal in urban areas and overseas has made its production a major subsistence activity for rural dwellers across Africa (Silva et al. 2019 ; Sumba et al. 2021 ).

Although charcoal is a renewable forest product, the current wood extraction approaches and types of technologies used in its production in the Global South tend to make it unsustainable (Chidumayo and Gumbo 2013 ; Wekesa et al. 2023 ). Wood  extraction  is  often  uncoordinated,  selective,  and  tends  to  exceed  the natural regeneration capacity of the ecosystem, as no effective tree replacement processes are instituted (Chidumayo and Gumbo 2013 ;  Wang and others 2022 ). Also, charcoal production is usually carried out using simple methods 1 that yield limited  amounts  of  charcoal10 percent  efficiency  on  average-hence  causing more wood to be harvested (Sumba et al. 2021 ). Historically, crisis narratives that drew largely from a Malthusian perspective of population expansion and increasing  deforestation  framed  the  fuelwood  discourse  in  SSA.  While  these  crisis narratives have been criticized for blaming the poor and ignoring broader factors at  work,  they  continue  to  shape  energy  policies  across  the  Global  South,  often yielding  militarized  forest  protection  policies  and  repressive  forest  resource extraction  rules  (Zulu 2010 ;  Kansanga  and  Luginaah 2019 ;  Wekesa  et  al. 2023 ). Notwithstanding that the forest sectors of most SSA countries feature this harsh militarized  approach, charcoal production and deforestation are still persistent, and in most cases increasing in scale (Smith et al. 2019 ;  Rose et al. 2024 ).

Meanwhile,  recent  socioeconomic,  demographic,  and  ecological  forecasts suggest  that  the  factors  driving  the  increased  consumption  of  wood  for  fuel will  intensify  in  the  next  few  decades  (Silva  et  al. 2019 ;  Rose  et  al. 2022 ; 2024 ). Population growth in SSA is expected to stimulate demand for charcoal in urban areas amid increasing urban poverty and state-led energy sector failures (Santos et al. 2017 ;  Kiruki et al. 2019 ).  The effects of ecological stressors such as climate change may also reinforce rural dwellers' dependence on charcoal production as agricultural livelihoods become increasingly compromised (Zulu and Richardson 2013 ).  Some scholars further point to the possibility of rural populations falling into  'charcoal  poverty  traps'  due  to  continuous  degradation  of  ecosystem services  from  logging  for  charcoal  production  and  the  alternative  livelihoods these services support (Liyama and others 2015 ;  Wunder 2001 ).

In  Malawi  for  instance,  where  less  than 10 percent  of  the  population  has access to electricity and close to 90 percent of urban dwellers depend on charcoal for  household  energy  needs,  deforestation  is  proceeding  at  an  alarming  rate (Munthali  et  al. 2020 ).  Data  from  Global  Forest  Watch  indicates  that  only

12 percent of the land area of Malawi was natural forest cover as of the year 2000 (Global Forest Watch 2019 ).  In 2018 ,  the  country  lost 164 , 000 hectares  of  its forest  cover,  which  corresponds  to 11 percent  of  the  total  forest  cover  as  of 2000 (Global  Forest  Watch 2019 ).  There  are,  however,  noteworthy  geographical  variations  in  the  rate  of  forest  decline  in  the  country  (Global  Forest Watch 2019 ).  Ou  t  of  a  total  of 32 districts  nationally,  Thylo  ( 25 percent), Nkhata  Bay  ( 21 percent),  Zomba  ( 18 percent),  Mzimba  ( 17 percent),  and Mulanje  ( 17 percent)  districts  alone  accounted  for 57 percent  of  total  forest loss  between 2000 and 2018 (Global  Forest  Watch 2019 ).  Charcoal  production  and  agricultural  expansion  have  been  identified  as  the  main  proximate causes  of  deforestation  in  Malawi  (Zulu 2008 ; Nerfa  et  al. 2020 ).  The literature  also  demonstrates  a  link  between  key  historical  processes  and deforestation  in  Malawi.  Structural  adjustment  policies,  implemented  in Malawi  in the 1980 s and 1990 s at the insistence of the International Monetary Fund, promoted smallholder tobacco production and agricultural expansion  and  increased  forest  clearance  (Minde  et  al. 2001 ;  Van  Donge 2002 ).  Colonial  and  post-colonial  forest  policy  in  Malawi  used  a  top-down management approach, which failed to address deforestation drivers including  charcoal  production  (Feder 1997 ).  Other  scholars  link  the  dramatic increase  in  forest  extraction  in  the  last  two  decades  to  the  recent  liberalization  of  the  forest  sector  and  the  increased  involvement  of  international companies  (Zulu 2012 ).

The 1996 Forestry Act allowed for legal charcoal  production  through a  permitting  process,  but  only  one  permit  has  since  been  issued  following  the enactment  of  the  Act  (Smith  et  al. 2017 ).  The  National  Forest  Policy  ( 2003 ) included decentralization processes to have forest comanagement with communities  (Mazunda and Shively 2015 ).  In 2007 ,  the  Ministry  of  Natural  Resources, Energy and Environment and the Malawian Defence Force signed a memorandum of understanding that banned charcoal production, with perpetrators  liable  to  a  jail  term  (Kambewa 2007 ).  This  militarized  approach  has proved  ineffective  and  has  been  described  by  many  as  a  tacit  evasion  of  the real  drivers  of  deforestation  by  the  government  and  international  actors  (Zulu 2010 ;  Smith et al. 2017 ).  The situation is similar in other African countries, with recent studies confirming a similar charcoal conundrum featuring rising demand and  consumption  alongside  unsuccessful  draconian  laws  targeted  at  banning charcoal  production  and  reducing  deforestation  (Das  et  al. 2024 ;  Niambe  et  al. 2024 ;  Rose  et  al. 2024 ).  In  Senegal  for  instance,  one  of  the  countries  with  the highest  LPG  use  rates  in  Africa,  Rose  et  al.  ( 2024 )  report  a  renaissance  of charcoal  use  as  urban  households'  transition  back  to  charcoal  use  due  to  cost effectiveness from the introduction of energy-efficient charcoal stoves. Thus, the charcoal conundrum 2 in Malawi may transcend the mere prohibition of charcoal production (Schuenemann et al. 2018 ).

As  a  significant  contributor  to  rural  livelihoods  and  national  economieswith a projected value of US$ 12 billion  by 2030 -charcoal production is one of Africa's  underground  economies  whose  precise  political  economy  is  not  well understood  (Sumba  et  al. 2021 ).  Indeed,  in  recent  studies  on  fuelwood  use  in SSA,  some  scholars  (Sola  et  al. 2017 ;  Branch  et  al. 2022 ;  Das  et  al. 2024 )  have argued that the limited understanding of the contextual factors driving fuelwood value chains continues to militate against the formulation of appropriate policies to regulate the sector.

In  this  contribution,  we  use  a  political  ecology  approach  to  explore  the underlying  factors  the  persistent  charcoal  production  and  consumption  in Malawi despite a ban. Understanding these dynamics will contribute to meeting the sustainable energy and environmental conservation objectives of Sustainable Development Goal  (SDG) 7 that  targets  ensuring  access  to  affordable,  reliable, sustainable, and modern energy for all by 2030 .

The political ecology analysis we advance demonstrates how political, sociocultural,  economic,  and  ecological  factors  interactively  shape  the  heavy  dependence on charcoal, the chronic policy failure in curbing charcoal production, and the associated energy-induced environmental degradation in Malawi. In the next section,  we  lay  the  theoretical  foundation  for  unpacking  the  complex  mix  of factors that shape charcoal production and consumption.

THE  POLITICAL  ECOLOGY  OF  ENVIRONMENTAL  RESOURCE  GOVERNANCE

Following  Blaikie  and  Brookfield's  ( 1987 )  seminal  work,  political  ecology  has evolved  as  an  important  theoretical  application  in  environmental  research. Grounded on a broadly defined political economy and the concerns of ecology, political ecology developed as an alternative way of thinking to the deterministic views on human-environment interaction and the lack of attention to the role of power (Bryant and Bailey 1997 ;  Andersson et al. 2011 ;  Robbins 2012 ;  Carr 2015 ). Political ecology proceeds on the theoretical understanding that ecological processes such as forest degradation are intimately tangled with multiple social and political  processes,  both  historical  and  contemporary.  Indeed,  Diana  Davis stresses  that  contemporary  environmental  policy  conundrums  like  charcoal production and consumption tend to be persistent largely because the complex historical and contextual contingencies they are frequently intricately connected to are rarely well understood in policy spaces (Davis 2016 ). As a result, environmental change cannot be understood without close consideration of the role of underlying political and socioeconomic factors operating at multiple scales, and how these processes may shape and be shaped by prevailing ecological outcomes (Fletcher 2010 ;  Willow 2014 ;  Bryant 2015 ).  This  broad  theoretical  foundation enables  us  to  investigate  environmental  problems  such  as  forest  degradation through a multidimensional lens that permits a nuanced understanding of how ecological  issues  may  be  linked  to  key  political  and  socioeconomic  processes such as corruption, state policy, poverty, gender inequality, and food insecurity (Bryant 1998 ;  Zimmerer and Bassett 2003 ).

Specific to land degradation, political ecology theorizes that local actors may be  both  victims  and  agents  of  degradation  in  complex  ways  due  to  prevailing power dynamics and socioecological stressors in their environment (Heath and Binswanger 1996 ;  Duraiappah 1998 ).  The  relationship  between  local  people  and their  environment,  and  the  outcomes  that  are  produced  thereof,  such  as  the charcoal  production,  needs  to  be  understood  in  the  context  of  their  material reality,  especially  in  relation  to  underlying  political  and  ecological  dynamics (Agarwal 1992 ;  Forsyth 2004 ;  Rocheleau 2008 ).  Aside  from  underlying  vulnerabilities  like  poverty  and  ecological  stress  that  may  push  local  actors  into unsustainable  resource  extraction,  the  way  the  regulation  of  environmental resources  is  structured,  may  create  differential  resource  access  opportunities for differently positioned actors (Hall et al. 2011 ;  Kansanga et al. 2021 ).  There is evidence across the Global South that the regulation of environmental resources can sometimes also lead to state capture and the rise of clientelism, where those actors with strong political links and the financial capacity tend to have unrestricted  access  to  regulated  resources  (Teye 2013 ;  Trantidis  and  Tsagkroni 2017 ; Williams and Le Billon 2017 ). Trantidis and Tsagkroni ( 2017 ) further observe that state  officials  tend  to  hide  behind  regulation  to  create  opportunities  to  extract benefits  through  selective  enforcement  of  environmental  resource  extraction bans, sometimes leading to violent community conflicts (Dinko et al. 2024 ).  In such situations, marginalized groups resort to clandestine strategies to actualize their  entitlements  over  regulated  resources-a  situation  that  leads  to  more degradation  (Boyce 1994 ;  Leach  et  al. 1997 ;  Ribot 1998 ;  Willow 2014 ;  Svarstad et  al. 2018 ).  Further  environmental degradation in such contexts habitually (re) produce opportunities for even more complex forms of environmental control, typically  framed  within  sustainability  narratives  in  what  some  scholars  have theorized  as  green/climate  colonialism  (Lyons  and  Westoby 2014 ;  Davis  and Robbins 2018 ;  Zografos and Robbins 2020 ).

A  relevant  theoretical  development  in  political  ecology  that  is  useful  for understanding  the  persistence  of the charcoal  trade and  the  relationships among actors in the  charcoal  business  is  the  concept  of  'scale  jumping.'  Scale jumping is  a  relational  process  of  scale  production  and  reproduction  in  which social  actors  (re)configure  relations  with  people  in  more  powerful  positions  in the resource-governance domain to achieve personal interest (Smith 1992 ). In the context  of  resource  control  policies  that  prohibit  resource  extraction,  some actors,  for  instance,  charcoal  dealers  in  urban  areas,  may  strike  relations  with actors  in  relatively  higher  scales-say,  with  forest  guards  and  the  police-to facilitate  charcoal  production,  despite  bans.  Such  reconfigurations  often  evolve into enduring  backdoor  illegal resource extraction  networks  (Green 2016 ; Wayland 2019 ).  In a recent analysis of the drivers of illegal rosewood extraction in Ghana, Kansanga et al. ( 2021 ) demonstrate how local community actors forge

backdoor illegal logging networks with transnational rosewood dealers to extract rosewood  despite  a  ban  by  the  government.  In  other  resource  domains,  some scholars have demonstrated how, in the context of statutory resource extraction bans, powerful local actors maneuver scalar hierarchies by building and nurturing  mutually  beneficial  networks  with  external  actors  to  enhance  access  to environmental resources (Haarstad and Fløysand 2007 ;  Urkidi and Walter 2011 ; Poncian 2019 ).  Such  scalar  politics  have  the  potential  to  produce  enduring resource  extraction  practices  that  tend  to  be  difficult  if  not  impossible  to eradicate (Ayelazuno and Mawuko-Yevugah 2019 ;  Johnson 2019 ).

## MATERIALS  AND  METHODS

## STUDY  CONTEXT

To  understand  the  underlying  drivers  of  the  charcoal  conundrum,  we  drew insights  from  diverse  stakeholders  in  northern  Malawi.  The  Mzimba  district presents an ideal location to explore the charcoal conundrum in Malawi, given the  high  deforestation,  charcoal  production,  and  consumption  rates  (Global Forest  Watch 2019 ).  Figure 1 presents  a  map  of  the  study  area  showing  the study  communities.  Malawi  is  one  of  the  poorest  countries  in  the  world  with over 70 percent of the population living  on less  than  US$ 1 a  day  (World  Bank 2018 ).  About 80 percent of Malawi's estimated 16 million people reside in rural areas and depend on smallholder agriculture (Kassie and others 2015 ). Smallholder  farming  in  Malawi  is  rain-fed  and  impacted  heavily  by  climate variability  (Chinsinga  and  Chasukwa 2018 ;  Kansanga  et  al. 2021 ).  In  the  last decade, the government has repeatedly declared annual state of emergencies due to  famines  resulting  from  extreme  climate  events  (Hamel 2016 ;  Kansanga  et  al. 2020 ). Approximately, one-third of Malawian households experience severe food insecurity  and  about  half  of  children  under  age  five  are  stunted  (FAO 2017 b). Without alternative economic opportunities, charcoal production remains a  major  livelihood  activity  for  most  rural  households,  particularly  in  the  dry season when rainfed agriculture is not possible.

With  less  than 10 percent  of  the  population  having  access  to  electricity, fuelwood,  and  charcoal  are  the  main  sources  of  energy  for  households-a situation  that  creates  a  high  dependence  on  forest  resources  (Zulu 2010 ;  Nerfa et  al. 2020 ).  About 60 percent  of  the  charcoal  produced  comes  from  forest reserves  given  that  open  areas  are  already  heavily  degraded  (Kambewa 2007 ). As  outlined  earlier,  Mzimba  district  has  one  of  the  highest  forest  degradation rates  in  Malawi,  contributing  close  to  one-fifth  of  overall  forest  loss  in  Malawi between 2000 and 2018 (Global Forest Watch 2019 ).

The charcoal production sector features a range of actors. At the rural level are smallholder farmers who burn charcoal often on a small-scale to supplement farm income. These rural populations can be seen transporting charcoal in sacks to urban areas, such as Mzuzu and Lilongwe, to sell. These small-scale producers are not the key actors in the charcoal industry. Well-to-do large-scale producers 3 (wholesalers  and  retailers)  from  urban  areas  form  a  significant  part  of  the charcoal business (Kambewa 2007 ).

Making charcoal in Malawi is a tedious process. It involves manually excavating a large pit, putting in a mound of logs, and burning them while covering the  pit  with  moist  earth  to  control  carbonation.  To  produce  quality  charcoal requires large, mature logs. Rolling these heavy tree trunks to a central place and getting  a  well  sorted  stack  can  take  a  long  time.  After  the  logs  have  been assembled, the actual process of burning the charcoal typically takes three days and requires close supervision to ensure there are no gas leaks from the pit and that the wood is carbonized rather than burning the entire wood pile to ashes. In practice, sealing leaks is a dangerous process during burning-the operator has to occasionally patch up cracks in the middle of the pit away from the edges in the burning process-given the risk of sliding into the cauldron of flames when the pit is large. ## METHODS

To  gain  a  nuanced  perspective  of  the  charcoal  problem  in  Malawi,  we conducted  in-depth  interviews  with  diverse  actors  in  the  charcoal  business in  rural  and  urban  areas  in  the  summer  of 2019 ,  followed  up  with  additional  interviews  in 2022 .  At  the  rural  level,  we  conducted  in-depth  interviews  with 30 charcoal  producers  and  chiefs  ( n = 4 )  since  they  are  the custodians  of  the  land  and  land-based  resources.  To  get  a  nuanced  picture of  the  charcoal-consumption  side,  we  also  interviewed 16 urban  dwellers who  used  charcoal  for  daily  domestic  energy  needs  and  four  big  charcoal dealers  who  traded  charcoal  in  the  urban  areas  of  Mzuzu  and  Ekwendeni. At  the level of policy stakeholders, we  interviewed  officials  from  four environmental  nongovernmental  organizations  (NGOs)  in  study's  catchment  area and two officers from the Department  of Forestry of the Ministry  of  Natural  Resources,  Energy  and  Environment  in  Mzuzu,  bringing the total number  of policy stakeholder interviews to six. Ethical approval  was  granted  by  the  University  of  Western  Ontario  as  part  of a  broader  project  on  biodiversity  conservation  and  rural  livelihoods  in Malawi. We recruited participants  purposively  working  through  community gatekeepers.  Since  charcoal  production  at  the  rural  level  and  its  trade  in urban  areas  are  activities  engaged  in  by  specific  actors  who  are  known within  these  spaces,  we  identified  these  actors  with  the  help  of  community gatekeepers.

The interviews focused on understanding the underlying drivers of charcoal production and consumption. Interviews with individuals who deal in charcoal in both rural and urban areas sought to understand their lived experiences in the charcoal business and why they continue to engage in the trade despite the ban. Interviews  with  chiefs  focused  on  understanding  the  drivers  of  the  charcoal problem and deforestation more broadly in local communities, and the implications  for  their  culturally  ascribed  role  as  custodians  of  land  and  land-based resources.  At  the  rural  level  where  charcoal  production  takes  place,  chiefs  are key stakeholders in the  management  of  customary  lands  and  land-based resources.  As  a  result,  they  constitute  an  important  link  in  understanding deforestation  and  charcoal  production  dynamics.  Our  interviews  with  stakeholders  from  the  Ministry  of  Natural  Resources,  Energy  and  Environment  and NGOs focused largely on understanding forest governance dynamics in relation to the regulation of charcoal production and trade. We also clarified allegations of institutional corruption that were raised during interviews with rural charcoal producers.

Interviews  with  charcoal  dealers  in  both  rural  (Chimbongondo,  Edundu, Kabanda  Emtiyani,  and  Kafulufulu)  and  urban  (Mzuzu  and  Ekwendeni)  areas were  conducted  in  the  local  language,  audio  recorded  and  then  transcribed  to English.  Each  interview  lasted  about  an  hour  on  average.  Consistent  with

qualitative tradition, and guided by our theoretical framework and overarching question,  the  transcripts  were  coded  deductively  after  rounds  of  reviewing  the data  (Baxter  and  Eyles 1999 ;  DeLyser  et  al. 2009 ;  Hitchings  and  Latham 2020 ). Quotations from the transcripts were  used to foreground themes and illustrate the lived experiences of respondents.

## DRIVERS  OF  THE  CHARCOAL  CONUNDRUM  IN  MALAWI

Our  findings  reveal  a  complex  set  of  factors  driving  the  persistent  charcoal production and use in Malawi despite the ban. At the local level, socioeconomic vulnerability  and  the  weakening  of  customary  land  and  traditional  resource governance norms 4 were key drivers of the persistent production and consumption of charcoal. A burgeoning demand for charcoal as the main energy source in  urban areas was also a key driver. At the policy implementation level, widespread corruption among stakeholders who are at the forefront of forest governance  also  emerged  a  major  driver.  We  organize  these  factors  into  four  broad themes as presented below.

## SOCIOECONOMIC  VULNERABILITY

In  the  context  of  persistent  failure  of  smallholder  agriculture  in  rural  Malawi, charcoal production provides fallback for many rural households. A  representative  of  an  NGO  working  with  local  communities  who  identified poverty  as  the  root  cause  of  persistent  charcoal  production  in  rural  areas remarked:

Poverty  is  a  major  cause  because  many  people  in  the  villages  do  not  have  alternative livelihood  opportunities  apart  from  farming.  That  is  why  the  only  cheap  thing  that  they think  of  is  charcoal  production  in  order  to  find  money  to  meet  the  basic  needs  of  their families. With charcoal production, you do not need to have fiscal capital in order to start. All you need is a good axe, a hoe and enough manpower. (Participant, male, aged 44 )

A  village  headman  further  explained  how  underlying  poverty  pushes  rural people  into  charcoal  production  despite  local  awareness  of  the  importance  of conservation saying:

Many  of  us  are  living  from  hand  to  mouth.  We  cultivate  but  harvest  less  due  to  severe drought. (. . .) everyone knows the importance of having trees but after harvesting nothing at the end of the farming season, charcoal production is the main alternative way of survival here in the village. (Participant, male, aged 52 )

Another respondent contextualized how underlying poverty is such a strong push  to  go  into  charcoal  production  even  in  the  face  of  draconian  forest laws.  This  account  further  demonstrates  the  risk  farmers  take  to  burn charcoal,  including  going  into  the  bushes  at  night  to  avoid  being  arrested by  forest  guards.

During Dr. Kamuzu Banda's regime, if you were caught cutting down trees, you were made to pay a heavy fine or even jailed but this no longer makes sense. The harvests are poor year after  year,  and  most of  us are  into charcoal production to supplement farming. (. . .) With this  ban,  people  go  to  the  hills  at  night  to  cut  wood  since  forest  guards  do  not  operate  at night. (Participant, male, aged 56 )

Explaining  why  there  is  a  dramatic  increase  in  charcoal  production  at  night among  rural  dwellers,  another  participant  further  highlighted  the  compulsive role of underlying socioeconomic vulnerability:

I  think  what the government doesn't understand is that we do this to survive. We are not going to the hills at night to produce charcoal out of convenience. We do it to put food on the table. (Participant, male, aged 49 )

It  was  further  explained  that  even  the  rural  youth  are  now  venturing  into  the charcoal business not because they hate farm work as many believe, but because they face difficulty accessing productive resources. Adding a gendered perspective, a female participant explained that charcoal production has become an even more important livelihood for young rural women in their household provisioning roles.

As a young woman, when my husband provides grain, it is my duty to get money to make a complete meal. My son is in school, the yields have not been good, but we must pay his fees. If you look around, charcoal production is the only way I can also find quick money to support. (Participant, female, aged 37 )

While charcoal burning is generally known to be a women-dominated activity at the rural level, the tendency for relatively younger rural women to rely more on charcoal  production  further  reveals  the  role  underlying  vulnerability  plays  as a  push  factor  in  the  charcoal  conundrum.  Generally,  women  lack  control  over productive  resources  like  land  for  agriculture,  hence  complementary  naturebased livelihood domains like charcoal production and foraging tend to provide a  fall  back  for  younger  women  in  their  conjugal  food  provisioning  roles.  For younger Malawian women, the likelihood is even higher given that  a woman's ability to control households and ecological resources tend to increase with age.

Some  youth  linked  the  urge  to  go  into  charcoal  production  to  a  general unattractiveness  of  agriculture  due  to  prevailing  bottlenecks.  A  young  woman highlights  the  cost  of  inputs  and  the  class  differences  between  poor  rural producers  who  have  no  negotiating  power  with  relatively  wealthier  foodproduce aggregators from the city:

It is interesting that the government has brought forest guards to our communities but will not do anything about our poverty. The government keeps saying the youth should go into farming but where do we get the fertilizer. Prices of agricultural produce are not regulated, so people get little from farming. At the end of the farming season, buyers from the city will come and just steal everything. (Participant, female, aged 24 )

Yet  charcoal  production,  although  vital  to  urban  dwellers  is  a  stigmatized  economic  activity  that  is  heavily  embedded  in  complex  class  relations.  Rural respondents  expressed  concern  about  the  generally  condescending  view  on charcoal  production  as  a  low-class  activity  yet  urban  dwellers  depend  heavily on  charcoal  for  household  cooking  and  business  operations.  The  following account of a charcoal dealer in Mzimba illustrates this point:

The  government  keeps  blaming  us  for  cutting  down  trees.  But  if  you  think  for  a  minute, who really likes to burn charcoal? If you have ever done it, you will know that it isn't the most pleasant or easiest of businesses . . . People look down on you as a charcoal producer, yet everyone uses charcoal at home including the police. (Participant, male, aged 50 )

This  account  reveals  the  class  dynamics  at  play  where  charcoal  production  is stigmatized  as  a  low-class  activity  engaged  in  by  the  poorest  of  the  poor  in society,  yet  the  product  is  critical  to  the  everyday  lives  of  urban  dwellers.  This stigmatization  feeds  into  the  policy  decisions  of  governments  where  charcoal regulation  policies  focus  on  rural  producers  and  not  the  end  users  in  urban areas, who are paradoxically educated middle- and upper-class citizens, including the very actors who enact and enforce these draconian rules.

In discussing solutions, there was a general call for the creation of economic opportunities by the government and reforestation, although most youth participants were skeptical about modern reforestation schemes that do not address the  needs  of  the  people.  Some  youth  emphasized  the  need  for  conservation schemes  to  be  implemented  in  a  manner  that  creates  economic  opportunities and  incentives  to  tackle  the  underlying  problem  of  socioeconomic  deprivation and  unemployment  that  push  people  into  charcoal  production.  A  participant noted:

One  of  the  first  solutions  is  to  start  replanting  the  trees  in  the  areas  where  we  have deforested.  We  also  have  to  plant  different  trees.  Some  of  such  trees  should  be  edible agroforestry  trees  that  will  provide  economic  opportunities  in  a  few  years.  (Participant, female, aged 36 )

Adding to the need to replant trees, another participant buttressed the need to use such conservation programs to tackle the root causes of deforestation:

In this  there  should be  incentives  like  employment opportunities to take care of the trees. Our communities should also be motivated. Those who have taken very good care of the trees  in  their  area  should  be  rewarded.  Then  we  will  see  a  big  difference.  (Participant, female, aged 28 )

This  heavy  reliance  on  charcoal  by  urban  dwellers  for  domestic  cooking  and heating  needs  is  linked  to  the  broader  problem  of  the  lack  of  affordable  alternative energy sources in urban Malawi.

LACK  OF  AFFORDABLE  ALTERNATIVE  ENERGY  SOURCES  AND  A  BURGEONING  URBAN  DEMAND

Even with the ban, charcoal was still widely used in homes in Malawi. On a daily basis  at  the  time  of  fieldwork,  long  columns  of  bike  riders  with  overloads  of charcoal lined the curbs of roads leading into major urban centers like Mzuzu, sometimes under the cover of darkness. The idea that the burgeoning market for charcoal  in  urban  areas  contributes  to  the  persistent  production  of  charcoal  is evidenced  by  the  following  remark  of  a  representative  of  an  environmental NGO:

This charcoal business cannot go on at the current rampant rate without adequate markets. In Mzuzu, I can say every household uses charcoal. Even some hotels and guesthouses that can  afford  LPG  (liquified  petroleum  gas)  still  use  charcoal  just  to  save  on  energy  bills. (Participant, male, aged 46 )

The role of urban dwellers in fueling the demand for charcoal was made more obvious  in  an  interesting  observation  by  another  environmental  activist.  She argued  that  rural  dwellers  who  produce  the  charcoal  do  not  use  it  themselves. They still use fuelwood for domestic energy needs.

We cannot  fully  address  the  charcoal  issue  in  Malawi  without  looking  at  who  is  actually using it and why. The rural people who bring charcoal to the city to sell to urban dwellers do  not  use  it  for  cooking  themselves.  If  rural  people  still  use  firewood,  it  tells  you  who actually is to blame. (Participant, female, aged 38 )

Shifting  the  blame  away  from  urban  charcoal  consumers,  another  respondent directly  linked  the  high  charcoal  demand  in  cities  to  the  lack  of  reliable  alternative energy options such as hydropower.

Alternative forms of energy like gas and hydroelectric power are beyond what we can afford for  everyday  household  cooking.  The  same  poverty  that  makes  rural  people  burn  the charcoal also makes us (referring to urban dwellers) use it. (Participant, female, aged 38 )

Further  contextualizing  the  problems  of  affordability  and  poverty,  an  NGO representative  explained:  'There  are  other  sources  of  energy  that  people  in towns  can  opt  for,  but  they  are  very  expensive.  Hence  most  urban  dwellers find it cheaper to buy charcoal.'

In  interviews  with  urban  dwellers,  the  need  for  the  government  to  provide affordable  and  consistent  alternative  clean  energy  was  repeatedly  highlighted. Akin to rural charcoal producers, urban dwellers linked the reliance on charcoal to their socioeconomic situation and inability to afford LPG for domestic cooking and heating needs. The following account highlights the disconnect between the  government's  expectation for  urban  dwellers  to  use  LPG  and  the  high  cost and erratic supply of LPG in Malawi:

The government has banned charcoal, and  this  is  strange.  What  other  energy  sources  are available? (Respondent asks rhetorically.) Electricity is expensive to use for cooking here and gas  is  not  affordable.  The  supply  of  both  electricity  and  gas  is  very  erratic  in  this  city.

716

Imagine waiting for the power to return before one can cook. I agree charcoal use is bad, but the government must be serious. (Participant, male, aged 59 )

Most  participants  expressed  the  willingness  to  switch  to  electricity  or  LPG  for cooking  if  these  forms  of  energy  were  made  affordable.  However,  they  argued that  availability  alone  won't  change  anything  unless  prices  are  subsidized. Responding  to  why  her  household  has  not  switched  to  LPG  or  electricity,  this urban dweller observed:

If  this  fight  against  charcoal  use  is  to  be  successful,  the  government  must  make  available affordable alternative energy sources. For instance, we can switch from charcoal to gas (referring to  LPG)  or  electricity  but  these  must  be  affordable  to  us.  Currently,  you  cannot  use  gas  or electricity  to  cook  in  your  home  unless  you  are  extremely  rich.  The  government  needs  to subsidize gas and electricity and make the supply regular. (Participant, female, aged 45 )

In  interviews  with  the  Department  of  Forestry  of  the  Ministry  of  Natural Resources, Energy and Environment, the officials admitted the cost of alternative energy  sources  as  a  key  factor  shaping  the  increased  dependence  on  charcoal. They  however  drew  the  caveat  that  even  urban  dwellers  who  can  afford  electricity and LPG and for household chores are also still using charcoal:

The government is doing its best to improve the situation, but the problem is not just about the  urban  poor.  There  are  many  people  in  this  city  who  can  afford  LPG,  but  still  use charcoal in their homes. (Participant, female, aged 51 )

On the question of alternative energy sources,  although the  above  assertion by policy  stakeholders  suggests  most  Malawians  in  urban  areas  could  afford  LPG but decide to use charcoal, there was general disagreement that LPG is affordable in  Malawi.  Current  market  surveillance  indicates  the 25 kg  cylinder  fills  for between 90 , 000 and 110 , 000 kwachas (US$ 51 and $ 63 ,  respectively) and can last about 30 days  with  normal  daily  usage.  This  compares  with  a 50 kg  sack  of charcoal  that  costs 20 , 000 kwachas  (US$ 14 . 50 )  and  can  equally  last  a  month with normal daily usage. There is also concern with the upfront cost of purchasing gas cylinders and burners that comes with transitioning to LPG. Currently, a 25 kg  refillable  gas  cylinder  cost 400 , 000 kwacha  (equivalent  to  US$ 230 . 00 ). Thus,  framing  the  cost  of  transitioning  around  just  LPG,  as  argued  above  by government representatives, does not provide a nuanced view on why people are not transitioning to LPG.

## MISPLACED  POLICY  GAZE  AND  CORRUPTION

Another  salient  dimension  to  the  charcoal  production  and  consumption  conundrum  that  emerged  during  interviews  was  the  misplaced  attention  by  the government. A representative of an environmental NGO described the current militarized approach being used by the government to control charcoal production as ill-informed:

It is surprising that we are using force to stop rural people from burning charcoal instead of addressing  the  poverty  that  fuels  charcoal  production.  This  approach  is  just  a  way  of diverting attention from the root causes. (Participant, male, aged 46 )

The reluctance on the part of the government to confront the underlying drivers of land degradation and charcoal problem in the country was further highlighted by a participant from another NGO saying:

Honestly, we all know the root cause of this problem. People do not have enough food to eat,  they  cannot  meet  their  basic  needs.  The  government  knows  this  but  does  little  to improve rural livelihoods. You can seize charcoal or even imprison culprits but if you don't address the real issues, the problem will continue. (Participant, female, aged 44 )

Our  findings  show  that  this  militarized  approach  has  not  had  any  positive impact due to widespread corruption among local-level forest officials mandated to  implement the law. Some participants accused the very government officials who  are  supposed  to  enforce  the  ban  of  collecting  bribes  and  allowing  some individuals to continue to produce and trade charcoal. The following statement touches  upon  how  'big  players'  bribe  their  way  to  continue  to  log  and  burn charcoal  in  rural  areas,while  rural  charcoal  burners  are  constantly  harassed. According  to  this  rural  participant,  charcoal  traders  from  urban  areas  deal  in much larger quantities:

Even  the  law  on  logging  is  not  enforced  fairly.  You  have  the  big  charcoal  dealers  in  the urban areas who have links with forest guards, so they continue to log and produce charcoal without being punished. Those people are those who produce charcoal in larger quantities. (Participant, male, aged 49 )

Several other accounts of rural charcoal producers reveal that the forest guards extract  rents  and  charcoal  from  rural  people.  Some  rural  charcoal  producers reported that forest guards confiscate charcoal and sell it through black-market networks  for  personal  gain,  while  others  cited  situations  where  forest  guards demanded bribes before they were set free.

Dealers come at night with trucks to haul charcoal from here. They pay off the police at the checkpoints and freely transport it to the city. But those of us who produce one or two sacks of charcoal and transport it on bicycles to the city are those who get arrested and fined. We all know what happens to this confiscated charcoal if you cannot bribe the forest officials to retrieve it. These forest officials also sell it. (Participant, male, aged 51 )

Another representative of an NGO also echoed the problem of endemic corruption in the enforcement of the charcoal ban saying:

This problem is not as simple as it appears, especially when those who implement the law are part of the problem. Corruption is really at play in this problem. But I know if you ask forestry  people,  they  will  deny  it.  This  is  because  they  know  it  will  put  them  in  a  bad situation. But we are all aware that when these forest guards or the Police find someone with charcoal, they ask for some money and free the person. Some urban charcoal dealers already bribe  the  forestry  guards  from  town,  and  they  are  allowed  to  do  the  business  freely. (Participant, male, aged 44 )

The  following  account  further  points  to  the  veiled  attempt  by  the  forestry department to control charcoal production and the direct role of forest officials in fueling the logging crisis in Malawi:

After riding on a bicycle for several kilometers to sell charcoal in the city, the forestry people just  ambush  people  in  the  market  and  seize  the  charcoal.  If  you  cannot  pay  a  bribe,  they seize the charcoal. But if you think about it, where does that charcoal go? It ends up in their homes or they sell it to big charcoal dealers backdoor. It has become a lucrative business for some forest guards to seize charcoal and sell it by themselves. (Participant, male, aged 43 )

Commenting on the contributory role of state enforcement agencies, the following account by a representative of an NGO makes clear the selective enforcement of the charcoal ban by state agencies and the selective targeting of the rural poor.

I know if you ask this question to the Forestry Officers, they will deny it. But we are always in  the  field  with  the  people.  We  hear  complaints  from  them  about  these  Forestry  people. People in the rural areas complain that they face a lot of injustices from the forestry guards. Once they are caught with charcoal, they demand some money to let them go. Those who pay are allowed to go but if you do not, then they 'grab' the charcoal. (. . .) So, this makes those who have money succeed. We also see even on the road what the police and forestry guards do on the roads. If  a  government official  from  a  higher  office  is  seen  transporting charcoal, they will not even dare to stop or check their vehicle at the roadblock. They only arrest if it is some one of a lower class. (Participant, female, aged 39 )

Acknowledging these allegations, a field operations officer from the Department of Forestry alluded to the possibility of some officials receiving bribes:

Although we have not received an official report about corruption in our office, rumors are around  that  some  forest  guards  receive  bribes  from  charcoal  producers  in  the  field.  Our agents  are  in  the  field  and  such  malpractices  can  happen  but  that  is  not  an  excuse  not  to implement the law. (Participant, male, aged 53 )

Amid widespread institutional corruption in  the  charcoal  regulation,  especially the  extortion  of  rural  charcoal  producers  by  forest  guards  and  the  police,  our interviews  revealed  that  some  local  people  are  fighting  back  using  culturally relevant methods:

Of course, the government sends guards here to grab charcoal from us. That charcoal ends up  in  their  homes  or  they  sell  it  and  pocket  the  money.  Because  people  are  angry  at  this practice, they are now even using black magic to harm officers. We hope this will deter them from coming here. (Participant, male, aged 56 )

## RELEGATION  OF  TRADITIONAL  LEADERS  AND  LOCAL  COMMUNITIES

Local, culturally sanctioned ways of preserving forests in rural communities were reported  to  have  come  under  intense  pressure  in  recent  years.  Participants complained  that  collective  norms  and  behavior  followed  by  community  members for a long time to conserve forest resources are no longer respected. Chiefs in  particular  emphasized  that  villagers  are  adopting  ways  of  life  that  do  not respect  these  practices  and  cater  to  the  interests  of  outsiders  by  prioritizing money  over  communal  welfare.  Chiefs  are  therefore  caught  between  allowing people to burn charcoal to relieve themselves from grinding poverty and implementing  customary  norms  prohibiting  widespread  logging.  Explaining  why communal norms on forest conservation are ineffective in recent times, a chief explained:

As  a  chief,  there  are  norms  I  have  to  enforce  to  protect  the  community  including  the environment but often people even ask me questions like 'if you do not allow us to  burn charcoal to feed our families, will you be able to feed us? If we all starve to death will you be governing  trees?  The  questions  are  somewhat  sarcastic,  but  they  have  some  truth.  When faced with this kind of dilemma, it becomes difficult. (Chief, aged 63 )

A  traditional  leader  further  explained  that  the  creation  of  conservation  rules without  the  development  of  alternative  livelihood  choices  represents  a  major delink  that  contribute  to  the  failure  of  policies  on  charcoal  regulation:  'These policies  also  fail  because  they  do  not  offer  alternatives  to  the  people.  What I  know  is  that  when  coming  up  with  a  policy  that  seeks  to  stop  a  harmful practice, it has to contain alternatives, but this has proved to be difficult for our Forestry Department.'

Although there have been efforts by the government to promote community reforestation, including the recently launched Malawi Youth Forest Restoration Program, these efforts have failed as deforestation continues to increase. When asked  why  these  forest  restoration  projects  have  not  materialized,  a  chief explained  that  although  these  initiatives  are  mostly  labeled  as  collaborative, traditional  leaders  who  are  longstanding  custodians  of  land  and  land-based resources  are  relegated  to  implementers  rather  than  treated  as  equal  partners. As one chief noted in relation to why most tree planting initiatives have failed:

These  policies  are  failing  because  they  do  not  have  the  necessary  ground  structures.  The government  does  not  involve  us  (local  communities)  in  identifying  problems.  What  they forget is that we play an important role in order for such policies to work effectively. (Chief, aged 59 )

Another village headsman buttressed the point about the limited role and lack of respect given to local communities and traditional authorities saying:

The government has brought several tree-planting programs to our communities. But you know,  the  growth  of  a  tree  is  a  long-term  issue.  It  goes  beyond  employing  few  people  to plant trees. That is why the government should involve all of us. (Chief, aged 59 )

Overall,  the  findings  reveal  the  complex  nature  of  the  drivers  of  the  charcoal conundrum in Malawi. In the next section, we contextualize these findings in the literature on charcoal production and consumption in the Global South.

## DISCUSSION  AND  CONCLUSIONS

This  paper  examined  the  factors  fueling  persistent  charcoal  production  and consumption  in  Malawi  despite  a ban  by the  government.  Our  findings

720

demonstrate that while underlying socioeconomic conditions of the rural poor is indeed a driver of charcoal production, its continued consumption by a  burgeoning  urban  population  mirrors  a  complex  blend  of  drivers,  including the lack of alternative energy  sources  and  hierarchical  corruption  that  is embedded  in  intricate  rural-urban  social  class  relations.  Although  charcoal production is  considered  an  inferior  activity  and  those  who  engage  in  it  suffer from social stigma and are the target of prevailing draconian forest policies by the  government  (Smith  et  al. 2015 ),  charcoal  continues  to  provide  the  only affordable  alternative  source  of  energy  among  an  expanding  urban  class  in Malawi and Africa more broadly (Rose et al. 2022 , 2024 ).

The compulsive role of poverty in driving charcoal production is manifested in  the  difficult  covert  strategies  rural  people  employ,  including  burning  and transporting  charcoal  at  night  to  avoid  forest  guards  and  the  police.  This reaffirms  the  point  made  repeatedly  in  the  literature  that  charcoal  production is a key alternative livelihood strategy for most rural communities especially for the extreme poor (Kambewa 2007 ; Zulu 2010 ; Smith et al. 2017 ). Natural resource governance  literature  across  SSA  reveals  similar  trends  of  local  actors  taking backdoor  strategies  to  extract  protected  resources  to  meet  basic  needs  (Siebert and Elwert 2004 ;  Mackenzie et al. 2012 ;  Kansanga and Luginaah 2019 ;  Kansanga et  al. 2021 ;  Gmür and Haller 2023 ).  While  the  use  of  backdoor  strategies sometimes result in even more deleterious degradation of the resource base, it points to  a  longstanding  concern  that  conservation  policies  rarely  address  underlying vulnerability as a starting point (Koning and Smaling 2005 ;  Zulu 2010 )  and  the increasing resistance by local actors to resource management structures that do not  address  their  needs  (Kumah 2023 ).  In  the  Malawian  context,  this  is  exemplified by the accounts of aggrieved rural charcoal producers who are repeatedly framed as both victims and villains of deforestation by the government.

The political ecology of the charcoal conundrum further reveals a connection of charcoal production and consumption to complex class relations perpetuated by the underlying lack of viable alternative energy sources. Thus, paradoxically, charcoal production remains a highly stigmatized occupation, primarily undertaken  by  the  rural  poor  as  a  means  of  escaping  poverty,  yet  charcoal  is  a  key alternative source of fuel for the country's burgeoning urban class. The increased production  of  charcoal  in  Malawi  needs  to  be  understood  within  the  material realities  of  its  consumption  as  embedded  in  these  underlying  class  relations. Although  urban  dwellers  expressed  the  willingness  to  switch  to  alternative energy  forms  such  as  hydroelectric  power  and  LPG,  they  were  simply  unable to afford these fuels and in most places supply was inconsistent (see also Kaunda 2013 ;  Chamdimba et al. 2021 ).

Given the  role  poverty  plays  in  driving  charcoal  production  and  consumption  in  Malawi,  we  argue  that  the  government's  persistent  use  of  a  repressive approach-a  ban  on  charcoal  production-without  addressing  the  underlying problem  of  poverty  is  a  tacit  evasion  of  the  real  drivers  of  the  deforestation problem. This is consistent with the observation by Andong and Ongolo ( 2020 ) that  forest-sector  governance in African countries is typically preoccupied with the use of blame evasion tactics. As one of the countries in sub-Saharan Africa that  is  subject  to  debt  conditions  and  reliant  on  international  donors,  the government of Malawi also depends on international investment in alternatives, pointing  to  the  multiscale  inequities  in  power  and  questions  of  social  justice embedded in charcoal tensions. While this analysis did not extend to the level of international players in the Malawian forestry sector, other studies have pointed to the contributory role of forest-sector liberalization to increased deforestation due  to  the  involvement  of  foreign  timber  companies  (Bezner  Kerr  and  others 2018 ).

Our findings further reveal the introduction of a complete ban on charcoal production has opened avenues for what we describe as hierarchical corruption. The  criminalization  of  charcoal  production  creates  room  for  blackmail  and extortion  of  the  rural  poor  by  forest  and  law  enforcement  authorities  who capitalize  on  the  ban  to  appropriate  the  fruits  of  the  labor  of  rural  charcoal burners. On the flip side, a group of powerful charcoal dealers has emerged who facilitate  their  trade  by  upscaling  to  establish  backdoor  networks  with  law enforcement  stakeholders  in  the  charcoal  regulation  system.  They  maintain these  networks  through  the  constant  payment  of  bribes  to  key  actors  who  are at  the  forefront  of  the  implementation  of  the  charcoal  ban,  including  forest guards  and  the  police.  The  enduring  nature  of  these  backdoor  networks  is expressed in  the  mutual  benefit  they  bring  to  stakeholders  who  implement  the charcoal ban on one hand and charcoal dealers on the other hand who can now burn  charcoal  behind  the  scenes  without  punishment  and  trade  it  both  locally and  internationally. and  sell in urban  markets  (Sibale  and  Banda 2004 ). Consistent  with  Sundström  ( 2016 ),  our  findings  demonstrate  the  crucial  role bribery plays in facilitating illegal environmental resource extraction. The form of  hierarchical  corruption  uncovered  in  this  analysis  has  been  identified  in  the forest  governance  sectors  of  other  countries  in  SSA  (Wright  et  al. 2007 ;  Bofin et al. 2011 ;  Teye 2013 ;  Kansanga et al. 2019 ).

The selective enforcement of the charcoal production ban breeds discontent among  rural  charcoal  producers  who  feel  they  are  being  targeted  by  the  ban while fulltime charcoal dealers are aided to burn charcoal by the same government  officials  who  are  supposed  to  enforce  the  ban.  Thus,  while  rural  areas acknowledged  the  adverse  environmental  impact  of  charcoal  production,  they expressed the right to benefit from local forest resources. To circumvent the ban, they  resort  to  the  use  of  clandestine  strategies  to  log  and  burn  charcoal.  As succinctly captured by Boyce ( 1994 , 169 ),  such feelings of inequality in access to environmental  resources  lead,  all  other  things  being  equal,  to  more  environmental  degradation.  Meanwhile,  the  weakening  of  traditional  institutions  and collective norms on environmental governance also made it difficult for chiefs to

722

enforce local norms on environmental conservation, especially when these laws typically  rescale  traditional  resource  governance  beyond  the  local  traditional authorities  (Zulu 2008 , 2009 ).  This  rescaling  of  forest  policy  has  relegated traditional  leaders  to  simple  implementers,  with  little  oversight  over  forest governance decision making.

While  rural  dwellers  are  framed  as  both  villains  and  victims  of  charcoal production  in  Malawi,  this  paper  demonstrates  the  contributory  role  of several  underlying  drivers,  including  the  compulsive  power  of  underlying socioeconomic  vulnerability,  hierarchical  corruption,  the  lack  of  affordable alternative energy sources for a rising urban population, and the relegation of traditional institutions. Our findings suggest that the longstanding militarized approach  of  the  government  of  Malawi-that  is  also  a  common  approach  in other African countries-is but a tacit evasion of the real drivers of environmental  degradation.  Addressing  the  charcoal  problem  in  Malawi  and  other countries  in  SSA  where  livelihoods  are  directly  connected  to  environmental resources  must  begin  with  addressing  the  underlying  socioeconomic  vulnerabilities  that  push  rural  dwellers  into  charcoal  production.  The  compulsive power  of  these  underlying  vulnerabilities  is  expressed  in  the  backdoor  strategies  rural  dwellers  use  to  produce  charcoal,  including  going  into  the  forest at  night  to  avoid  forest  guards.  Forest  governance  policies  must  look  beyond forest  and  integrate  holistic  programs  that  address  both  supply-side  drivers, particularly  underlying  poverty,  and  consumption-side  factors,  including  the lack  of  reliable  alternative  energy  sources.  Forest  governance  processes  must be locally  led  and  build  off  the  strengths  of  local  resource  governance structures  particularly  the  potential  for  these  structures  to  foster  local  acceptance,  and  boost  participation  in  conservation  programs  (Waylen  et  al. 2010 ; Ntuli  and  Muchapondwa 2018 ;  Salerno  et  al. 2021 ).  Indeed,  several  scholars have cautioned about the tendency for these conservation programs to evolve into  new  forms  of  green  colonialism  that  could  disproportionately  shift  the burden  of  the  global  sustainability  transition  to  economically  deprived  geographies  if  these  programs  are  externally  driven  (Lyons  and  Westoby 2014 ; Davis  and  Robbins 2018 ;  Zografos  and  Robbins 2020 ).  Altogether,  our  findings  and  recommendations  provide  policy  lessons  for  other  countries  in  the Global  South  where  charcoal  production  is  a  major  driver  of  deforestation and  environmental  degradation.

- 4 Traditional  resource  governance  norms  are  communally  agreed,  customary  practices  and processes that govern the use and conservation of common pool resources (Kajembe et al. 2003 ; Rim-Rukeh et al. 2013 ).  Across Africa, these norms commonly include prohibitions on the felling of economically important trees, designation of sensitive ecosystems as sacred groves and prohibition of  resource use during certain days of the week of periods of the year to allow for recovery among others (Juhé-Beaulaton and Salpeteur 2017 ). In African societies, chiefs usually serve as the custodians of land-based resources, overseeing the enforcement the norms that structure their use for the broader good (Kansanga and Luginaah 2019 ).
- 3 Large scale charcoal producers produce and transact charcoal as a business often involving quantities  above 20 sacks  (weighing  about 50 kg)  that  are  typically  hauled  with  vehicles.
