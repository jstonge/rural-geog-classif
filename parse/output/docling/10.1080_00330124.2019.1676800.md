## Examining Spatial Disparities of Obesity: Residential Segregation and the Urban -Rural Divide

Heewon Chea and Hyun Kim

University of Tennessee

This study investigates spatial disparities in obesity among different racial and ethnic groups from the urban -rural perspective through a case study of the southeastern United States, a region marked by its prevalence of chronic diseases and degree of residential segregation. Two proposed measures -local/global entropy indexes and a set of the obesity -segregation combined index (OBESEG) -are prescribed to exploit the degree of spatial disparity between counties ' obesity rate and residential segregation in the urban -rural perspective. A strong interrelationship is confirmed between obesity rates and residential segregation, particularly for high levels of obesity and residential segregation, as well as for low levels of obesity and residential segregation at the county level. The findings identify several clusters of obesity -segregation correlation across the region. This urban -rural perspective helps reveal that clusters of rural areas have a high degree of segregation, whereas urban areas tend to create clusters of counties having less segregation and less obesity. Key Words: entropy index, obesity -residential segregation, obesity -segregation combined index, spatial disparity.

M ore than a third of U.S. adults suffer from obesity, which is becoming one of the most pervasive critical health issues in the United States (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC] 2018). Obesity is cited as a leading cause of various negative chronic health outcomes, such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, stroke, liver and kidney disorders, cancers, and mental issues; as a result, it is responsible for diminishing quality of life (Ogden et al. 2014). The main risk factors for obesity include lifestyle, eating habits, and genetics (Ogden et al. 2015). Research has found that different levels of obesity are observed among diverse racial and ethnic groups, particularly whites, blacks, and other minority groups (Cozier et al. 2014; CDC 2017a, 2017b). Recent research has attempted to unravel the relationship between geographical characteristics and the prevalence of obesity to determine whether physical environment and cultural representation affect obesity by creating residential segregation, or vice versa, over time (Caprio et al. 2008; Kershaw and Pender 2016). Residential groups that are heavily exposed to unfavorable socioeconomic conditions and environments often engage in negative health behaviors, which usually manifest as a unique spatial pattern, such as residential segregation or geographic clusters (Finch et al. 2010). Although previous studies have scrutinized the relationship between residential segregation and obesity, few studies have developed appropriate methods for doing so, giving rise to different interpretations depending on geographic scale and representations of obesity data within the context of residential segregation. The reasons for these inconsistent results and analyses are twofold: First, the methods used should reflect the characteristics of obesity issues without careful deliberation on the variability of spatial units and extents. Traditional segregation index measures, a popular method of geographic pattern analysis, do not capture the local characteristics of the region and thus produce misleading impressions, however (Cozier et al. 2014; Kershaw and Pender 2016). On a global scale, most research has focused on the national or state levels as the standard extent of spatial analysis. Although the prevalence of obesity is well studied at the national and state levels, patterns of residential segregation are identified differently depending on geographic scale. Nationalor state-level analysis is often too broad to depict detailed but significant spatial patterns of obesity and differences among diverse groups, suggesting the need for downscaling analysis to the county or census tract level when data are available. Second, current research has focused more on the urban setting than on rural areas, because the former is recognized as a more critical and more complicated means of explaining trends in obesity rate and residential segregation (Whitaker and Orzol 2006; Day et al. 2013; Cook et al. 2017). Considering the dominance of obesity, however, rural areas should be considered a comparative countersetting to urban areas.

<!-- image -->

This study investigates spatial disparities in obesity among different racial and ethnic groups in the urban -rural perspective through a case study of the southeastern United States. This region is selected for two reasons: (1) its greater prevalence of chronic diseases, such as high blood pressure and diabetes, which are linked to rate of obesity, and (2) its lack of diversity amid a notable degree of residential segregation (Hale 2010; Sharma 2014). Moreover, the region has a wide range of obesity rates and experiences residential separation across counties as a result of its clear rural -urban distinction. The study also seeks to develop appropriate measures with which to exploit the degree of spatial disparity between counties in the urban -rural perspective and specify which areas are of critical concern or require immediate attention. The proposed measures are designed to identify areas of interest both locally and globally. The results will improve health outcomes by highlighting spatial disparities, helping health and regional policymakers mitigate potentially negative health disparities between regions, and providing reasonable evidence for the benefits of living among diverse populations. The research consists of three phases. The first phase explores adult obesity rates at the county level in the United States with a view to examining the statistical significance of the southeastern United States. This region includes twelve states, delineated based on the functional homogeneity of their economies, as determined by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA 2018): Arkansas (AR), Mississippi (MS), Louisiana (LA), Alabama (AL), Tennessee (TN), Kentucky (KY), West Virginia (WV), Virginia (VA), North Carolina (NC), South Carolina (SC), Georgia (GA), and Florida (FL). In the second phase, the degree of residential segregation among the racial and ethnic groups of the study area is measured and characterized as clusters. For both phases, we propose a set of integrated measures designed to determine the positionality of each state from a two-dimensional perspective: adult obesity rates and residential segregation within an urban -rural perspective. The main merits of the proposed measures -(1) the local/global entropy indexes (LEI and GEI) and (2) the obesity -segregation combined index (OBESEG) -are twofold. First, the measures enable the different ranges of multiple indexed measures, which are driven by socioeconomic or geospatial variables, to transit multidimensional positionality planes through a process of standardization. In the second phase, the measures classify the criticality of an instance (in this article, county) into four categories by degrees of obesity and segregation. Furthermore, the composition of racial and ethnic groups is used to investigate the underlying relationship between obesity rates and residential segregation. In the third phase, several key impressions for health and spatial policymakers are drawn by juxtaposing these processes into an urban -rural context.

## Background

## Factors of Obesity

Prior research on obesity issues primarily sought to identify crucial factors contributing to, or eventual causes of, obesity. Such research has generally focused on individual factors such as genetics, environment, physical activity, diet, and stress level. Essentially, genetic and environmental factors have long been thought the most important key risk factors for obesity. Recent research by Hill and Melanson (1999), however, identified causes of overweight by examining longitudinal cohort data sets from multiple countries. Counter to common belief, they concluded that genes do not significantly affect obesity but rather that environments conducive to decreased physical activity and limited sleep hours result in reduced energy expenditure, which was identified during the late twentieth century as the main factor in increased obesity. Gangwisch et al. (2005) also confirmed that adults who sleep fewer than seven hours tend to have a higher than average body mass index (BMI). Very recently, Kowaleski-Jones et al. (2017) indicated that walkable neighborhoods affect female obesity rates. Unhealthful eating habits are another factor in obesity: Fast food and alcohol are known to cause and exacerbate obesity. Jeffery et al. (2006) revealed that the frequency with which people eat fast food has a significant effect on energy intake, because frequent eating of fast food tends to create more fat in the body, causing a higher BMI than in those who are physically active and who eat a healthy diet. Alcohol consumption was revealed as another factor mediating the mechanism that stimulates appetite and increases food intake, thus reducing energy balance and increasing the risk of obesity (Yeomans 2010). Sinha and Jastreboff (2013) also cited stress as a key risk factor in addiction, because it can refocus eating habits toward highly palatable foods and thereby increase the risk of obesity. The effects of stress on obesity have attracted the interest of many researchers. Moore and Cunningham (2012) systematically reviewed the effects of stress deriving from social position on obesity, concluding that those who hold high social positions tend to have lower levels of stress and healthier dietary habits and thus have lower body weights than individuals of lower social status. The relationship between socioeconomic status (SES) and obesity has been recognized as another factor in adult obesity. Critical SES factors include income inequality, which affects the very foundations of obesity and provides further complications over the long term (Pickett et al. 2005). Racial and ethnic factors, another issue in obesity research, are related to SES. Cozier et al. (2014) found that experience with racism has a high correlation with obesity and weight gain among African American women. Scharoun-Lee et al. (2009) analyzed the role of racial and ethnic disparity in obesity among adolescents transitioning into adulthood, and Whitaker and Orzol (2006) concentrated on preschool children; both studies focused on the effects of transitions during the life cycle.

## Residential Segregation and Health Disparities

A different perspective involves focusing on place, which provides space for living and restrains the environment on behalf of residents. Various studies have examined the relationship between health outcome and place, with special focus on health inequality and disparities. Macintyre, Ellaway, and Cummins (2002) explored the relationship between health and place, dubbed place effect . Bernard et al. (2007), insisting on the need for proper conceptualization of place, which can contribute to the measuring of local health outcomes and disparities, proposed a theoretical concept of neighborhood based on Giddens ' s structuration theory. Finch et al. (2010) explored the neighborhood effect on health outcomes by examining concentrations of advantages and disadvantages between highly educated and less educated neighborhoods. The concept of neighborhood can be substituted by the concept of residential segregation -an archetypal example of limited environments ' effect on everyday life that is thus linked to residents ' health outcomes. Conversely, the segregation of residential areas is a representation of different characteristics among neighborhoods. Recently, Caldwell et al. (2016, 2017) examined racial and ethnic residential segregation in terms of the health care opportunities available in urban versus rural areas. The findings highlighted race and ethnicity and geography as factors in the health disparity seen in rural areas, indicating that residential segregation can aggravate the accessibility to health care amenities. In particular, certain racial and ethnic groups (e.g., African Americans and Hispanics living in rural areas) are marginalized from health care. According to Massey and Denton (1988), residential segregation is the degree to which two or more groups live separately from one another in different parts of the urban environment. Residential segregation emerges from either voluntary or involuntary motivations (Charles 2003). Voluntary residential segregation involves a group of people who purposefully reside together based on their common preferences and culture -for example, similarities in SES, educational level, and race or ethnicity. The goal of voluntary residential segregation focuses on enhancing residents ' satisfaction. In contrast, involuntary residential segregation occurs when a lack of finances or information drives people to live in an unfavorable region (Bernard et al. 2007), reducing opportunities for economic activity and limiting access to resources and services (Williams and Collins 2001). Ultimately, such adverse conditions negatively affect the health outcomes of residents in involuntarily segregated communities. Cozier et al. (2014) identified racial and ethnic status as an important indicator of SES. Suspicions of a link among racial and ethnic differences, genetic characteristics, and community environment (CDC 2018) have raised questions about the potential relationship between obesity rate and residential segregation by racial and ethnic group, because residential segregation is associated with SES and environment as an underlying cause of obesity in the United States. A traditional approach to segregation measures usually focuses on examining similarities and dissimilarities between two groups, such as white versus black or white versus non-white. Such a dichotomy allows exploration of whether segregation is present in the composition of dominant racial and ethnic groups relative to minorities, but this approach minimizes the significance of other groups that might play an important role in residential segregation. Although obesity has been shown to have a strong relationship with residential segregation, virtually no studies have considered it in a rural -urban contrast. Although most obesity research has focused on metropolitan areas (Whitaker and Orzol 2006; Day et al. 2013; Cook et al. 2017), it has ignored metropolitan areas ' differing levels of urbanization among subregions, as well as rural areas ' differences in living conditions and environment, such as racial and ethnic composition. For these reasons, the southeastern United States is a suitable setting for exploring the relationship of obesity and racial and ethnic segregation from an urban -rural perspective.

## Data and Methods

Adult obesity rate is defined as the percentage of the adult population aged twenty or older that reports a BMI  30kg/m 2 . The adult obesity data set for counties in the United States is derived from the County Health Rankings &amp; Roadmaps database (CHRR) shared by the Population Health Institute of the University of Wisconsin (County Health Rankings 2018). Note that the CHRR data are compiled from various sources related to health, medical, and demographic characteristics and that the baseline spatial unit for the variables is aggregated at the county level. The original source of the adult obesity rate is the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) of the CDC.

Residential segregation data at the county level were collected from the U.S. Census Bureau ' s American Community Survey (ACS) 5-Year Estimates of 2012 to 2016 (U.S. Census Bureau 2018). The data include a cross-sectional proportion of racial and ethnic groups at the county level for the entire United States. For this research, we reclassified the original racial and ethnic composition into four groups: (1) non-Hispanic white, (2) non-Hispanic black, (3) Hispanic, and (4) nonHispanic others, including the subgroups Asian, Native Hawaiian, and other Pacific Islander, as well as some other races from Table B03002, ' Hispanic of Latino origin by race. Note that populations of ' two or more races ' are excluded from this research, because their total population is too small to be applied to the analysis. To ensure consistency of time period for both sets of data, 2015 adult obesity rates were used for this study, because this was the year that best matched the census ACS data set. All data sets were structured to the county level using geographic information systems (GIS). Finally, the classification scheme of urban and rural is made from the definition by the U.S. Census Bureau (Ratcliffe et al. 2016). The southeastern United Sates includes 704 rural (66.2 percent) and 359 urban (33.8 percent) counties ( n ¼ 1,063). To identify any interrelationship between obesity ratio and residential segregation, two methods are used: (1) two entropy indexes (LEI and GEI) to measure racial segregation and (2) a set of integrated interrelationship indexes called the OBESEG with which to examine the degree of geographic interrelationship between segregation and obesity. Both methods were used to identify areas of particular interest (e.g., prevalence areas) based on the magnitude of both criteria. The LEI and GEI essentially measured the degree of segregation of one group over other groups using individual observations or total number of observations (White 1986). ## Results

Spatial Clusters by Obesity Rates

Figure 1 depicts a distinctive pattern of adult obesity rates across U.S. counties using seven classes of standard deviation (SD). The map clearly points to a greater prevalence of obesity in the southeastern United States than in other regions, such as the West Coast or Northeast, with obesity rates in the southeastern United States ( M ¼ 33.6 percent, median ¼ 33.7 percent) higher than the national average ( M ¼ 31.3 percent, median ¼ 31.7 percent). Notably, the rates of the top four states in the southeastern United States, hereafter referred to as obesity concern states (OCSs), Alabama, Mississippi, West Virginia, and Arkansas, are significantly higher than those of other states, considering both the national average and the state average in the southeastern United States, and thus form a core of high obesity within the region.

Within the southeastern United States, the box plot in Figure 2 gives county obesity rates for twelve states, with each box plot displaying the maximum, minimum, and median obesity rate values for reference. Notice that all states except Virginia and Florida have greater median and average obesity rates than the national level, with a wide range of maximum and minimum obesity rates seen in all states, even Virginia and Florida. The presence of upper and lower outlier counties (displayed with black dots) in a state ' s box plot is an indication of the essential spatial disparity of obesity rates among the counties of a state. As can be seen, the OCSs have several extreme outliers that are nearly 50 percent of obesity rate, indicating that spatial disparities in obesity are not pervasive in the southeastern United States but rather are pronounced in select states. Another critical issue is seen when the urban -rural perspective is applied.

Figure 3 indicates that most counties that have a lower obesity rate are in urban areas (blue classes having negative SD), whereas more obese counties are usually in rural areas (red classes having positive SD). With this geographic pattern, five clusters are statistically captured by applying local Moran ' s I (local indicators of spatial assocation) to the range of obesity rates across the counties. In this analysis, the local indicators of spatial assocation was effective at detecting the areas of spatial clusters based on the presence of local spatial autocorrelation (Anselin 2010). First, the northern mountain cluster in West Virginia and Kentucky is a small area, but it has the highest obesity rate (38.0 percent) and the highest rural proportion (78.8 percent) among identified clusters. The southwestern cluster, formed across Arkansas, Mississippi, Louisiana, and Alabama, is characterized by neighboring counties that have extraordinarily high obesity rates (37.8 percent) and by the greatest proportion of rural areas (67.7 percent). The eastern cluster shows a similar pattern of obesity rate (36.2 percent) but modest rural population (61.6 percent). Interestingly, the Appalachian ridge cluster, consisting of Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, and Georgia counties along the Appalachian ridgeline, has relatively low obesity rates (29.1 percent) but 50 percent rural areas. Finally, the southern cluster covers counties in Florida that show the lowest obesity rate (26.9 percent) and have the smallest proportion of rural areas (17.6 percent).

## Relationship of Residential Segregation to Obesity Rate

Table 1 compares the degrees of residential segregation in terms of H for three different geographic scales in the urban -rural perspective. Compared with the H values for all counties, taking residential segregation in the United States (0.18) as a reference, the southeastern United States is less segregated (0.14) but the OCSs are more segregated, both within the region and at the national level. Seen from a different dimension, distinctions among residential segregation rates are evident when applying the urban -rural perspective. The segregation level of rural areas far exceeds the urban level on all three scales. Differences in H values between urban and rural areas in the OCSs (0.08) are much more profound than on other scales. Two findings are worthy of note: First, residential segregation in the southeastern United States might not be of critical concern compared to segregation on the national level -a finding counter to those of previous studies of segregation that have hypothesized or concluded that the southeastern states are more segregated than other areas in the United States (Brown and Sharma 2010; Hale 2010). Second, residential segregation is more accentuated when the urban -rural perspective is imposed, revealing that the OCSs are deeply segregated between urban and rural areas. The result indicates an urban -rural divide in residential segregation is evident in the southeastern United States.

## Obesity -Segregation Disparities with Urban -Rural Perspective

OBESEG quadrants in Figure 5 give an overview of counties ' positionality in terms of obesity -segregation disparity in the urban -rural perspective. For urban areas (Figure 5A), the most dominant quadrant is Q3 (44.8 percent), which stands in significant contrast to the rural areas (Figure 5B), where only 15.8 percent of rural counties fall in the Q3 quadrant. By contrast, Q1 of urban areas includes only 10.3 percent of urban counties, whereas Figure 5B for rural areas is 30.3 percent. The results indicate the presence of a strong relationship between obesity rate and residential segregation rate for the southeastern United States, characterized by Q3 (LL) in urban areas but Q1 (HH) in rural areas, respectively. Further analysis using OBESEG uncovers the characteristics of the obesity -segregation disparity relationship to the different dimension of geographic scale.

## Conclusions

Although possible interrelationships of obesity and residential segregation have been suggested, virtually no previous studies have clearly revealed such a relationship between them or identified areas of critical concern. This study demonstrates that a set of proposed measures in conjunction with the urban -rural perspective and different geographic scales (state and clusters) reveals an underlying relationship between obesity rates and residential segregation. As presented in the results, significant interrelationships, in varying degrees, can be uncovered depending on the method selected. In this study, the southeastern United States was selected to examine the interrelationships, because the region is known for a greater prevalence of chronic diseases and degree of residential segregation. In sum, high -high and low -low levels of obesity and residential segregation relationship are well identified at different geographic scales -the state and cluster levels -from an urban -rural perspective using the proposed measures. Identification of such clusters using these measures has important policy implications, because the identified clusters typically have a single prevalent racial or ethnic composition and bring concerns about health disparities between urban and rural areas. Finally, according to this study, the proposed measures could be useful when researchers explore how unique combinations of socioeconomic factors are explicitly interrelated at various times and geographic scales. /H17039
