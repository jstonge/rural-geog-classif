## Where Do International Students Work after Graduation? Optional Practical Training and the New Geography of Jobs

James Kaemmerer

Kent State University, USA

## Matt Foulkes

University of Missouri, USA

This article examines the geographic distribution of Optional Practical Training (OPT) employment by international student graduates of Missouri ' s colleges and universities between 2007 and 2017. OPT is a temporary employment program that international students are eligible to apply for after graduating with a degree from a college or university in the United States. It offers twelve months of employment authorization, and students can work anywhere in the United States provided the work is related to their degree. OPT has seen remarkable growth over the past two decades, but employment has mainly concentrated in major urban centers. Based on more than 24,000 OPT employment addresses of Missouri graduates, this research shows that nearly 97 percent of Missouri ' s OPT took place in metropolitan areas, most of which was out of state. Within Missouri, OPT employment was regional, with Missouri ' s OPT students more likely to cross the country to work than to cross the state. Most local OPT work in college towns was located on campuses, rather than in the surrounding community. Indeed, the data show how Missouri ' s smaller cities, towns, and rural areas -and their local economies -have all but missed out on the benefits of homegrown OPT employment. Key Words: economic development, international students, migration, Missouri, optional practical training.

T he number of international students studying around the world has grown significantly in recent years. In 2000, 1.6 million students were studying in another country; by 2020, an estimated 5.6 million students globally were studying abroad (Project Atlas 2020). The total number of international students in the United States reached an all-time high of nearly 1.1 million as recently as 2018 before dropping the following year for the first time in more than a decade. Since 2016, well before the onset of the global pandemic in 2020, the number of new international students coming to the United States to study has been declining annually (Open Doors Report on International Educational Exchange 2020).

There has been growing global recognition that international students bring significant benefits to the countries and communities in which they study, not only academically and culturally but also economically. Bhandari and Blumenthal (2011) noted that ' many countries are formalizing the link between higher-education and the skilled job market by implementing policies that encourage international graduates to enter the workforce of the host country ' (11). Within the United States, states themselves are seeing international student enrollments as instrumental in promoting their economic development (Owens, Srivastava, and Feerasta 2011). Indeed, as countries, states, and localities regain control over the coronavirus pandemic, international enrollments and high-skilled international graduates will likely have a role to play in driving post-COVID economic recoveries -that is, of course, if students stay around.

An important subset of the international student migration literature has focused on international students ' intentions to remain in country after graduation or return home (Hazen and Alberts 2006; Baruch, Budhwar, and Khatri 2007; Han and Appelbaum 2016; Ugwu and Adamuti-Trache 2017). For international students studying in the United States, decisions about whether to stay or go are often personal and complex (Alberts 2019) and can vary by such characteristics as country of origin and school attended (Kim, Roh, and de Barroso 2018). Employment opportunities and other career-related factors can have a significant influence on students ' decisions to stay in country (Hazen and Alberts 2006; Musumba, Jin, and Mjelde 2011; Gesing and Glass 2019). Other push -pull factors such as macroeconomic and labor market conditions in both the United States and students ' home countries have also been found to contribute to students ' decision making (Grogger and Hanson 2015; Agbonlahor and Ampaw 2021). Students who do remain often have to juggle personal, professional, political, financial, and regulatory considerations as they try to navigate the postgraduation employment landscape (Pottie-Sherman 2018).

The Optional Practical Training (OPT) program has played a pivotal role in students ' decisions to stay (Farrugia 2016), not least because it offers one of the few legal avenues through which international students can extend their time in the United States after graduation (Grimm 2019). OPT has also been identified as an important, if underappreciated, tool in economic development efforts (Ruiz 2014; Peri, Basso, and McElmurry 2016). Most Americans are likely unaware of OPT, but it has been described as ' the largest high-skilled worker recruitment program ' in the United States (Neufeld 2019, 2). OPT is a temporary employment program that international students are eligible to apply for after graduating with a degree from a U.S. college or university. It offers twelve months of employment authorization, and students can work anywhere in the United States provided the work is connected to their degree. OPT has seen wide expansion over the past two decades. In 1999, 24,857 international students were participating in the program, representing about 4.8 percent of all international students in the United States at the time. By 2019, the number of OPT participants had grown by more than 799 percent to 223,539, now almost 21 percent of the total (Open Doors Report on International Educational Exchange 2020).

There has been relatively little geographic research on the internal postgraduation college-towork mobilities of international students, especially research that treats F-1 international students as a distinct group apart from H-1B work visa holders or other immigrant categories (Gottlieb and Joseph 2006; Peri, Basso, and McElmurry 2016). Despite the significant influence OPT participation has had on international students ' postgraduation employment migration, the OPT program itself remains understudied. Ruiz (2014) was among the first to investigate postgraduation employment patterns using OPT data, finding that, on average, between 2008 and 2012, 45 percent of OPT participants stayed to work in the same metropolitan area in which they attended school. Later, Ruiz and Budiman (2018) showed that OPT employment was concentrated mainly within U.S. metro areas but unevenly distributed -echoing wider clustering trends identified by Moretti (2012). Neufeld (2019) analyzed the effects of OPT participation on economic indicators, including local patent creation and average earnings, finding statistically significant positive impacts on both. Other studies have demonstrated that the presence of OPT participants in an area is associated with lower rates of unemployment for U.S. workers, signifying the power of agglomeration economies (Zavodny 2019).

Research has also demonstrated the power of innovation-based entrepreneurship in attracting highly skilled migrants, and immigrant diversity is associated with economic growth, entrepreneurship, and innovation (Berry and Glaeser 2005; Kemeny 2017). OPT has the potential to deliver some of these benefits to localities that can attract or retain them. Much of the existing research has examined OPT at a national scale, however. Although informative, the extent to which OPT-related migration follows typical geographic patterns of highskilled migration is not clear. Also, little has been written about the prevalence of OPT in smaller cities, towns, and rural areas; nor have there been detailed studies of OPT-related migration trends for a particular state. State policymakers, economic development officials, economists, and academics would benefit from understanding how each state interacts with OPT employment clusters, as well as the OPT migration patterns within their state.

We seek to extend the knowledge of OPTrelated migration. Here we present a study of the postgraduation employment mobilities of international students from the state of Missouri using data from the OPT program. By mapping at the national, state, and local levels where international students from Missouri ' s colleges and universities have found work on OPT after graduation, we seek to understand not only what places have benefited from this postgraduation migration but, crucially, what places have not. In this article we give an overview of the OPT program, followed by a presentation of our findings on Missouri ' s OPT geography, and conclude with suggestions for further research.

## Overview of Optional Practical Training 1

Most international students studying in the United States enter the country on an F-1 visa, which is considered a ' nonimmigrant ' visa in that lawful status within the United States is for a limited amount of time and it confers no path to permanent residency (U.S. Department of State, Bureau of Consular Affairs n.d.). 2 Nor is it categorized along with employment visas like H-1B. Rather, an F-1 visa is a student visa wherein individuals are admitted to the United States for the ' duration of status '-that is, as long as students are fulfilling the terms of their academic program they are considered to be in ' valid status ' and thus lawfully allowed to remain in the United States. While in school, F-1 students stay ' in status ' by enrolling in the required number of credit hours. After the completion of their program, and if they apply and are approved for OPT, they stay ' in status ' by being employed (U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Service [USCIS] 2020).

Off-campus work that is academically related and connected to a student ' s degree is permitted for F-1 students but first requires authorization (U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement [ICE] 2020a). Referred to as practical training, there are two types: curricular practical training and OPT (ICE 2020b). Curricular practical training is work authorization that is connected academically to a student ' s field of study and takes place during a student ' s academic program. It is often used for internships or other on-site training. OPT, on the other hand, is work authorization generally done after a student ' s program. 3

Known interchangeably as postcompletion OPT or regular OPT, it is the flagship practical training program for international students and lasts for twelve months. All F-1 visa holders who have been ' lawfully enrolled on a full-time basis for [at least] one full academic year at a college, university, conservatory, or seminary that has been certified by the U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) Student and Exchange Visitor Program (SEVP) ' are eligible to apply (USCIS 2021b). Students must be enrolled in an academic program to apply, and eligible degree levels include associate ' s, bachelor ' s, master ' s, and doctoral degrees. Unlike other visas such as H1B, which have specific employer restrictions, OPT students might work for one or multiple employers (provided the work is directly related to their field of study); they might change employers at any time; and the work might be paid or unpaid, full-time or parttime (i.e., at least twenty hours per week). Students on postcompletion OPT are even welcome to be selfemployed and start their own business (U.S. Department of Homeland Security [DHS] 2020a). 4

International students can apply for postcompletion OPT regardless of whether they have secured employment, and all degree fields are eligible, science, technology, engineering, and math (STEM) and non-STEM alike. Based on their individual OPT start date, students can accumulate up to ninety days of unemployment searching for a job. If a student does not find a job, or if they exceed the ninety days of unemployment during the twelve months of OPT, the student will no longer have lawful F-1 status in the United States (U.S. DHS 2020b).

This article focuses exclusively on postcompletion OPT, but two federal actions in recent years have greatly expanded the OPT program overall. In 2008, the Bush administration issued an executive order that established a seventeen-month extension to the standard twelve months of postcompletion OPT (U.S. DHS 2008). This OPT STEM Extension is available to students with degrees in select STEM fields (U.S. DHS 2018). Students also must be actively employed on OPT to apply for the OPT STEM Extension (U.S. DHS 2020b). Later, in 2016, the Obama administration issued another executive order that increased the STEM Extension to twenty-four months (U.S. DHS 2016b; Ruiz and Budiman 2018). The 2016 order also grants students the eligibility to apply for up to two STEM extensions -based on separate degrees -over the course of their academic career (USCIS 2021a).

## Data and Methods

A Freedom of Information Act request was submitted to ICE, a division of the DHS, for all practical training authorization data covering all F-1 international students who had studied in SEVP-certified institutions within the state of Missouri from 2004 through 2018 (ICE 2018).

All data received from ICE arrived preanonymized so that no personal information could be identified. The data were then filtered by postcompletion OPT only. The data included address information for students ' graduating institutions and places of employment. Only those observations with both employer information and a full address were included in our analysis. Fields that contained unknown, ASE (actively seeking employment), not currently employed, or N/A were designated as having no employer information and were not used in the analysis. Next, because the addresses were all self-reported, the data contained many spelling variations, which required extensive cleaning to standardize city names. The finished product was then geocoded to obtain corresponding decimal degree coordinates based on employer ' city name ' and ' state ' fields. City and state were deemed the best options for geolocation because they were the most commonly provided address data in the data. For the more detailed street-level analysis of specific metros, each address was individually reviewed, cleaned, and geocoded. OPT year was based on the start date year of each employment record. To accurately code which degrees were designated STEM degrees, the data were matched with the Department of Homeland Security ' s 2016 STEM Designated Degree Program list.

Using GIS software, U.S. Census Bureau (2018) Core Based Statistical Areas (CBSAs) were spatially joined with all geocoded points that fell within a metropolitan or micropolitan area, providing pointcount data within each polygon. Rural areas were coded as any area outside the Census Core-Based Statistical Areas. The geography of the data set was further limited to the continental United States. We selected 2007 as the start year to capture OPT numbers before the implementation of the OPT STEM Extension in 2008. We chose that as the cutoff year because the original OPT Freedom of Information Act request was made in fall 2018 and therefore 2017 was the last year that a full twelve months of OPT approvals could be captured. With these additional filters in place, the total number of OPT locations was reduced to 24,119. This final OPT count included bachelor ' s, master ' s, and doctoral students, as well as a small number of associate ' s degrees and a handful of undefined ' other. ' Unless stated otherwise, the data presented in this article refer to all 24,119 postcompletion OPT employment locations (Figure 1), including all majors, both STEM and non-STEM.

## Data Issues and Limitations

OPT data collected by the federal government provide some of the most detailed and immediate snapshots of postgraduation migration and employment available. Caveats are warranted, however. The data here -and, indeed, in all OPT studies -are to varying degrees incomplete. At best, they are an approximation. In this section we address some of the potential issues and limitations.

All practical training information for international students studying in the United States is entered into the online records system known as the Student and Exchange Visitor Information System (SEVIS), which is administered by DHS ' s SEVP (U.S. DHS 2020a). Individual colleges and universities and their international offices ' staff are tasked with maintaining the records of every international student in attendance at their institutions using the SEVIS system. After finding a job on OPT, students are required to report their employment information, including employer name and address, either to their schools, who input the information into SEVIS, or, since March 2018, through an online self-reporting portal (U.S. DHS 2018).

The decision to exclude OPT STEM Extension data and focus our analysis solely on postcompletion OPT was deliberate. Unlike the OPT STEM Extension, postcompletion OPT is available to all international students regardless of their field of study, and we see merit in understanding where art history majors find work as much as computer science grads. 5

There is also a difference between OPT approval and actually working on OPT. After all, a student could be granted approval by USCIS but for legitimate reasons never end up working on OPT. 6 The original data set of Missouri OPT students that we received from ICE contained more than 51,000 observations. We considered most of these 51,000 as Missouri international student graduates who had at least been approved for OPT. After applying our filters, however, the number of observations was reduced by more than half. It was this final number, 24,119, that we chose to designate as students who had found a job and worked on OPT.

Both our Missouri data and those of other national OPT studies (e.g., Ruiz and Budiman 2018) show a large, sudden spike in OPT numbers starting around 2015. For Missouri particularly, the increase coincides with major enrollment jumps at certain schools at around the same time (see the Results section). More broadly, though, the rise is attributable to a records reporting limitation in SEVIS, wherein before 2015 the system provided only enough space to input information for one employer at a time -and if the student worked for more than one employer during OPT, this space usually went to the employer for whom the student worked the most. More employer fields were added starting in 2015 and, as a result, ' students may appear in the data multiple times if they changed employers. [This] appears to create an artificial increase in the number of students approved for OPT beginning in 2015 ' (Zavodny 2019, 6). Moreover, there was not a strict reporting requirement for employer information in earlier years of the program (Zavodny 2019), but it has since become codified (U.S. DHS 2016a).

Finally, the SEVIS system into which OPT data are entered serves as a regulatory compliance portal for participating SEVP-certified schools; it was not intended nor designed to be used to identify postgraduation employment migration flows. Technically, the OPT data used in our analysis are not showing the number of individual OPT participants, per se. Rather, it is more accurate to understand the data as the available OPT employment addresses listed in each student ' s SEVIS record, of which there could be more than one if the student had worked at multiple jobs. The data likely fail to capture many student employment locations early in our study period, nor can the data easily identify whether there were moves between employment locations. We do not think these caveats jeopardize the overall value of the OPT data set, but we do think it is necessary to acknowledge them up front to more clearly understand what the data can -and cannot -show.

## Results

Most of the OPT employment by Missouri ' s international student graduates was located out of state, and the rate at which Missouri has retained OPT employment in state has been decreasing. According to our analysis, almost 97 percent of the 24,119 total OPT employment addresses of Missouri graduates were located within U.S. metro areas containing 50,000 or more residents (see Figure 1). Of those, 26.5 percent were located within Missouri and its metro areas. Since 2007, however, the overall percentage of in-state OPT employment locations has been decreasing yearly. By 2017 only around 20 percent of Missouri ' s OPT employment locations were located inside Missouri itself. Master ' s students were less likely than bachelor ' s and doctoral students to report employment locations in state.

OPT employment within Missouri was also predominantly metropolitan, although at a slightly reduced percentage compared to the OPT employment found out of state. Eighty-two percent of Missouri-based OPT addresses were located within Missouri ' s metro areas, nearly half of which (46 percent) were STEM degrees. Moreover, Missouri ' s metros drew mainly from their surrounding regions for OPT employment. For example, OPT from schools on the western side of the state, to the extent it remained in Missouri at all, clustered largely around Kansas City, whereas OPT from eastern schools clustered largely around St. Louis. In fact, most of Missouri ' s OPT graduates were more likely to cross the country to work than to cross the state.

Some places in Missouri were more successful in putting local graduates to work than others, with size and economic strength conferring an advantage in retaining OPT-participating students. Three of the state ' s metros -St. Louis, Columbia, and Kansas City -showed local OPT retention rates of 44 percent, 25 percent, and 14 percent, respectively. Here we define retention as the percentage of local employment addresses relative to the national total, either by school or by metro. St. Louis ' s success in retaining OPT students likely reflects its economic prominence in the state: In 2019, its economy accounted for half of the state ' s total gross domestic product (Missouri Economic Research and Information Center n.d.).

The Missouri OPT data at the undergraduate level, though, further suggest that close geographic proximity to labor markets is also beneficial. For example, bachelor ' s degree OPT students in St. Louis were more likely to remain in the St. Louis area to work than their graduate-level counterparts. It could be that being close to -or, for St. Louis students, within -thick labor markets helps make the postgraduation job search less daunting and disruptive for younger bachelor ' s students because they have had four years to gain familiarity with their local community (e.g., transportation, housing, networking, and job prospects), thus lowering barriers to entry for OPT employment.

Missouri ' s OPT data can also highlight the mixed geographies of employment by degree level, revealing patterns among bachelor ' s, master ' s, and doctoral degree holders (Figure 2). For example, master ' s degree OPT addresses were spread across 232U.S. metro areas (out of 378), with New York and Dallas-Fort Worth containing a quarter of all master ' s-level employment locations. Bachelor ' s and doctoral degree holders on OPT were found in 157 and 168 U.S. metros, respectively. St. Louis alone accounted for nearly a quarter (23 percent) of all metro-area OPT locations for bachelor ' s students. This was most likely attributable to St. Louis being home to nearly twice the number of higher education institutions as the next largest Missouri metro, Kansas City, many of which are private liberal arts universities with higher international student enrollments. Indeed, St. Louis ' s relatively large shares of all three degree levels further point to the city ' s success in both producing and retaining a diversity of OPT employment.

Micropolitan and rural areas have largely failed to attract and retain Missouri ' s international students in the OPT program. Table 1 shows how the percentage of OPT employment locations in each geographic region has remained relatively stable over time, despite the yearly increases in overall OPT numbers and SEVIS reporting changes. The share of OPT located in rural areas has remained at 1 percent or lower throughout the eleven years of study. Micropolitan areas have fared little better, accounting for just over 2 percent of total OPT locations by 2017.

The retention numbers posted by schools in smaller cities and towns can also be misleading. On closer inspection, many locally based OPT addresses are located on university campuses. 7 Very few are actually found in the surrounding community. Consider the top area for nonmetro OPT locations: Rolla, Missouri, a college town of around 20,000 residents and home to Missouri University of Science and Technology. The Rolla micropolitan area contained 252 OPT total employment locations. When looking more closely at the reported employers, however, only thirty-two, or about 13 percent, were located off campus at a nonuniversity address. Missouri University of Science and Technology produced 1,801 OPT employment locations nationally, but less than 2 percent stayed in Rolla to work in the local nonuniversity workforce. Only two of Northwest Missouri State University ' s 1,118 employment locations were located at a nonuniversity address in its hometown of Maryville, Missouri. We estimate that the number of OPT employment addresses in Missouri that were both located in a nonmetro area and for a local nonuniversity employer constituted less than 1 percent of the more than 24,000 OPT addresses. This serves as a stark example of the challenge smaller communities face in retaining international students for local employment.

For greater detail, Figure 3 zooms in to the city level to show where University of Missouri international students on OPT found work locally in Columbia, Missouri. According to the data, unlike most other college towns in Missouri, and even much larger Kansas City, Columbia was able to retain around a quarter of its total OPT. Most of the OPT in Columbia came from University of Missouri graduates, and, as the map indicates, most of it took place on campus. A healthy percentage of OPT addresses, however (nearly 30 percent), were located beyond campus, out in the community. Nonuniversity employment clusters are clearly visible in the downtown area, as well as business districts to the northeast, east, southeast, and west of downtown.

Finally, we close this section by highlighting one Missouri university ' s brief ascent to become one of the largest OPT-producing schools in the country. The rise of OPT at the University of Central Missouri (UCM) is critical to understanding the larger story of Missouri ' s OPT employment geographies. Aided by strategic institutional decisions and targeted recruitment, and driven by the demands of the U.S. tech-sector labor market (Redden 2017; Swaminathan 2017), 9 UCM was able to cultivate massive enrollment and OPT numbers relative to its size and compared to the rest of the state in only a few short years (Redden 2017; ICE 2020c). As recently as 2012, 152 students from India were studying at UCM, but by 2015 the number had jumped to 2,429 (Redden 2017). 10 Between its main Warrensburg campus and its newer Innovation Campus near Kansas City, our data show UCM produced more than 7,000 OPT employment locations between 2007 and 2017 -two-and-a-half times more than the next-largest OPT-producing school in Missouri. 11

The data indicate UCM ' s OPT ' boomlet ' helped establish -or reinforce -dedicated migration flows by Indian master ' s students graduating with technology degrees from western Missouri schools to certain metro areas with active high-tech sectors, primarily to Dallas -Fort Worth and New York but also Atlanta and Detroit (see Figure 4). It could be that many students placed a high value on security and predictability: Being able to plan their full U.S. education trajectory -that is, knowing where they would attend school, how long it would take, and how much it would cost and having a high degree of confidence about where and for whom they could work after graduation -helped reduce uncertainty, mitigate risk, and control costs along the migration path and, in doing so, informed and facilitated the process for successive students (Massey et al. 1993; Somerville and Walsworth 2015). More recent data, though, suggest such boomlets can dissipate just as quickly (Redden 2017; ICE 2020c). Enrollment and OPT numbers in these programs have since dropped relative to past highs, in some cases precipitously, due to a number of factors, including increased competition from other university programs, fluctuations in the home country economy, and a reduction in visa issuances in some areas (Redden 2017).

## Conclusion

The results of this study show how one state has received, at best, uneven benefit from its homegrown OPT. For us a major takeaway is less why major metropolitan areas have attracted and retained so many students on OPT and more why smaller cities and towns have attracted and retained so few. It is a story with implications for the rest of the country, a story about how small cities, towns, and rural areas have largely missed out on the benefits of OPT work from the many highly skilled, highly motivated international student graduates cultivated in their own backyards every year.

Missouri ' s small towns and cities have different economic needs and labor market capacities than their larger metropolitan counterparts. In many places, jobs are just harder to come by. What, then, would it take to cultivate and retain more OPT employment in state? Similar in intent to Canada ' s Provincial Nominee Program (Pandey and Townsend 2011), Peri, Basso, and McElmurry (2016) proposed issuing work visas based on targeted geographic needs. Such place-based incentives could encourage OPT students to relocate to underserved regions beyond major metro areas or to stay and work in the communities where they graduated, thus ' keeping human capital where it was developed ' (Peri, Basso, and McElmurry 2016, 19).

We also see the self-employment permission under postcompletion OPT as a promising option to explore, promote, and expand in geographically underserved areas. To that end, the federal government might consider extending the time period of postcompletion OPT (or eliminate the STEM-only eligibility for the OPT Extension altogether). This would recognize that all degree fields have something to offer local economies and that successful entrepreneurialism often takes longer than twelve months. Indeed, the relatively short duration of postcompletion OPT work authorization can often be a barrier for student entrepreneurs and employers alike (McFadden and Seedorff 2017).

In addition, Zavodny (2019) noted that OPT ' gives employers a way to see whether a foreign student is a good fit before trying to sponsor the student for an expensive and scarce H-1B temporary skilled worker visa ' (2). As yet, it remains unclear to what extent OPT participation influences H-1B prospects (Nitzschke 2016), for both students and prospective employers. It could be that international students are inclined toward employment that could lead to H-1B sponsorship and eventually to permanent residency -something the F-1 visa does not yet permit, nor something businesses in smaller communities could easily support.

Ultimately, this article represents a small contribution to a much larger research agenda. More research is needed, for example, to understand the employment intentions and geographic preferences of international students beyond the initial decision to stay or leave the country after graduation, as well as comparing international students ' postgraduation geographies to those of domestic students. Further exploration is needed of the role student agency plays in the postgraduation employment migration space to gain a deeper understanding of and appreciation for students ' unique and often challenging lived and work experiences while on OPT. /H17039
