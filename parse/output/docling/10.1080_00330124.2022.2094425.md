## The Public Land Survey Landscape in the Intermountain U.S. West

## Joe Weber

University of Alabama, USA

Most of the United States was surveyed before settlement with a rectangular survey system called the Public Land Survey (PLS). Surveyors mapped out square townships that were subdivided into one-square-mile sections that could in turn be further divided for sale or homesteading. The system began in northwest Ohio and spread westward through the Midwest and across the Great Plains. Farms took the form of these squares, roads ran along section lines, and county and other boundaries were aligned along them. The effects of the PLS in rural areas are well known; the rectangular survey produced a characteristic checkerboard pattern in agricultural areas and resulted in higher road densities in surveyed areas. The survey is often treated as homogenous, and a ubiquitous sign of federal authority inscribed on the land. This statement is not true in the Intermountain West; although the PLS was applied throughout this area, the extent of the PLS landscape in the interior West is small. There are several distinct types of gridded landscapes distinguished by the presence of agriculture and urbanization. The future spread of these landscapes is likely to be limited, although some will likely shift between types. Key Words: Intermountain West, John Wesley Powell, Public Land Survey, roads, urban growth.

T he American Public Land Survey (PLS) was created in 1785 to rapidly survey the western frontier of the new United States and allow for the quick sale of the country ' s public domain (Pattison 1957; Johnson 1976; White 1983; Hubbard 2009). The system was based on the surveying of square townships six miles to a side. These would be subdivided into thirty-six sections of one square mile, which in turn would be divided into smaller squares and rectangles as needed. The survey was first applied in Ohio before being pushed westward and also appearing in several Southern states. The survey was extended west of the Mississippi River after the 1803 Louisiana Purchase and jumped to the Pacific Coast in 1855 and to Alaska in 1905, eventually covering the West except Texas and Hawaii (Figure 1).

Surveying the boundaries of townships and sections left little direct physical trace beyond survey markers, but a distinct landscape emerged soon after. Perhaps the best known feature associated with the PLS system are roads. In the Midwest and Great Plains, a seemingly endless grid of rural roads can be seen, with roads running straight north -south or east -west (Thrower 1966; Johnson 1976, 1994; Webster and Leib 2011; see Figure 2). Most of these are along section lines, paralleling one another at one-mile intervals. They go up and over hills and require traffic to contend with sharp turns if moving diagonally through the network. A rectangular road network made sense in an area where land was being purchased as squares and rectangles. The spread of this landscape was separate from the spread of the survey itself and might be delayed many decades before appearing.

Despite its ubiquity, this PLS section line landscape was not required by or even provided for in the PLS. It was instead a result of state and local laws (Johnson 1976). Their existence ran counter to good road-building practice of the time, which called for roads to go around hills rather than remain on a straight alignment (Gillespie 1853; Harger and Bonney 1912; Wiley 1935). Engineers sought to bridge streams at locations requiring the shortest spans and curved roads so that they would cross streams at right angles. The abundance of rural roads along section lines became a problem for county highway budgets in a time of shrinking rural populations; more than a half-century ago it was evident that the Great Plains were ' over-roaded ' (Clawson 1966). Closing excess rural roads is a problem for states in this region. Once they appeared, they took on a permanence, not only in the road pattern, but with respect to county boundaries, which often follow survey lines. If roads along section lines adopted as county lines are subsequently paved, it can be said that political boundaries ' are cemented on to the surface of the land through highway improvements ' (Thrower 1966, 117).

Geographers have compared areas surveyed with this system to others or before and after the PLS was applied (Thrower 1966; Johnson 1976, 1994; Milbauer 1997; Hart 1998). In these areas, the PLS has usually been associated with a higher road density in rural areas, even when population densities are higher in ungridded areas. This difference has been observed as far back as 1875, long before automobiles and state highway construction became the norm (Thrower 1966). In the automobile era, the difference between gridded and ungridded areas was less than in 1875, although gridded areas still had substantially more roads. Most of this difference was due to the presence of more local roads rather than state highways, which were distributed more evenly between the areas. These local roads do not appear on many small-scale maps, underestimating the apparent mileage difference. The grid pattern provided for better accessibility, as much less land was more than half a mile (805 m) from a road than with an irregular pattern (Thrower 1966). These roads were less successful as long-distance highways, requiring frequent right-angled turns to accommodate diagonal travel. These grid road systems also required more bridges, as straight roads ignored the underlying drainage system and meant frequent crossings that could otherwise be avoided. These bridges were also often longer than necessary had they crossed streams at right angles. Roads were about twice as likely to run along property boundaries (rather than cut through them) in areas with section line roads than where the PLS had not been applied.

The presence of the PLS landscape in the Midwest and Great Plains is nearly ubiquitous, but there has been little investigation of how extensive these patterns are across the country. Studies in the Midwest might take its existence for granted, but elsewhere its impact is an open question. Johnson (1994) noted that flying west one will see the grid stop at the Rocky Mountains and return when reaching the West Coast, with ' fragments of north -south and east -west lines appear[ing] ' (144) between the Rockies and Coast ranges, but the distribution of these fragments is left unexamined. This work will map this landscape in the U.S. West and apply Thrower ' s methodology of comparing surveyed and unsurveyed areas to examine the question of whether the PLS is ubiquitous in the West and has created a distinct landscape here despite the absence of agriculture or substantial rural settlements in much of the region. This work is significant because it shows that mapped patterns are often quite different from what exists on the ground. It also shows that the nation ' s land survey system is not a relic of the eighteenth century but still an active feature shaping the western landscape.

## Methods and Data

This article agrees with Wyckoff ' s (2014) tenet that ' It is the predictable rectangularity of the sectionline roadscape that most clearly reveals the survey system in the rural West ' (248). Mapping section line roads provides an effective way of mapping the PLS landscape, but this is easier said than done.

Section line roads are not a distinct or meaningful category of roads; the Federal Highway Administration ' s Highway Performance Monitoring System is the nation ' s database on highways, but it does not distinguish section line roads as it does for roads with different ownership, number of lanes, pavement type and condition, traffic, or level of access control (Federal Highway Administration 2019). Geographic information systems (GIS) street networks (whether TIGER or from other sources) include no attributes identifying roads along section lines.

There is no simple GIS methodology that can be used to identify section line roads. Like survey lines, streets in GIS are represented by lines with no dimension but length. There is no reason why these road lines would match up exactly with survey lines or boundaries, allowing them to be selected using a simple spatial overlay (Tresidder 2005). Section line roads in TIGER can be as much as 190 feet (58 m) away from a section line; selecting out all roads within this distance will include a vast number of side or parallel streets that must be edited manually. There is no basis for identifying section line arterial roads other than visually identifying those roads or streets that appear to be coincident with section lines, and no method to identify the mileage of section line roads. Agricultural patterns are similarly difficult to map. Although the Cropland Data Layer provides a national map of farms (U.S. Department of Agriculture 2021), neither this nor summary data exist to show which sections or townships are being farmed.

The project looks at the U.S. West, defined by Wright (2014) as bounded by the Mexican and Canadian borders, the 98th meridian, and the Pacific slope. This area is defined in large part by aridity, and also by elevation and a largely clustered population distribution and federal land ownership. The eastern boundary includes much of the western Great Plains, whereas the Pacific Coast is a different physical environment, so a more restrictive definition is of the Intermountain West, defined here using physiographic provinces (Fenneman 1916; U.S. Geological Survey 2003). The Basin and Range province, Columbia and Colorado Plateaus, Wyoming Basin, and SierraCascade and Rocky Mountains provinces were used to represent this region. This definition includes all of Arizona, Utah, Nevada, and Idaho, as well as the eastern two thirds of Washington and Oregon; the Sierra Nevada mountains and Mojave Desert in California; the Rocky Mountain portions of Montana, Wyoming, and Colorado; and all of New Mexico west of the eastern boundary of the Rio Grande watershed. A small area of Basin and Range extends into Texas but this was excluded as that state was never surveyed for the PLS.

Google Earth was used to visually identify areas of the interior West where the grid landscape is apparent based on road and crop patterns. I examined each state, looking first at urbanized areas and then in each rural county. Polygons were drawn around gridded areas and brought into GIS, where they were overlaid on the boundaries of townships and sections available from the Bureau of Land Management (BLM), the heir to the General Land Office, the agency that carried out the PLS (BLM 2021). Townships within the gridded areas were clipped out.

## Results

Although the PLS has been extended to much of the West, the extent of the familiar rectangular landscape derived from it is nothing like Figure 2. The purpose of the survey was to make land available for agricultural settlers, and early surveys were carried out where settlement was most likely, leaving unsurveyed land in mountainous terrain (Hubbard 2009). Yellowstone National Park, removed from the public domain in 1872, was never surveyed; neither was the Grand Canyon. Areas that had been surveyed using other systems, such as Spanish and Mexican land grants in the Southwest, were not covered by the survey. Lands allotted to Native Americans were initially skipped but then often surveyed, although not all these surveys remain on the map (Weber 2021b). Even this is misleading in the Intermountain West, though, where agriculture is limited, other surveying systems exist, and most land remains part of the public domain. It is instead an archipelago of gridded landscapes, visible only in agricultural areas and fast-growing cities (Figure 4, Table 1). They do not match counties or other political boundaries and might cross state lines or include land surveyed from different baselines and meridians. Most of these gridded areas were identifiable long ago in the development of the West (Meinig 1972). The largest area is the Columbia Plateau, a vast semiarid agricultural basin covered in loess soils (Hunt 1967; Meinig 1968). It became an agricultural and ranching area irrigated by water impounded along the Columbia and Snake Rivers. The gridded area contains numerous voids within it due to the channelled scablands, where ancient floods have scoured the soil to bedrock. California ' s Imperial Valley is the lowest gridded landscape at 230 feet (70 m) below sea level. Arizona ' s Salt River Valley includes Phoenix, the largest population center within the gridded West. Las Vegas, Salt Lake City, and Tucson are smaller centers. Much smaller grids are apparent in the scattered valleys of the rural West. The Central Rockies has a few isolated valleys with gridded areas, among them the San Luis Valley. Much of this valley is over 7,800 feet (2,377 m) in elevation, the highest of any gridded area.

There are several distinct types of gridded areas, distinguished by land use and population (Figure 5). Many are agricultural in nature, created by federal water projects, but they exist on a continuum from urban to rural. Some now have large urban centers at their core, which has transformed farmland into urban neighborhoods as they grew (Figure 5A).

Phoenix was founded as a farm town in 1870 in a region that had been farmed by Native Americans for over 1,000 years. The federal Salt River Project began in 1903 to build the first of many dams to store water for valley farmers. The acreage of farmland peaked in the early 1950s, with most of that since being converted to urban land supporting a metro area with almost 5 million residents, with section corners now marked by Circle K convenience stores. Some farmland remains on the margins of the city awaiting conversion in the next housing boom.

Other agricultural areas remain rural or with scattered towns or small cities. Aside from the Columbia Plateau, these tend to be small and are often linear, either located along a river or in north -south trending valleys. Others are irregular and can appear far from any river due to extensive groundwater pumping. Amargosa Valley, Nevada, is one example (Figure 5C), developed as an agricultural area in the 1950s (Lee, Coleman, and Nicholson 2005). Here, and throughout the West, farming makes use of center pivot irrigation (McKnight 1979, 1983; Webster and Leib 2011). This was invented in 1949 on the Colorado plains before seeing widespread use in Nebraska and other plains states and slowly spreading into the Western states. The system is a more efficient user of water, requires less labor, and can be used on uneven land or hillsides. Although based on circles, it fits easily within the rectangular PLS system: The circles are either a quarter or full section in size and rarely cross section lines.

Finally, the federal government has directly created new communities on the public domain to support its activities. Fort Irwin, California, is an example (Figure 6D). The town was created in the 1980s and has a population of more than 8,800, living in distinct subdivisions and provided with a hierarchical system of streets.

## Grid and Nongrid Areas Compared

Thrower (1966) and Johnson (1976) documented substantial differences between grid and nongrid areas in the Midwest. Do these exist in the Intermountain West as well? To compare gridded and nongridded areas, census block groups were overlaid on gridded areas in GIS and clipped to isolate those within the gridded areas. Because many block groups had only a portion of their territory within gridded areas, the population values will be an overestimate. Over 90 percent of the population of Idaho and Utah reside within the PLS grid landscape, followed closely by Arizona and Nevada (Table 2). Thrower (1966) also found a larger number of bridges in a surveyed than an unsurveyed area in Ohio, as roads within the unsurveyed area followed drainage divides and avoided unnecessary stream crossings. The expectation that gridded landscapes should have more bridges was tested using the National Bridge Inventory (NBI), a geospatial database of the nation ' s highway bridges, defined as any structure with a span of 20 feet (6 m) or more (Federal Highway Administration 2020). Those within gridded areas were selected. In every state the density of highway bridges is greater within the gridded areas than in nongridded areas (Table 3), although the reason is different from that noted by Thrower. In the gridded areas of the West, a common source of bridges are freeways; large cities will have freeway systems with interchanges, increasing the densities of bridges. For Las Vegas, for example, the NBI reports 502 bridges, which is likely to be a considerable undercount, as the NBI does not include many newer bridges. Of these 50.6 percent of are associated with a freeway, which will increase as freeways continue to be built.

Considerable variation exists among the different gridded areas (Table 1). The density of population and roads is highest in Las Vegas, a purely urban gridded area. This population density is less than that reported for the U.S. Census Bureau urban area, which does not include lower density areas included here. Urban, nonagricultural areas have the highest average population, road, and bridge densities, followed by urban agricultural areas. Rural agricultural grids have the lowest average population and road densities, with rural, nonagricultural areas having the lowest bridge density.

Differences between gridded and nongridded areas can encompass more than just densities of roads and bridges; they can also include the existence of different land uses. The BLM manages cattle grazing allotments and wild horse herd management areas throughout the interior West, but none are in gridded areas. In contrast, cattle feedlots can be found within rural gridded areas, such as Pinal County, Arizona, where they might sometimes take up an entire section. These appear to be absent from nongridded areas of the West.

## Conclusions

Most of what has been written about the imprint of the PLS on the West is based on the Midwest or Great Plains, where this imprint is strongest. Although the PLS has been described as a ' ubiquitous human imprint on the western landscape ' (Wyckoff 2014, 248) this is appropriate in only a few scattered areas of the West, although these are where most of the region ' s population live. Nor does the survey remain legally ubiquitous as a means of describing boundaries; Arizona law allows for five different methods for defining property boundaries, including not just the PLS, but also metes and bounds, latitude and longitude, state plane coordinates, and lot numbers within surveyed subdivisions (Arizona Department of Revenue 2011). In an overwhelmingly urban state, subdivision names are far more meaningful geographic descriptions than sections.

Complaints about the lack of sustainability of settlement in the West have been heard for many years. In 1877, the explorer-turned-ethnologist John Wesley Powell argued that the land survey system being used was part of the problem (Powell 1879; Johnson 1976; Sauder 1990; Hubbard 2009). This was the PLS, based on marking out a square grid on the land and selling off land in parcels of land as little as forty acres, to become family farms. West of 100 degrees west longitude, increasing aridity made the possibilities for dry farming less likely and the need for irrigation more urgent, but water resources in the West were scarce and available to only a few settlers. Powell presented an alternate vision for the settlement of the West, one based on the availability of water. Watersheds should be mapped first, with farms (requiring irrigation ditches) limited to twenty acres along rivers; larger grazing parcels on higher, unirrigated land; and the remainder of the watershed used as common grazing land. The survey and settlement of the West continued along the usual lines. Powell ' s larger vision for Western settlement is evident in Figure 5, which is a map of water resources: Only areas with water appear as gridded landscapes. Powell ' s vision was for rural agriculture based on local surface water; he did not envision the largescale tapping of groundwater, the growth of large cities, or the massive infrastructure needed to supply them. Instead of the survey being changed to fit water resources, the reverse has occurred: Water resources have been adjusted to fit settlement patterns to ensure that each developer has access to the water needed to make the land usable.

What is the future of the PLS landscape in the West? Because gridded areas are on private land and the public domain was closed in 1976, the gridded survey landscape is unlikely to spread except in a few cases where there are large amounts of private undeveloped land, as between Tucson and Phoenix. The growth of cities such as Las Vegas is severely limited by surrounding public land. A lengthy process is in place to allow for occasional transfers of public land to facilitate future growth in that city, and this migght become necessary elsewhere (BLM 2004; Brean 2018). Las Vegas is already the most densely populated gridded area in the West (Table 1) and this density will only increase in the future.

Within gridded areas, there will likely be considerable land-use and population change. Agricultural areas that are partly or largely urban will become increasingly urban; agricultural areas of Phoenix will come to resemble Las Vegas as the remaining farmland becomes urban. Rural nonagricultural areas such as Golden Valley might become more densely urban, although it is less likely to happen soon. Residential subdivisions were planned here but development activity evaporated during the 2008 recession (Friess 2008). Rural agricultural areas are the most precarious, dependent on both water and agricultural markets. Farming will not increase in Amargosa Valley due to the cost of groundwater pumping and federal controls on pumping. Should the aquifer drop, agriculture will cease, although low-density residential development might remain or even intensify, converting the area to a rural nonagricultural area. Nearby Pahrump, Nevada, has passed through this transition since the 1980s when cotton fields were replaced by subdivisions. Arizona ' s Sulphur Springs Valley might be next, although it is not clear what, if anything, will replace agriculture (Shannon 2018). Even if any of these communities were to be abandoned, the PLS

gridded landscape will not soon disappear. Dirt roads a century old can be seen in this region; nobody has yet lived long enough to learn how long they might remain (Webb, Steiger, and Wilshire 1986; Bolling and Walker 2000; Brooks and Lair 2009). In Italy, the grid pattern of roads built by the Roman Empire is still plainly visible in rural areas (Dilke 1971), and the section line landscape of the PLS will remain in the West for centuries to come. /H17039
