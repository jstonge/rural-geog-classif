## New West and Old West in the Twenty-First Century: The Rich Get Richer

## Samuel M. Otterstrom and J. Matthew Shumway

Brigham Young University, USA

The rural U.S. West is increasingly valued for its unique natural beauty, tourism potential, and the outdoor recreational activities it provides rather than for its grazing potential, lumber mills, or extraction of valuable minerals. With this transition in mind, we examine demographic changes in the region within the context of selective migration. We build on research conducted in the late 1990s that studied the changing spatial patterns of migration and income migration within the rural Mountain West. We update the current economic and demographic structure of the region and then document how 1990s patterns have persisted through an analysis of population concentration and income migration using U.S. Census and Internal Revenue Service data. Positive net migration and income migration have focused on New West (high amenity) counties, whereas the Old West, comprised of government, mining and manufacturing, farming, and other (diversified) counties, has fallen farther behind in income migration. Consequently, selective migration to areas of natural beauty and recreation potential has increasingly transformed the rural New West into its own peculiar geography, distinct in demographics as well as environment from the Old West, revealing a growing spatial inequality between these two areas, especially in income and housing. Key Words: amenity migration, American Mountain West, income migration, New West, rural geography.

I n the U.S. West, migration continues to be influenced by a number of environmental factors. Indeed, for environmentalists, tourists, policymakers, and even some locals, the rural West is increasingly valued for its unique natural beauty, tourism potential, and the outdoor recreational activities it provides rather than for its grazing potential, lumber mills, or extraction of localized deposits of valuable minerals (Power 1995). The attractions of natural amenity areas have led to the creation of new protected areas such as the Bears Ears National Monument in 2016 and Grand Staircase-Escalante National Monument in 1996, both located in Utah. Bears Ears National Monument encloses approximately 1.35 million acres -or just over one quarter of San Juan County, Utah -a significant amount of territory (San Juan is the largest county in the state). The creation of these monuments can attract outsiders who value these areas for their natural beauty and recreational potential, although many locals want these federal lands to be open to more traditional economic activities such as mining. In a larger sense, migration throughout the rural Mountain West brings outsiders, who often have more income, into certain New West areas that are deemed more attractive, sometimes pushing locals out who cannot afford the increased living costs associated with these areas (especially in housing). Meanwhile Old West counties continue their reliance on growth that is spurred by less wealthy migrants and more traditional economic activities. These movements continue to exacerbate the spatial inequality between Old and New West areas and reveal the limits of environmental amenities as an avenue to economic growth.

The effect of the creation of the Bears Ears and Grand Staircase National Monuments (under the authority of the Antiquities Act), or the decision by the U.S. Forest Service to close or restrict roads to off-road vehicles (U.S. Department of Agriculture 2005), or the even more provocative push by states in the western United States to transfer federal control to the states (McQuillan 2015), illustrate land-use practices and policy that epitomize the differences in the economies of Old and New West counties. Specifically, the Old West with its oldtimers who are more reliant on traditional natural resource extractive economies, have a different economic makeup than New West counties with their wealthier newcomers who move there for desirable amenities available in certain parts of the rural and nonmetropolitan western United States.

Such differences in the way that Old and New West areas use the natural environment are neither new nor settled and are the result of two factors. First, most of the rural West is public land managed by the federal government. For example, approximately 83 percent of the land surface in Nevada is federally controlled; that figure is between 65 and 70 percent in Utah, Idaho, Wyoming, and Alaska; and between 45 and 50 percent in Arizona, California, and Colorado (Bureau of Land Management 2017). Second, the economic restructuring of the rural West from a productive to an increasingly consumptive region of the country is well underway.

With this economic transition in mind, it is instructive to examine demographic changes in the region within the context of selective migration. Migration is a selective process -selective of who moves in various migration streams. Who migrates where is as important, if not more so, than either the net or gross flows. The dual selective migration processes of who and where, together with the impact of overall gross and net flows determines, to a large extent, the demographic characteristics of places and how those characteristics change over time. Migration is both reflective of and a source for the changing nature of the rural West. As different economic sectors gain (tourism, recreation, retirement) or decline (mining, farming, ranching) in importance, different types of people move into and out of the region. Consequently, with this changing demographic composition in the region, practices and policies related to land use also change.

Our goal is to reexamine patterns of income migration within the rural Mountain West region. By reexamine, we mean that we will build on the research conducted in the late 1990s and early 2000s by scholars wherein they studied the changing spatial patterns of migration and income migration within the rural Mountain West (Shumway and Otterstrom 2001). In this article, we update the current economic and demographic structure of the rural Mountain West and then document if the patterns from the 1990s have persisted or changed, and why.

One of the main findings from previous papers was that both population and income were concentrating within regions that had attractive natural environments that facilitated and engendered extensive recreational activities. These high-amenity counties were classified as the New West, and they experienced economic and demographic growth that supported Power ' s (1995) environmental model of rural development (Shumway and Davis 1996; Shumway and Otterstrom 2001). Shumway and Otterstrom (2001) also concluded that if these particular patterns persisted, then the rural Mountain West would become a relatively low-income region with limited opportunities, harboring a few islands of rapid economic growth and employment opportunities with an increasing concentration of income. In other words, a new rural geography with pronounced inequalities in income, wealth, and opportunity was being established. The current question is this: Did this in fact happen over the last twenty or so years?

## Amenities, Migration, and the New West

Factors influencing migration in the United States have changed in their order of importance due to increases in overall wealth. Based on the demographic transition model, Zelinsky (1971) developed the concept of the mobility transition to explain how and why amenities became such a driving force of migration in the post -World War II United States. The basic concept is that as countries (regions) developed, the magnitude, type, and direction of migration would change from rural-to-urban to a more mixed pattern that included urban-tourban and urban-to-rural movements. The idea has its roots in Engle ' s Law that demonstrated as household income increased, a smaller proportion of income was spent on basic necessities. As applied to migration, Engle ' s Law suggests that as a society increases in income and wealth, people ' s choices concerning where to live also expand.

Increases in disposable income, then, have led to the rise of migration to areas with agreeable environmental conditions and other amenities. Many decades ago, Ullman (1954) illustrated how regional growth in the United States could be partly attributed to the occurrence of certain attractive amenities in these regions. Graves (1976, 1980) continued this idea by demonstrating that the value of natural amenities increased as the United States became wealthier, and by showing how attractive climates have influenced migrants in their moving outcomes (see also McGranahan 1999). Climate continues to be a key factor in migration decisions (Piguet 2013). Amenities in general are now considered as a normal or superior good because of the increased ability to take advantage of geographic differences in environmental conditions. As a result, much of the late twentieth-century migration in the United States can be explained by residential preferences for location-specific environmental amenities.

Power (1995, 1996) and Rasker (1993, 1995) argued that the growing importance of natural amenities in explaining migration patterns is not limited to the increased ability of individuals and families to express preferences for natural amenities when they choose where to live, but also includes the increasing importance of protecting what is seen as a scarce resource -environmental quality. They also argued that the increasing importance of environmental protection has led to a structural transformation of the rural West ' s economy away from traditional extractive types of activities (mining, forestry, and grazing) to economic activities that coincide with the consumption of environmental quality. Environmental quality is encapsulated in such items as an unspoiled natural environment, recreational opportunities, cultural richness, reasonable costs of living, safe communities, and quality public services. Such amenities attract highly skilled labor and entrepreneurs (Rudzitis 1998). They also attract retirees and tourists, creating demands for additional services. These processes in turn create additional economic activity, which is cumulative, and provide the fuel to perpetuate continued economic viability.

Thus, environmental quality is the basis for economic sustainability, not the unsustainable activities that are associated with the extraction of natural resources.

The basic concepts associated with an environmental quality model of economic development have also found support in other world regions. Lundgren (2009) revealed that environmental designations led to increases in per-capita incomes and migration in Swedish mountain regions. PallaresBlanch (2012) discussed similar strategies conducted in the Pyrenees Mountain region using a ' natural protected areas ' designation that supported employment opportunities -particularly for young people and women.

The environmental quality conceptual framework for understanding rural Mountain West economies does not mean that all natural resource extraction ceased to take place. It indicates that it tends to be less widespread and concentrated in those areas that have a global competitive advantage. As the U.S. economy became more integrated into a global economy, through advances in communication and transportation infrastructures as well as more specific production technologies, areas of marginal productivity within the United States were replaced with areas of greater productivity (lower costs) outside the United States (Gosnell and Abrams 2011). This competitive pressure resulted in an increase in local economic activities that could compete globally or amenities that would actually benefit from increases in global access (i.e., environmental quality and locally specific and scarce natural amenities). For example, farmers from Aberdeen, Bingham County, Idaho (an Old West farming county) have developed a way to get more protein-rich alfalfa leaves harvested separate from stocks, and they are working to make products that can use this nutritious food, thus potentially expanding the basic alfalfa market outward (Menser 2019). In terms of amenity-rich areas, cities like Jackson, Wyoming, have grown tremendously with the influx of migrants who have capital ties far outside of the state to many regions of the country and world (Nelson and Hines 2018).

Starting in the 1990s, high-amenity counties became the fastest growing nonmetropolitan counties in the Mountain West (Shumway and Otterstrom 2001). Many of these high-amenity areas would be familiar to those with an interest in the Mountain West. Such counties include Summit County, Utah (Park City); Teton County, Wyoming (Jackson Hole); Blaine County, Idaho (Sun Valley); or Eagle County, Colorado (Vail). Less well-known places are also represented such as Lincoln County, New Mexico (Ruidoso); and Montrose County, Colorado (with Black Canyon of the Gunnison National Park; Beyers and Nelson 2000).

The patterns of growth in the 1990s were a shift from the growth in the 1970s population turnaround, and 1980s rural retrenchment periods. In the 1970s, most growth took place in resource-rich or college-dominated rural counties such as those in the Uinta Basin in Utah and Colorado (oil); Campbell County, Wyoming in the Powder River Basin (coal); or Coconino County, Arizona (Northern Arizona University; Long and DeAre 1988). After the limited rural population growth of the 1980s, however, the 1990s saw a renewal of rural population growth in counties with two main characteristics: First, they had some type of locally unique environmental amenities (Power 1996); second, they were relatively accessible (Rasker 1993; Shumway 2003).

The shift to more rapid growth in high-amenity areas also signaled the beginning of a change in economic structure from the expropriation of natural resources to the protection of unique environments for use as recreation, retirement, second homes, and areas with protected status (Power 1996). This shift makes sense within the context of increased economic globalization where increased competition in natural resource production led to the loss of jobs in that sector, shifting to growth in employment opportunities in sectors with less direct global competition (i.e., location-specific natural amenities; Gosnell and Abrams 2011).

Nationally, scholars have shown the propensity for migrants to move more readily between certain places and to bring their money with them. For instance, migration flows and income gains from movers to smaller cities and rural regions in the 1990s tended to come from those originating in higher levels of the urban hierarchy (Manson and Groop 2000; Plane, Henrie, and Perry 2005). During that period, rural areas of the Rocky Mountains and New England were especially prone to receiving new migrants with high incomes (Nelson 2005). Additionally, as certain rural areas have been the recipients of migration flows, rural gentrification has resulted with its attendant changes in the affected communities and connection to capital far outside the local sphere (Nelson, Oberg, and Nelson 2010; Nelson and Hines 2018).

The previous history of the rural West was marked by boom-and-bust economies associated with the unsustainable nature of primary-based extractive economic sectors. An environmentalbased economic model is, according to Power (1996), much more sustainable because it seeks to preserve the nature of the economic base that provides the initial impetus for economic growth. If this is the case, we are less likely to see a boom-and-bust pattern of population growth -or at least one with fewer extremes in growth and contraction. Additionally, the type of resource-reliant economy, one more based in extraction (e.g., mining or logging) or one focused on nonextractive activities (e.g., tourism and real estate development) affect the economic success of these areas (Mueller 2020, 2021).

Other recent studies summarize the main themes of the ongoing shifts in rural population in the Mountain West and the amenities that are part of the attraction to some places. For example, the historical cultural landscape, and the chance to make that a part of a newcomer ' s identity, is part of what drives some amenity migrants to new places (Post 2013). On the other hand, the local sense of place of New West areas could be affected by the migrants who come there, through increases in population that potentially change the community character (Keske et al. 2017). As migrants affect the population structure of New West counties, the demographic comparisons between New West and Old West locations show greater or lesser degrees of separation due to these shifts (Winkler et al. 2007). Finally, over the life course, migrants tend to seek out different types of amenity counties according to their ages (e.g., nicer climates in the retirement years; Whisler et al. 2008).

In terms of population changes, Weber (2019) outlined three dominant streams of migration into the Mountain West region: (1) net in-migration to metropolitan areas and counties that are adjacent to metropolitan areas (migration here is largely to access the metro region); (2) net in-migration to high-amenity counties; and (3) general net outmigration from remaining rural counties. The rural counties in this last migration stream are predominantly Old West types with extractive economies (farming, ranching, mining) with limited recreational and retirement appeal, or relatively isolated. Weber labelled these places the ' Bypassed West. ' Most of these areas are dominated by federal land ownership and even more specifically the Bureau of Land Management. Weber ' s perspectives can help inform our analysis.

In sum, there are three nonmutually exclusive forces that have been or are converging to provide the context within which demographic patterns in the rural Mountain West are manifest. These are migration selectivity, increasing importance of environmental quality, and the integration of the region into a global economy. All of these forces operate within the context of most rural land management controlled by the federal government. We posit that over the last two decades, these forces have resulted in a continuing divergence between wealthy, highamenity locations, and rural areas built around resource extraction with relative levels of isolation either mitigating or exacerbating the effects of selective migration and environmental quality. This article will add to the literature by determining the extent to which the New West that emerged in the late twentieth century has continued to concentrate people and money in select high-amenity regions of the Mountain West during the first decades of the twenty-first century. This point will be examined through the ongoing relevance of environmental quality of life for migrants with the means to relocate, and the fuller integration of the region into the global economy. Additionally, we will point out how this concentration is leading to greater socioeconomic inequalities across the Mountain West.

## Methods

With all the research that has sought to describe and analyze the reasons behind population changes in the rural West, scholars still struggle to agree on the exact areas that are part of the New West (Robbins et al. 2009). In this article we chose to reevaluate the same New West counties designated by Shumway and Otterstrom (2001) for consistency.

Thus, we separated counties in the Mountain West states (Arizona, Colorado, Idaho, Montana, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming) into the same five groups according to their economic structure: (1) government; (2) high amenity (New West); (3) mining and manufacturing; (4) farming and ranching; and (5) diversified or ' other ' (counties without a single dominant economic sector; see Figure 1). We used k -means cluster analysis of data from rural counties related to county typology codes and natural amenity index data developed by the Economic Research Service of the U.S. Department of Agriculture as well recreation-based economies designations developed by Beale and Johnson (1998) to classify high-amenity counties and the other types of counties. We also included the same counties as the previous study, even those that have since been classified as urban by the census. 1

Our procedure is to calculate and evaluate population change, net migration, income migration, and changing patterns of income and population concentration across the five categories of counties. In the first step we simply calculate the population changes, growth rates, and relative populations of the five categories of counties in the Mountain West from 2000 to 2019. The next step is to calculate a long-term view of population concentration for the five types of counties from 1950 to 2019. The last step is to derive several income migration measures for each year from 2011 to 2019 and then aggregate them. Following is a discussion of population concentration and income migration methods.

## Population Concentration

In the years since the previous research (Shumway and Otterstrom 2001), we still expect population growth to be increasingly spatially concentrated in high-amenity areas. One way to demonstrate this is by calculating a Hoover index, which measures spatial focus over time (Long and Nucci 1997; Otterstrom and Shumway 2003). 2 The principal use of the index is to identify whether population trends exhibit tendencies toward more uniform distributions, or whether they serve to focus population in a more concentrated pattern (Plane and Rogerson 1994).

## Income Migration

Income migration is not the migration of income; it is instead the change in income due to migration. That is, after accounting for migration, income change is measured in both the sending and the receiving areas (counties in this case), including those people who did not move outside the county during the year. Both the total amount of income (aggregate income) in an area and an area ' s per-capita income

(PCI) are affected by the net movement into and out of a county and the differences in income between who moves in, who moves out, and who does not move (stayers). It is important to consider both aggregate income changes and PCI levels because the aggregate shows how much the overall economy of a county or group of counties is affected by the migration of people with varying incomes, whereas the PCI values can show the income levels by county type as well as the differential incomes of stayers, inmigrants, and out-migrants in each type of county to underscore how the income levels have changed in these areas.

Measuring such income change due to migration can get complicated. For example, an area can have an increase in PCI even though it experiences net out-migration if both out-migrants and in-migrants have lower incomes than stayers. For aggregate income change in a county to be correct, outmigrant income from the previous period must be assigned to the county of origin, not the destination, and the same income must be assigned to the destination in the current period. We use a series of formulas developed by Plane (1999) that use combined Internal Revenue Service (IRS) and U.S. Census Bureau data to examine the question of whether income migration continued to focus on these highamenity areas.

## Population Growth Patterns

As seen in Table 1 and Figure 2, all the county groups grew in population between 2000 and 2019, but population concentration was only occurring within the high-amenity category. To explain, the overall Hoover index of population concentration in the rural Mountain West increased from 36.2 in 1950 to 42.0 in 2019, but the region-based subcomponents of the Hoover index in Figure 2 show that the concentration increase is attributed to the growth in New West counties. These subcomponents add up to the total Hoover index values and indicate the portion of the total concentration that comes from that region. Thus, not only is the population in the rural Mountain West growing more in the high-amenity counties than in other categories, but the distribution of that increase is raising the population concentration overall in rural counties, meaning that the population growth in the highamenity counties is focused on a limited number of counties rather than spreading out evenly. This suggests that population growth is higher in higher density counties, and the patterns observed in the earlier time period are reinforced by current patterns.

At this point, the selectivity of migration and place becomes an issue. Although our data are limited with respect to several selective characteristics (age, education, occupation, etc.), we do have some information on income. High-amenity areas tend to attract households with higher incomes, ceteris paribus (Shumway and Otterstrom 2001). Because one of the underlying rationales for the environmental model of development is the protection of unique environments, and because much of the land in the rural Mountain West is controlled by the federal government, this limits the amount of housing that can be built. This limitation tends to put upward pressure on housing costs -and in turn attracts higher income households and, thus, impacts the economy. The first impact reinforces the initial patterns as high-income households build expensive housing that increases overall housing costs; the second impact forces lower income households, those necessary for the newly created service jobs, into neighboring communities and counties with lower housing costs. In previous periods, this was in fact the case as most of the income migration into rural Mountain West counties went to high-amenity counties (Shumway and Otterstrom 2001) forcing lower income households into neighboring counties (Gober, McHugh, and Leclerc 1993). It is likely that this pattern has continued, marked by population growth in both the amenity counties and their neighbors. Locals can benefit from economic and population growth in New West counties, but their incomes might not keep up with the increased costs of living there (Hunter, Boardman, and Onge 2005).

In terms of straight population change, all types of counties in the Mountain West from the categories classified by Shumway and Otterstrom (2001) have been growing in population since 2000, including metropolitan counties that we do not examine here (Table 1). The high-amenity (New West) counties are the most populous and have experienced the greatest percentage of population growth -an annual growth of 1.35 percent. The ' Diversified ' category also had greater than 1 percent a year growth, but the remaining categories are all below 1 percent annual growth (e.g., the farming-dependent counties, which increased by less than 8 percent over the whole time period), and they are the least populous of all the county clusters at just below 400,000 people in 2019.

An example of differing patterns of growth is found in the neighboring Emery and Grand counties in Utah. Emery County is a county dominated by mining, farming, utilities (coal-fired power plants), construction, and government. In other words, it is a typical Old West type of county. Between 2000 and 2019, Emery County ' s population declined by approximately 8 percent. Right next to Emery County is Grand County, the home of Moab, Utah, Arches National Park, and some of the most spectacular scenery anywhere. Again, this is a typical New West type of county. Grand County grew by 15 percent over the same time period. Both counties are bisected by Interstate 70 and are accessible (although far from major population centers), yet the outcomes relative to population growth are completely different. The picture is complicated, however, by the fact that the median household income was actually higher in Emery County ($52,055 from 2014 -2018) than in Grand County ($48,673 from 2014 -2018; U.S. Census Bureau 2019a), although January 2021 home sales data show much higher median sales prices in Grand County than Emery County ($435,000 and $153,000, respectively; Wood and Eskic 2021). Thus, Grand County ' s population growth success might be helpful for some (e.g., those newcomers with higher incomes), but difficult for others priced out of a home by their lower incomes.

Another way to look at this population shift is to consider the relative shift in population shares among the four regions (Table 2). The New West (high-amenity) counties increased their population share from 43 to 45 percent of all the nonmetro counties, and the diversified other counties also saw their population share grow from 10 to 12 percent. An example of a county in the ' Diversified ' category is Madison County, Idaho, which has a diverse economic base (education -private -professional, retail, recreation), that saw an increase by 45 percent between 2000 and 2019. Another example would be Dolores County, Colorado. Dolores County ' s economic structure is based on farming, education and health care, recreation, construction, and retail. Dolores is a county that does not easily fit into one of the other categories because of its diverse economic base. Nevertheless, Dolores County grew over 11 percent between 2000 and 2019.

## Income Migration

After establishing the continued population growth of high-amenity counties in the Mountain West, we now turn to income movements associated with population change. Our findings support the view that not only people, but money is continuing to flow to New West counties, although there is some indication of growth in the diversified counties.

## Changes in Per-Capita Income

PCI is one way to look at how the different nonmetro clusters have been affected by migration. Table 3 contains data on PCI by county type across nonmigrants (stayers), in-migrants, and out-migrants. Table 3 also indicates what the percentage income of in- and out-migrants is of stayers, which allows us to determine the impact of in- and out-migration on overall county PCI. In most migration flows, inmigrants tend to have lower incomes than stayers because migrants, on average, tend to be younger than stayers (Plane 1999). The same is true for outmigrants. For example, in government counties, nonmigrant (stayer) PCIs were some $2,000 to $4,000 more than in-migrant and out-migrant PCIs. Thus, most per-capita changes in income accrue to differences in income between in- and out-migrants.

This pattern mostly holds true for nonmetro Mountain West counties except in two cases. For New West counties, which have the highest PCIs, in-migrants have 6 percent higher PCIs than stayers and 21 percent higher incomes than out-migrants, both of which lead to a significant increase in PCIs within this grouping of counties (see Table 4, bottom panel). The other anomaly in the data comes from the farming category. Out-migrants from farming counties have higher incomes than both inmigrants and stayers (by some $500 to $1,500), which leads to a loss in PCIs of over $1,000 in this time frame. We speculate that the out-migration from farming counties has been weighted toward the younger demographic who work in well-paying service positions that can more easily translate into migration to similar jobs in other rural or urban places. Out-migration of young adults from rural counties across the country has been an ongoing phenomenon that can lead to concentrations of poorer populations in these areas and increasing spatial inequality among the distinct types of rural counties (Johnson and Lichter 2019). Relative to differences in PCIs of in- and out-migrants, all of the other county groups ' in-migrants had higher incomes than out-migrants, although this is most evident in the government group, where in-migrant income was six percentage points higher than that of out-migrants, leading to a larger increase in PCIs ($22,247 vs. $20,766 for in-migrants and outmigrants, respectively).

## Changes in Aggregate Income

Changes in aggregate income due to migration at the county level occur through two processes: the total net movement of migrants, and the differences in income between in-migrants and out-migrants. Generally, if the total number of net migrants is positive, then aggregate income will likely increase (and vice versa). On the other hand, even if net migration is negative, aggregate income can increase if in-migrant income is higher than out-migrant income. These two factors can either work together to increase or decrease aggregate income or work in opposite directions to each other. An example of the negative effects of income migration is found in Elbert County, Colorado. Elbert County is located just east of some of the fastest growing counties along Colorado ' s Front Range. Its population increased by approximately 35 percent between 2000 and 2019 (from just under 20,000 to approximately 27,000 people), with most of that growth (5,000) coming from migration (U.S. Census Bureau 2019b). Through analysis of the IRS data, most of the in-migration into Elbert County is spillover from Front Range counties. Between the 2011 -2012 and the 2018 -2019 tax year data, aggregate income in Elbert County decreased by $245 million. The decrease was due to in-migrants with significantly less income than either out-migrants or stayers, as there was actually an increase of approximately $247 million from net migration. This was more than offset, however, by losses in income of close to half a billion dollars due to differential income between inand out-migrants. Elbert County is not a poor county (data show a median household income of around $90,000 with a median house valued at over $300,000). Nevertheless, the number of people classified as poor or struggling by the U.S. Census Bureau (2020) went from 13 percent in 2000 to 17 percent in 2018.

Another example is an Old West county that has struggled to keep up is White Pine, Nevada. White Pine has traditionally been reliant on mining (classified as mining and manufacturing in this study), and consequently, its population has vacillated wildly over the decades. For example, the county had 12,377 residents in 1940 (point of highest population) and just 8,167 in 1980. Its population in 2000 was 9,181, which increased to 10,030 in the 2010 census. Since that time, the population has declined to an estimated 9,580 in 2019. Between 2011 -2012 and 2018 -2019, aggregate income declined by over $21 million due both to net out-migration and differential income losses. PCI also declined by over $3,000. White Pine is typical of many Old West counties and is hoping that its economy can diversify to keep afloat, such as through renewable energy (Eggen 2013; Rowley 2014).

On the other hand, there are places like Grand County, Colorado. Grand County is a high-income county (median household income of $65,000) that is a typical New West type of county with 30 percent of its employment based on recreation services -more than twice any other category. Between 2011 -2012 and 2018 -2019, Grand County had a net positive in-migration of about 700 people. What is remarkable about this turnover through migration is that aggregate income increased by almost $78 million, with the substantial majority of that increase ($53 million) coming from in-migrants having higher incomes than out-migrants, and in this particular case, in-migrants also had higher incomes than stayers. The result is an increase in overall income within the county as well as a PCI gain of $2,368.

A comparison of current migration and income status to the previous 1990s paper illustrates the continuing dominance of New West counties (Table 5 and Table 6). ## The New West in the TwentyFirst Century

The Mountain West has continued to evolve through migration in a way that reinforces and amplifies the patterns of the 1990s. Positive net migration and income migration have focused on New West (high-amenity) counties. The Old West, comprised of government, mining and manufacturing, farming, and other (diversified) counties, has fallen further behind in income migration. Additionally, its population growth lags the New West significantly, as most of its positive increase has necessarily relied on natural increase because more people are leaving these areas rather than coming to live there. Selective migration to areas of natural beauty and recreation potential has transformed the rural New West increasingly into its own peculiar geography, distinct in demographics as well as environment from the Old West. The Old West is still there, but it is just holding its own.

All of this suggests two things. First, Power ' s environmental model of development works for those areas with relatively high levels of natural amenities and moderate levels of accessibility within the context of relatively elevated levels of wealth (Engle ' s Law), as it requires the migration of wealthier individuals and families as one of its key elements. It might also enhance the competitiveness of places within the region with some type of competitive advantage in the production of primary goods as nearby areas restructure their economies toward natural amenity resources. This might be a pattern that can be replicated in other regions in the United States or in other relatively wealthy countries under the right set of circumstances. Still, the question remains whether economic and population growth based on environmental amenities is sustainable due to the inequalities produced with concentrated development in limited locales. For example, Teton County, Wyoming (home to Jackson Hole), experienced greater in-migration than out-migration for each of the five years between 2011 and 2016, but from 2016 to 2019 outmigrants outnumbered in-migrants during each of those years. This observation suggests that some New West places ' growth might be hampered by exceedingly high costs.

Second, those bypassed areas (Weber 2019) that do not have high levels of natural amenities or are more isolated (or both) might never find an economic niche that will be sustainable. These observations are significant because they show how the continuing success of the New West to attract moneyed migrants has put other areas of the Mountain West in the unenviable position of being on the lower end of PCI and having negative net migration income, resulting in low-income concentrations in these places (e.g., farming counties). These socioeconomic and spatial differences among the different categories of counties are also likely playing out within the New West counties at the local scale where inequality is increasing because of the more pronounced differential levels of income of the inmigrants who have more income, the stayers who try to keep up, and the out-migrants who sometimes cannot afford to live in these places. Specifically, housing costs have increased considerably in these New West areas, exacerbating spatial inequality related to income. This has been caused by the transfer of wealth from areas higher in the urban hierarchy that already have expensive real estate valuations (Shumway and Otterstrom 2010; Otterstrom 2015). This issue has been studied by Nelson and Hines (2018) in Jackson Hole, Wyoming, and also noted by news media about Jackson Hole as well as other New West locales such as Park City, Utah, and Aspen, Colorado (Hansman 2022). Indeed, nationally, the geographic differences in housing affordability among different types of rural counties as well as urban counties continue to be amplified through population change (Brooks 2022). Thus, success of New West counties in terms of attracting higher income migrants has magnified spatial and social inequality within and among different rural counties in the Mountain West.

Our article has illustrated the ongoing income and migration differences between Old West counties that have relied on natural resource extraction or government employment for their livelihood, and the New West that has benefited from rising interest in and value of environmental amenities. Indeed, national monuments such as Bears Ears and Grand Staircase Escalante are symbolic of the transitions that have come to define the New West as places that contain landscapes that attract amenity migrants who are generally better off financially, and who in turn seek to protect these environmental spaces even in the face of local disagreement. Although the costs to live within the environmental landscapes of the New West and the spatial inequalities that have resulted might eventually test the limits of the amenity development model, still, after more than two decades of the twenty-first century, the Old West and the New West continue to experience different economic and demographic trajectories, with the New West holding the economic advantage for the foreseeable future. /H17039
