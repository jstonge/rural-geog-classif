## Regional Drinking Water Supply in Pakistan: Regional Disparity, Inequality, and Development Pattern

Yangyi Wu

<!-- image -->

Wuhan University, China

Yehua Dennis Wei University of Utah, USA

This article explores the regional disparities in water supply coverage in Pakistan in the context of intraregional development patterns. We applied the Gini index, Esteban and Ray index, and Moran ' s I to measure regional development patterns in terms of inequality, polarization, and spatial concentration and build an integrated framework combining them with other widely accepted factors to understand regional differences in water coverage quantitatively. Our results show that improved water (tap and pump water) is more widely supplied in Pakistan with lower inequality but higher spatial concentration than tap water supply coverage, although the patterns differ by urban -rural divisions and provinces. Western regions with smaller populations tend to have higher tap water coverage, and eastern regions with large populations are more covered by improved water. The regression models show various mechanisms of improved water and tap water coverage. Both are related to local economic conditions but tap water coverage is more related to local environmental conditions, and improved water is more related to local demographic conditions. Strong and dense population centers decrease improved water coverage but promote tap water coverage. Urban planners and policymakers should address polycentric regional development to fulfill various development targets and improve regional equity of drinking water coverage in terms of accessibility, availability, and quality. Key Words: inequality, regional disparity, urbanization, water supply coverage.

S afe drinking water has long been a top concern worldwide, especially in developing countries and undeveloped areas (Johnstone 1997). It is one of the focuses of the World Health Organization/ United Nations International Children ' s Emergency Fund (WHO/UNICEF) Joint Monitoring Program for Water Supply and Sanitation (JMP). Governments, nongovernment organizations, and scholars have been working closely to address this issue. Despite the tremendous progress made globally, the development of drinking water systems raises the concern of inequality in the accessibility of safe drinking water (Moe and Rheingans 2006), which has been regarded as an important part of multidimensional inequalities (Tudor Monica 2012). Similarly, the related target has also transited from ' safe drinking water coverage ' of the Millennium Development Goals (MDG) between 2000 and 2015 to ' reasonable access ' of the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDG), mandating that ' no one is left behind ' (UN General Assembly 2015). Considering the importance of water inequality in human and sustainable development, as well as the important role of local contexts in the right to water (Gleick 1998; Guneralp, Guneralp, and Liu 2015; Sultana and Loftus 2015), understanding the patterns and underlying factors of drinking water disparity from the perspective of policy and planning is essential for improvement.

Scholars have examined the inequality in water supply in multiple dimensions by applying various measurements (Malakar, Mishra, and Patwardhan 2018). Within these measurements, spatial inequality has been found to be of high importance because of the explicit spatial pattern in water supply and the close linkage between water supply and geographic conditions (Sohn 2011; Yu et al. 2014). Despite the awareness of the importance of spatial heterogeneity (He et al. 2018), the analysis of spatial inequality is still limited (Afifah et al. 2018), and there is an insufficient understanding of spatial effects and spatial inequality mechanisms in the water supply. Thus, examination of the spatial effects and mechanisms contributes insights into disparities in drinking water supply.

The determinants of water supply vary according to scales. Different factors have been identified at the national, regional, and individual levels (Givental and Meredith 2016). Although scholars have noticed multiscale inequalities in water supply, the multiscale understanding is mostly on patterns rather than mechanisms (Anthonj et al. 2020). Regarding vulnerable populations, studies of underlying factors are usually concentrated on the individual level (Irianti, Prasetyoputra, and Sasimartoyo 2016), leaving the mechanism on the regional level unclear. This topic is of high importance because local development patterns could affect regional drinking water coverage (Capps, Bentsen, and Ramirez 2016). Although related dimensions are discussed by previous studies (Dos Santos et al. 2017), quantitative empirical examinations are still insufficient.

This article studies district-level drinking water coverage in Pakistan. We first explore the spatial patterns of improved drinking water coverage and then relate them to the local conditions and development patterns regarding the economy and population. We aim to answer the following questions: What is the spatial pattern of improved drinking water coverage in Pakistan at the district level? How do urban development and natural settings affect the supply coverage of drinking water? What is the role of local development patterns in drinking water supply coverage?

## Literature Review and Analytical Framework

The disparity in drinking water supply coverage is an important issue in developing countries, as it is highly related to environmental justice and human well-being (Perez-Foguet and Garriga 2011; Garriga and Foguet 2013). As SDG 6 shifts the focus of drinking water supply from access to water management, deeper explorations of reliability, quality, and affordability of water supply, as well as equitable, sustainable, and efficient water management and governance, are needed (Sadoff, Borgomeo, and Uhlenbrook 2020). The JMP report, however, pointed out that the coverage of safely managed drinking water services remains low worldwide, especially in sub-Saharan and central and southern Asia, and improvements are stagnant or even backward in many countries with great inequalities, such as urban -rural and subnational disparities (Jimoh and Jacob-Oricha 2022; Rani 2022). These issues might be related to multiple factors, including natural, built, and sociopolitical environments, with multiple actors such as governments, communities, and households (Balazs and Ray 2014). Thus, understanding the regional water supply disparity is the basis of sustainable development and equity planning (Smit et al. 2011; Kumar 2022). Based on these contexts, this section first reviews the previous findings in spatial dimensions of water inequality, scales, mechanisms of regional water supply, and the quality and security issues of improved water sources. We then summarize previous findings and propose an analytical framework for regional water supply coverage disparities.

Scholars have applied a series of measurements to understand the disparity and inequality in the drinking water supply (Cetrulo et al. 2020). Inequality indexes have been important means, such as the Gini index (Liao and Wei 2012), which could reflect the differences in accessibility among people or regions (Malakar, Mishra, and Patwardhan 2018). As water supply is highly related to regional infrastructure, natural settings, and institutional contexts (Soares et al. 2002; Bain et al. 2014), recent studies have highlighted the importance of spatial measurements, such as geographic inequality (Pullan et al. 2014). They have found that water supply is usually clustered, inequality at a smaller scale is usually greater than at a larger scale (Yu et al. 2014), and spatial inequality is more significant than nonspatial inequality (Pullan et al. 2014). Thus, an understanding of spatial inequality of drinking water is essential in monitoring, policy, and programming (Anthonj et al. 2019; Carolini and Raman 2021). Current studies, however, stop at measuring spatial inequality of water access, leaving the underlying spatial effects and mechanisms underinvestigated (Wei 2015).

The importance of understanding drinking water inequality is also reflected in the role of scale (Cole et al. 2018). Globally, it is highly related to geopolitics (Brichieri-Colombi and Bradnock 2003; Renner, Meijerink, and van der Zaag 2018; Mianabadi et al. 2020), climate change (de Wit and Stankiewicz 2006), and national institutions. Geopolitics influences shared water resources (e.g., rivers or lakes), climate change affects drought and flood, and national institutions lead to diversity in water policies (Givental and Meredith 2016). Regionally, infrastructure and water resources are the primary sources of water inequality (Givental and Meredith 2016). Locally, local agencies and governance are the most critical factors (Schreiner and Hassan 2011; Givental and Meredith 2016; Adams and Smiley 2018). At the individual level, socioeconomic conditions contribute to the family water supply (Ezbakhe, Gine-Garriga, and Perez-Foguet 2019; Wang et al. 2019). These factors lead to heterogeneity in water accessibility development between countries (Yu et al. 2016) and inequality within countries (Fuller et al. 2016). Although previous studies identified the various mechanisms on different scales, a deep understanding of regional disparities is essential to urban management and might be affected by multidimensional factors.

Previous studies have highlighted the importance of understanding water disparities at the regional level, a critical dimension in public services (Gazzeh and Abubakar 2018). As noted by the JMP report, significant inequalities are identified between subnational regions worldwide (Jimoh and Jacob-Oricha 2022). Local coverage might be determined by the combination of local infrastructure development and population, closely related to economic conditions and urbanization (Capps, Bentsen, and Ramirez 2016). Due to historical development and infrastructure policies, local infrastructure development and conditions vary from place to place (Boakye-Ansah et al. 2016). It depends heavily on local economic conditions, as poorer regions usually struggle to have enough infrastructure and workers to serve high-density populations (McMichael 2000). Bad conditions of infrastructure, such as low availability, quality, reliability, or accessibility, could also drive people to other (unimproved) water sources (Abubakar 2019; Naus et al. 2020). On the other hand, scholars link water inequality to demographic characteristics, such as urban space, gender, urban class, and social exclusion (Truelove 2011; Deitz and Meehan 2019). Based on these linkages, scholars have started to analyze the role of regional development patterns (Wong, Filion, and Speight 2020). A sprawling urbanization pattern in the urban area could result in high costs and the inability to develop and maintain infrastructure, and connecting informal settlements to the water supply could affect the quality and regularity of the supply (Cobbinah and Aboagye 2017; Dos Santos et al. 2017; Shao et al. 2021). The overconcentration of the population, however, might decrease the accessibility and availability of water sources (Dano et al. 2020). The patterns of economic activities might also be related to the potential water supply system. On the one hand, good water infrastructure will encourage local economic development (Fosu and Twumasi 2022), resulting in centering or sprawling patterns; on the other hand, local economic development could ensure the local development and maintenance of water supply systems. Moreover, although the urban population is more likely to have access to piped water systems, the progress of urban water access is not as good as in rural areas (Sinharoy, Pittluck, and Clasen 2019). This is because of rapid urbanization, mostly as the outward expansion of metropolitan areas and secondary cities and the development of informal settlements spontaneously (Dos Santos et al. 2017), which results in limited access to essential services and inequality in water access. Combined with the underdeveloped infrastructure, the poor populations do not have access to or cannot pay for the formal water system, whereas the rich population is hardly affected (Dill and Crow 2014; Aliyu and Amadu 2017). The availability is intermittent, resulting in water storage or water safety concerns, and people might seek alternative water sources (Pierce 2017). These problems lead to more time costs for water access and limit the water consumption to essential needs (Olvera, Plat, and Pochet 2003). Thus, water access is particularly a problem for small towns and periurban areas, as they have the characteristics of both urban and rural areas (Allen 2003). Adams and Smiley (2018) further highlighted the variation of water access between urban and periurban areas, from formal to informal settlements and from rural trading centers to rural villages.

Based on these findings, a few studies have explored these regional-level factors. Hopewell and Graham (2014) used population, urbanization, population density, inequality, gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, GDP per capita growth rate, education level, development assistance, and flood as determinants of regional water supply disparity in Africa. Focusing on urbanization, Li et al. (2020) linked urbanization rate, population size, consumption, per capita consumption, college student ratio, industrial structures, GDP per capita, built-up area, and per capita built-up area to measure regional water scarcity in China. Although these pioneer studies have recognized local conditions and development patterns as key factors in regional water access, the empirical examination is mostly limited to local conditions rather than development patterns and spatial organization. The comparative examination of regional disparities in local water supply coverage is insufficient, and a quantitative understanding of local development patterns is urgently needed.

Moreover, rather than the concentration on water sources of the MDGs, the JMP uses a ' service ladder ' to classify the service levels of drinking water as a global indicator for SDGs, including water source, accessibility, availability, and safety (Fukuda, Noda, and Oki 2019; Jimoh and JacobOricha 2022). Safely managed water is defined as drinking water from an improved source accessible on premises, available when needed, and free from fecal and priority chemical contamination. To distinguish from the previously used ' improved water supply, ' the JMP uses the term ' basic ' water supply to represent drinking water from an improved source within a thirty-minute round trip. In this regard, rather than only focusing on accessibility and availability, quality and equity issues should be a research concern, which could largely depend on the water infrastructure and supply system. Although the definition of the improved water source includes piped water, boreholes or tube wells, protected dug wells, protected springs, rainwater, and packaged or delivered water, people might prefer piped tap water (Dhalla 2017). It might be because tap water shows more potential for centralized treatment (Luh and Bartram 2017), which could result in better water quality (Yang et al. 2013). For example, Dorea et al. (2020) found that piped systems might have the least risk of contamination even with high access to improved water sources. Under these circumstances, even though tap water might also be contaminated (Rashid, Khan, and Jalbani 2021), overlooking the difference between water sources could result in a biased understanding of development progress, and improving quality and access to more reliable water sources should be a development direction. In this regard, if a region has a well-developed water supply system of improved drinking water, the next step might be developing the piped tap water system (Wolf et al. 2014) and improving accessibility, availability, and safety. The diverse development of these water supply systems and the underlying mechanisms of their coverage are seldom considered, however, especially at the regional level.

In sum, scholars have revealed the existence of inequality in reliable drinking water coverage. Most studies, however, focus on the individual or national level rather than the regional level. The spatial effects should also be addressed as studying the nonspatial aspect of regional inequality masks the spatiality of inequality, such as spatial polarization and poverty traps (Wei et al. 2020). Furthermore, the multidimensional mechanism of drinking water coverage has rarely been comprehensively and quantitatively studied, especially the role of local development patterns (Olvera, Plat, and Pochet 2003). Besides, it is worth distinguishing the various development mechanisms of different water sources to provide more equal and safer water access.

Based on the literature review, we construct the following analytical framework for our further analysis, which also serves as the basic hypothesis for our research (Figure 1). The framework links the spatial patterns to the underlying factors with a specific focus on the role of intraregional development patterns of population and economic activities. There are two major groups of factors -local conditions and local inequality -with five dimensions: demographic conditions, environmental conditions, economic conditions, economic patterns, and urbanization patterns. Local conditions, including demographic, environmental, and economic conditions, have long been regarded as important determinants of the water supply system at the household level or regional level (Irianti, Prasetyoputra, and Sasimartoyo 2016). We add local economic patterns and urbanization patterns to reflect local development patterns, including social exclusion, urban form, and urban inequality, which is a critical issue in water inequality (Adams and Smiley 2018; Omarova et al. 2019). It is worth pointing out that we exclude the inequality measurements of environmental factors, as they are natural settings that are regarded as control variables. Their intraregional patterns might only reflect the diversity of the natural environment rather than regional structure and socioeconomic disparities.

We also propose that these factors might act differently on the urban population and rural population, as there is strong inequality within the urban -rural division (Wang et al. 2019). These variations result in different patterns of the water supply in rural and urban populations, and the final regional water supply coverage is the composition of the coverage conditions in the urban and rural populations. To better understand water supply coverage, we applied three quantitative measurements to comprehensively reflect regional development patterns: Moran ' s I reflects spatial concentration and organization, the Gini coefficient represents nonspatial inequality, and the Esteban and Ray (E.R.) index measures polarization and high-density development. Moreover, despite the definition of ' safely managed drinking water services ' and ' improved water sources ' characterized by WHO/UNICEF, tap water is usually preferred and shows more potential in safety and quality. Thus, although improved water supply coverage is the primary focus of this study, we further focus on tap water, as both could have distinctly different water quality, cost, and infrastructure requirements (Alam et al. 2014).

## Data and Method

Data and Study Area

Pakistan is regarded as a part of central Asia as well as south Asia (Figure 2). According to the 2017 census, there is a population of more than 200 million. There are four provinces and one capital territory in Pakistan. The main rivers in Pakistan are the Indus River and its tributaries, which are mainly located in the eastern part of the county. This leads to a significant west -east division in surface water resources in Pakistan.

Several important factors make Pakistan a good research subject for drinking water coverage. First, Pakistan has long been regarded as a country with serious drinking water problems (Pappas 2011). Thus, the government of Pakistan has also created the Pakistan Social and Living Standards Measurement (PSLM) survey, which provides a regional data set for drinking water coverage. Second, Pakistan is going through rapid development in drinking water infrastructure, which could result in regional inequality in water access. Studying such patterns and mechanisms would provide stylized facts and further policy suggestions for not only Pakistan, but all other developing countries to achieve sustainable development and equity planning for water supply. Third, according to the JMP report, Pakistan ranks in the middle among the dimensions of accessible on-premises and available water when needed (76 percent and 79 percent, respectively, as of 2015), but performs badly in water free from contamination (36 percent as of 2015), which implies that the Pakistan authorities need to consider improving the infrastructures to ensure a better quality of drinking water.

Our data are collected from multiple data sources. The data set of the PSLM survey of 2015 provides the statistics on people ' s main sources of drinking water, categorized into tap water, hand pump, motor pump, dug well, and others. Tap water is a delivery system based on pipes with treated water. The difference between the hand pump and motor pump is the operation type rather than the water source, as they can be operated at the same hole. In this study, we regard tap water, hand pump, and motor pump as improved water sources because there is no information on whether the dug well and other sources are protected. We need to note that some districts are missing in the survey; thus, we only have the statistics for 112 districts in Pakistan, as shown in Figure 2. Population data are derived from the Pakistan census data (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics 2017), which include all population, urban population, rural population, and local population growth rates at the district level. The GDP purchasing power parity (GDP PPP) data set is estimated by Kummu, Taka, and Guillaume (2018) and contains high-resolution raster data of GDP estimation in 1990, 2000, and 2015. We also use global intercalibrated nighttime light data to represent urbanization from 1992 to 2012 (Zhang, Pandey, and Seto 2016). Other data, such as slope and water resources, are collected from online open data sources.

## Method

We applied multiple statistical methods to measure regional drinking water coverage inequality. Nonspatially, we used the Gini index as the measurement (Bhattacharya and Mahalanobis 1967). We then applied a decomposition of the Gini index to capture the regional differences (Elbers et al. 2005; Wei, Yu, and Chen 2011). For spatial inequality, we applied Moran ' s I to evaluate the general spatial distribution and spatial concentration (Wei, Yu, and Chen 2011).

Moreover, we applied generalized linear models based on the quasi-binomial function to estimate the effects of each factor on local water supply coverage. The quasi-binomial function is applied because it is typical to deal with percentage data (Gabriel and Tscharntke 2007; Harbridge and Malhotra 2011), whereas our independent variables are the percentages of the population covered by tap water or improved water sources. A spatial filtering weight matrix is added to control the underlying spatial autocorrelation. Two model sets are constructed based on the aforementioned analytical framework of the Pakistan water supply coverage. One studies the distribution of tap water supply coverage, and the other studies the distribution of improved water supply coverage. Each model set consists of three regression models corresponding to all, urban, and rural populations.

## Demographic Conditions in Pakistan

Understanding demographic conditions is essential for evaluating drinking water coverage, as the affected populations in districts are largely different. Figure 3A shows the population by districts in Pakistan, and several districts are excluded because of the lack of water data. We can find a significant east -west division in population distribution. Such distribution is highly associated with the distribution of water resources: The east, especially the northeast part of Pakistan, has abundant surface water resources. Concentrating on the urbanization level, Figure 3B shows the urbanization rate by districts in Pakistan. The spatial pattern of urbanization level differs greatly from the population, as the urbanization rate is more evenly distributed in the whole country and mainly clustered around the main cities. The northeast part is not the largest cluster of urbanization, and the role of rivers is not that clear in urbanization. These differences indicate several characteristics of the demographic conditions in Pakistan. The majority of the highly populated regions are still rural areas; thus, a larger population does not necessarily link to cities. Only the leading cities and their neighbors have a high urbanization rate, which implies that the development might focus mainly on leading cities in Pakistan at the current stage but not ordinary regions.

Overall, in 2015, 27 percent of the population had tap water as their main drinking water source, 59 percent relied on pump water, and 14 percent relied on dug wells or other sources. Thus, the primary water source in Pakistan is pump water. There is a significant difference, however, between urban and rural populations, as 51 percent of the urban population had access to tap water, but only 13 percent of the rural population did.

Both tap water and pump water are regarded as improved water sources, but studies also show that pump water is more likely to be polluted (Yu et al. 2016). Thus, we list tap water alone as a comparative study object, which might be a potential further development target, and the two water sources could lead to different health outcomes and well-being.

Tap Water Coverage in Pakistan

Generally, Pakistan does not have high tap water coverage nationwide, and there are significant differences between provinces. There is substantial spatial autocorrelation, and several clusters in the west, south, and north can be identified, possibly because of the infrastructure development there. The spatial pattern changes due to the urban -rural division. If we only concentrate on the urban population, as shown in Figure 4B, we can identify a very large cluster in the west and north. It could result from better infrastructure development in the urban area and the natural water supply. On the other hand, as shown in Figure 4C, although the Moran ' s I index remains high and has a similar spatial pattern to the urban population, the cluster is not that identical for the rural population, as most rural populations do not have high access to tap water supply.

## Determinants of Water Coverage in Pakistan

The results of the models are listed in Table 4. Many factors identified in our conceptual framework influence local water coverage. The R 2 values of the models are relatively high, confirming that our framework is able to explain a large part of the water coverage variation. Models of improved water supply coverage perform better than models of tap water coverage, especially in rural areas, implying that our conceptual framework is more suitable to explain the coverage variation of improved water sources. Comparing the two models, we find that the results vary hugely, and except for the GDP PPP, other significant determinants show inconsistent effects on tap water and improved water coverage. Moreover, urban and rural populations show various sensitivities to different factors.

For tap water coverage to all populations, demographic conditions are not of high significance. The main reason is that the related (especially high-quality) facilities are usually a result of better economic conditions (GDP PPP) but not the population. Not surprisingly, a larger scale of population decreases the coverage of the tap water system in rural areas. Physical environment variables, basically river and mountain areas, are the most important, as confirmed by previous studies. The river is an important water source for tap water, and mountains might provide springs as water sources (Baig et al. 2017) and serve as ' natural water towers ' (Yamanaka and Yamada 2017). Economic condition, namely GDP, is also critical, which also meets our expectations as regions with higher economic conditions might have better abilities to build a tap water system. It is no longer a significant factor for the urban population because the population in the urban centers can benefit from better infrastructure because of the concentration of economic and political power (McDonald et al. 2014). On the other hand, only regions with higher GDP can extend infrastructure to cover the rural population and urban fringes, so local GDP is still critical to rural coverage and becomes even more important regionally. The GDP increase rate is reported as a negative factor in urban tap water coverage, however. It reflects the fact that the development of the tap water system cannot catch up with economic development. The fastgrowing regions might also face the problems of the informal urban population, slums, and development and maintenance costs (to cover more people). Concerning the intraregional patterns of economic activities, despite the relatively low significance level, the high level and dense concentration of GDP reflected as a high level of polarization negatively affects local tap water coverage. It could be because a more economically decentralized region has the ability, demands, and motivation to expand its tap water system. The nonspatial inequality and polarization of population distribution promote local tap water coverage because water infrastructure might be more efficient and cover more populations with high density (See 2015), which is not necessarily spatial. These economic and demographic inequality factors are insignificant to rural or urban populations, however. It could be because these factors mainly contribute to the urban periphery, not urban centers or remote rural areas.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Drinking water supply coverage is an important goal in developing countries. Securing regional water supply equality is critical to social justice, as drinking water is an essential component of human rights (Gleick 1996). Thus, urban planners and policymakers should address the patterns and factors of regional drinking water coverage variation to promote local drinking water supply coverage and eliminate inequality. Current studies, however, have not paid enough attention to spatial inequality, spatial concentration, and, more specifically, the underlying spatial effects among the mechanisms. Moreover, as most studies explore the mechanisms of water consumption at the individual level, there is still an insufficient understanding of regional variation, especially in the aspects of regional planning. There is also an insufficient understanding of the differences in mechanisms of different water sources and urban -rural divisions. Thus, concentrating on regional-level patterns and factors, this study explores the water supply coverage patterns and inequalities both spatially and nonspatially in Pakistan and discovers the underlying factors of the regional variation of water coverage.

We found that the spatial patterns of tap water and improved water (tap water and pump water) coverage are inverse: Regions of high levels of tap water coverage are concentrated in the western part of Pakistan with a small population scale, whereas regions of high levels of improved water coverage are located in the eastern part of the country with large populations. The urban -rural divisions between tap water and improved water coverage are also different. The spatial pattern of tap water is similar between rural and urban populations, with a huge inequality between the urban -rural coverage level. For improved water, though, urban coverage is more equal nationwide, and high rural coverage areas are concentrated on the east side of the country. These patterns imply that although the development of tap water is an urban-based (or even urban-center-based) diffusion, pump water is the major contributor to improved drinking water coverage and might be more efficient in the current stage in Pakistan. The statistics also reveal the variation of inequalities between provinces and between spatial and nonspatial measurements.

The further regression model finds out that tap water and improved water coverage are affected by different factors. Although both are related to local economic conditions, tap water coverage is more related to local environmental conditions, and improved water coverage is more related to local demographic conditions. It also reconfirms that improved water supply might be more efficient in getting more people covered with reliable drinking water. The economic conditions mainly contribute to rural coverage rather than urban coverage, as the urban population could benefit from better infrastructure in the urban area. Both types of water supply coverage are sensitive to local population distribution patterns but show distinct reversed effects. Tap water supply has high coverage with a high level of population concentration, but improved water supply can cover more population with the decentralized population with high geographical clusters. These differences could imply that the development strategies in terms of water, population, and economics should be considered comprehensively to ensure the success of regional development. The urban -rural divisions and their differences from the regional model also raise concerns about the urban periphery ' s water coverage.

Going along with the development of the water supply system in Pakistan, this study provides insights into the development of equity and equality. At the current stage, while Pakistan still has a large proportion of the population who is not covered by any improved water sources, it is hard to arbitrarily conclude that expanding improved water supply coverage with low cost and efficient means (e.g., pump water), should be the first (or only) priority. Surely getting more people covered in the less-developed regions is appropriate, as Pakistan has made tremendous progress with a low level of regional inequality. The reason that people do not rely on improved water sources, however, might not be because they are not covered. As the JMP report points out, as of 2015, 89 percent of the population is covered by at least a basic water supply, but 76 percent have high accessibility, 79 percent have high availability, and 36 percent have high quality. Contamination is a big issue, and low accessibility and availability further aggravate this problem, as water could get polluted during collection, after collection, and during storage (UNICEF and WHO 2020). It is also related to the infrastructure of sanitation facilities, as the largest water pollution sources are human activities (Azizullah et al. 2011). Thus, although expanding the coverage of improved water supply is still important, it is also essential to maintain, intensify, and upgrade the current water supply (e.g., from hand pump to motor pump) and sanitation facilities. The mismatch between spatial and nonspatial inequality indicates that despite the high coverage and (relatively) low inequality, spatial inequality and exclusion are still critical issues and should be carefully considered by policymakers. These findings are similar to other dimensions of regional inequality, such as economics, and should be stressed together (Wei et al. 2020). On the other hand, access to tap water, which might show higher potential in increasing drinking water quality than other water supply systems, is still very low nationwide. It is not only because of the infrastructure coverage, as the limited supply and quality issues might force people to turn to other water sources (Hasan, Akram, and Jeuland 2021). With the urbanization process, the high efficiency of water treatments could be stressed, especially in urban areas. Even at the current stage, developing tap water systems and increasing coverage, availability, and quality should not be ignored. There is much to do with the current tap water system because the piped water system in Pakistan also faces problems of limited availability, contamination, and infrastructure issues. On the other hand, the different factors between urban and rural populations call for equity planning and development in rural and urban regions, as the rural population rarely benefits from economic development and is more vulnerable in terms of socioeconomic conditions and water infrastructure (Ducrot and Bourblanc 2017). Local inequality and regional development patterns, reflected by the inequality of economics and urbanization, are related to the local water coverage level, which also should be addressed by planners to improve water supply efficiency and equality. Our study also indicates that polycentric regional development might be a potentially sustainable solution, as it provides the balance between centering and sprawling with strong spatial clusters that could be adjusted according to the development target to increase improved water coverage or more focused on tap water coverage. These different development targets and strategies should be carefully considered because the urban development patterns are also related to other dimensions of urban inequality (Wu, Wei, and Li 2020; Wu et al. 2022). The rural population is an essential focus, but efforts should also be put into urban fringe residents and informal settlements, as they might be the middle ground between the two water supplies with the concern of inequality and exclusion. More importantly, we propose a framework for quantitatively analyzing regional water supply coverage variations and inequality, while highlighting the role of local development patterns in water supply coverage. This provides insights for a more comprehensive understanding of regional water supply coverage by introducing local development patterns.

Still, this article has limitations, and further investigation is needed. First, as noted in the literature review, institution plays an important role in the local water supply system and should be addressed in future studies. For example, what are the roles of community, municipality, and private water services in the local water supply? Second, more efforts are needed to improve Pakistan ' s water supply systems. Our study is based on the major water sources, and as many families in Pakistan rely on multiple water sources (Luby et al. 2000), how and why they might use unsafe drinking water are substantial research concerns. Last, this study calls for deeper explorations on safely managed drinking water at both individual and regional levels regarding accessibility, availability, and quality, which could require field work and a combination of qualitative and quantitative research methods. /H17039
