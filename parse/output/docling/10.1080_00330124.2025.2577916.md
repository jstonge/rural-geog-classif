## From the ' Right to Energy ' to the ' Right to the Countryside ' and Back Again: Contextualizing Rural Energy Transitions

<!-- image -->

Matthias Naumann a and David Rudolph b

a University of Wuerzburg, Germany; b Technical University of Denmark, Denmark

<!-- image -->

Rural areas as well as their interlinkages with urban agglomerations have gained increasing attention as the main sites where the current energy transformation toward the use of renewable energy is taking place. Due to this, contestations over transitions and emancipatory efforts to transform energy infrastructures cannot be detached from the structural conditions of rural areas and their entanglements with other spatial scales. In this sense, sustainable energy transitions reflect various differences between as well as within rural areas. Consequently, rural energy transitions are embedded in broader processes of rural change. This contribution to the Focus section illustrates interconnections between energy transitions and rural change as they relate to issues of land-use change in rural areas, rural poverty, and marginalization, along with rural populism. In doing so, we argue that energy transition plays an important role for all these issues of rural development and advocate for a systematic integration of the transformation of energy systems and rural change. We, thereby, believe that contextualizing rural energy transitions can contribute to the realization of a ' right to the countryside ' and of just and sustainable rural futures within the energy sector and beyond. Keywords: land use, populism, poverty, rural areas, sustainability transitions.

C urrent transformations of energy infrastructures shape the development of cities and regions. Rural areas, however, have been particularly exposed to various effects of climate change but also transformed by responses to these threats, including the implementation of adaptation and mitigation measures such as the development of wind farms, solar parks, and biogas facilities. Rural areas have reemerged as locations where current energy transitions toward the use of renewable energy resources are being materialized (Naumann and Rudolph 2020). Rural areas have already been affected and transformed by the extraction of energy resources and the building of infrastructure for the large-scale production of electricity and heat, but the decentralized and horizontal deployment of renewable energy requires larger surface areas due a to a lower power density (Huber and McCarthy 2017). The expansion of renewable energies additionally incorporates areas in the energy system that had previously been less affected by energy infrastructures. Current energy transitions have the potential to reshape regions or result in the abandonment of spaces that have been integrated into fossil energy systems. This will produce both new energy landscapes and postcarbon landscapes in rural areas (e.g., Lennon and Scott 2017; Batel 2020; Pasqualetti and Smardon 2025). Therefore, the peculiarities and implications of rural energy transitions deserve attention from research and practice. The example of energy, however,

Furthermore, Woods ' s (2007) notion of the ' global countryside ' reminds us how rural places are intertwined with global flows of capital, resources, people, and information.

In turn, energy transitions have become formative elements of ' planetary rural geographies ' and how they are contoured (Wang, Maye, and Woods 2025a, 2025b). Planetary rural geographies have been proposed as a research agenda to incorporate rural areas as spaces of crisis, conflict, and hope (Wang, Maye, and Woods 2025a). Although crisis and conflict dimensions of rural areas have been explicitly linked to rural change, such as marginalization and poverty, transformations of land use, and emerging populism in rural areas, rural spaces of hope are associated with more equitable and just rural futures as well as the use of participatory forms of democracy to reshape rural economies. As we argue in this short contribution to the Focus section, however, although all three geographies of planetary rurality resonate with rural issues connected to the global energy transition, the transformation of the energy system should be viewed more explicitly from the perspective of rural change to allow for its critical consideration in and as part of the formative processes that entail rural spaces of crisis, conflict, and hope.

Our argument is twofold. First, infrastructures related to resource extraction and energy supply as well as their transformation profoundly impinge on rural development. Second, the expansion of renewable energy is also shaped by broader trends of rural change that provide the fundamental conditions for energy transitions. Repercussions of structural rural change on energy transitions have remained less explicit in existing research, however.

In a first attempt to shed a critical light on the effects of rural change on energy transitions in rural areas, we follow the contours of the geographies of planetary rurality as identified by Wang, Maye, and Woods (2025a, 2025b) to consider challenges such as rural land-use change, rural poverty and marginalization, and rural populism and how they relate to the expansion and utilization of renewable energies.

First, the expansion of renewable energy facilities is dependent on what land resources are available. Therefore, the utilization of energy resources contributes to conflicts around rural land use and adds further pressure on already changing and contested agricultural, residential, and touristic uses of land, for example, in coastal and marine areas. Second, rural poverty and marginalization also have an energy dimension or, turning it around, energy poverty is one aspect of the social marginalization experienced in rural areas, which creates social demands on energy transitions. Third, contested issues around energy transitions are raised by right-wing populists, especially in rural contexts that cannot be detached from wider structural issues and hardships felt in rural areas.

Due to the entanglement of these manifestations of the energy transition with different aspects of rural change, we propose a more contextualized understanding of rural energy transitions. For that purpose, we briefly outline the debates about landuse change, rural poverty and marginalization, and rural populism as well as their dialectical interrelations with rural energy transitions. All three topics are affected by and enrolled in processes of energy transitions, and rural energy transitions are taking place in a context of land-use change, poverty, and populism.

Building on that, we advocate for a more holistic understanding of rural energy transitions, where the energy sector is only one strand among a multitude of rural transformations. We argue that such a critical contextualization of rural energy transitions can contribute to the realization of a ' right to the countryside ' (Barraclough 2013), of equitable and sustainable rural futures of the energy sector and beyond. In that sense, we connect not only to other approaches for democratizing infrastructure provision (e.g., Van Veelen and van der Horst 2018) but also to wider debates on rural planning (Gkartzios, Gallent, and Scott 2022) and livelihoods (Shucksmith 2018; Gallent et al. 2025).

## Rural Energy Transitions and Rural Change

Different modes of energy provision have historically influenced rural areas and have entailed specific challenges, while also presenting opportunities for rural development. From a geographical perspective, the transition from a system based on fossil fuels toward a system predominantly based on decentral, flexible, low-carbon, and renewable energy consists of more than just substituting fuels. It involves structural changes to the energy supply, distribution, and consumption of energy, as well as a reconfiguration of related spatial patterns of economic and social activities (Bridge et al. 2013). In what follows, however, we use the term energy transitions to merely focus on transformations on the side of energy supply and its energetic sources.

Access to variable renewable energy resources like water, biomass, wind, and sun, the availability of land for the deployment of energy infrastructures, and a lower population density are main reasons why material expressions of the energy transition mostly manifest in rural areas. The focus of academic and political attention has often been on urban energy transitions, however (e.g., Cast  an Broto 2019). It is only recently that a debate about rural energy transitions has started to emerge (e.g., Clausen and Rudolph 2020; Calvert et al. 2022; Uleri, Vigan  o, and Musolino 2025). The transition toward an energy system based on renewable energy sources requires the often-contested designation and allocation of locations where new energy infrastructures can be established as well as hopes and desires to realize emancipatory energy projects contributing to rural development (Naumann and Rudolph 2020). This transition entails profound sociotechnical transformations that will have spatially uneven consequences due to legacies of the declining energy regimes, the geographical distribution of renewable resources, land requirements, and public and industrial policies supporting their uptake (Slee 2020). Additionally, consequences of localized energy transitions are relevant for rural areas in the Global North and the Global South and are, thus, also embedded in and exposed to variegated politicaleconomic dynamics that inform rural change. For example, in the Global North the COVID-19 pandemic accelerated a trend toward remote working in rural areas due to migration from urban areas to the countryside (e.g., Gonz  alez-Leonardo, Rowe, and Fresolone-Caparr  os 2022).

Land-use change, poverty, and populism are not exclusively linked to rural areas. They each feature a specific rural dimension. In the following, the three issues are used as emblematic examples for shedding light on the interrelation of energy transitions and rural development in planetary rural geographies (Wang, Maye, and Woods 2025a, 2025b). All of these issues are highly relevant for rural development but are also contested within debates about the future of rural areas and their energy systems. Therefore, the following subsections briefly introduce each issue and its amalgamation with rural energy transitions.

## Land-Use Change and Energy Transitions in Rural Areas

The large-scale rollout of renewable energy requires land on which infrastructures like solar and wind farms can be deployed, necessitating a far-reaching return to a horizontal surface-based energy regime in contrast to the use of subterranean fossil fuel sources (Huber and McCarthy 2017). Consequently, rural areas have gained critical attention in the energy transition, in particular, as contested resources for the deployment of renewables that undergo dual processes of revalorization and marginalization. In particular, linkages between available land resources in rural areas and renewable energy development have become the focus of critical scrutiny. Foremost in the rural context, renewables have turned into a formative element of the global land rush, green grabbing, and land deals (Scheidel and Sorman 2012).

The interrelationship of renewable energy infrastructures with rural areas remains ambiguous, however. On the one hand, the expansion of renewables has been associated in the Global North with expectations for rural economic development (e.g., Clausen and Rudolph 2020), linked to employment during the development and operation of the sites, the localization of the supply chain, increased tax revenues, and proactive benefit sharing. Regarding rural regions in the Global South, enhanced electrification and the local anchoring of the supply chain have also been highlighted as a positive. On the other hand, large-scale renewables are a key driver of land-use changes (Poggi, Firmino, and Amado 2018) that often interfere with the agricultural sector and contribute to the marginalization of already deprived and left-behind places, as precarious activities are displaced (Yenneti, Day, and Golubchikov 2016).

Thus, transformations of land use induced by energy transitions often involve significant contestations. As land-use changes affect agricultural landscapes and agrarian livelihoods (Paprocki and McCarthy 2024), they raise questions about the interplay between housing; tourism; nature conservation; the maintenance, intensification, or ecological modernization of agriculture; and the growth of renewable energy. The aforementioned influx of urbanites relocating (temporally) to the countryside affects the provision of housing, alters the character of rural communities, and changes the associated expectations of ' authentic ' rural landscapes. Despite the widespread articulation of a political desire to advance approaches to coexistence and creating synergies with other rural land uses, however, the commercial appetite from developers to integrate nature-based solutions remains negligible.

The energy transition imperative, the dominance of large-scale projects, coercive development practices, and land acquisitions have evoked associations of energy and infrastructural colonization (Dunlap 2021), especially in the Global South. The growing pressure on agricultural land due to the expansion of renewables is not unique to the context of the Global South. For example, the rapid expansion of large-scale solar and wind farms in Europe has increasingly been made possible partly through clandestine land acquisitions (e.g., Kirkegaard et al. 2023; M € uller and Pampus 2023). In contrast, there is a potential to reuse, repurpose, and replace energy infrastructures that enables a greater durability and a recycling of energy landscapes (Pasqualetti and Smardon 2025). For example, the use of agricultural bioenergy provides the opportunity to contribute to energy and heating self-sufficiency in rural areas, allowing residents to combine agrarian uses of land with energy production and supply while becoming independent from large and centralized energy providers (e.g., Rossi and Hinrichs 2011).

Current understandings of rural energy transitions that employ one-sided perspectives in terms of the provision of benefits, compensation, and placatory activities associated with the utilization of rural resources embedded in corporate social responsibility statements are insufficient to address the structural changes that rural areas are currently facing. Ambitions toward a large-scale rollout of renewable energy facilities in rural areas, therefore, need to be approached from a cross-sectoral perspective (Silva and Sareen 2021) that provides more flexibility through bottom-up and participatory solutions that address social issues as well.

## Poverty, Marginalization, and Energy in Rural Areas

There is an increasing awareness of the problem of rural energy poverty, especially in the Global South (Acheampong et al. 2023). For the Global North, physical access to electricity and heat supply is a less critical issue, but increasing costs can contribute to energy or fuel poverty (Roberts, Vera-Toscano, and Phimister 2015). Rising tariffs on gas, fuel, heat, and electricity supply particularly affect low-income households in rural areas.

Studies have revealed the rural characteristics of energy poverty, for example, in terms of the interlinkages between fuel and mobility poverty (Mattioli, Lucas, and Marsden 2017). In remote rural areas, in particular, car dependence is connected to a higher vulnerability to fuel poverty. Energy poverty depends not only on the income of households but also on the level of education, the conditions of buildings, and technological equipment (Boardman 2009). This illustrates that energy poverty needs to be viewed as embedded in different expressions of social deprivation in rural areas, such as the shortage of affordable housing (Kordel and Naumann 2024), or rural poverty in general (Shucksmith et al. 2023).

Rural energy transitions have ambivalent impacts on the issue of poverty. On the one hand, new forms of electricity or heat generation can provide new income opportunities for rural areas. For example, the shift toward renewable energy can enable new modes of collective ownership of the energy provision that address issues of fuel poverty as well as economic and social development in rural communities, such as the establishment of energy communities (Morrison and Ramsey 2019). On the other hand, energy transitions can also be connected to the rising costs of fossil fuels and the need for existing buildings to be retrofitted. Furthermore, in regions that used to depend on the exploitation of fossil or nuclear energy sources, energy transitions can lead to job losses in these sectors -an issue that is closely connected to the rise of antienvironmental populism (Thorleifsson 2016).

In general, rural energy transitions tend to reflect existing social differences between urban and rural areas, as well as within and across rural regions and communities. Whereas some places (e.g., bioenergy villages or self-sufficient energy communities) are able to become actively involved in energy transitions and benefit from them, other places with fewer financial resources and social capital might largely be confronted with the detrimental impacts of energy transitions, without having the opportunity to shape their local trajectories (Hanke, Grossmann, and Sandmann 2023). This discrepancy raises numerous conflicts and is increasingly being coopted by right-wing populists.

## Rural Energy Populism

The term rural populism (Jadhav 2021) illustrates the rural peculiarity of current populist movements. Rural concerns and issues have been increasingly coopted by populist movements in different countries (McCarthy 2019). A particular aim that echoes the antielitist element of populism (Mudde 2004) is to contest urban interests and transition narratives that make claims on rural areas, thereby creating tensions between urban and rural areas while neglecting their mutual coproduction within larger processes and structures of capitalist development (McCarthy 2025). On the one hand, populism preys on concerns about the expansion of renewables in rural areas and fears of rural livelihoods being converged and sacrificed to global and urban interests related to climate change (Wang, Maye, and Woods 2025b). On the other hand, populist rhetoric evokes and incites nostalgia and blame, thereby raising grievances directed toward the decline of fossil fuel industries in rural areas, along with a desire for their renaissance (Meyer 2022).

Hence, in recent years, energy infrastructures and the transformations they bring about have become an issue for right-wing populist mobilizations (Beveridge, Naumann, and Rudolph 2024). For example, Wanvik and Haarstad (2021) considered the right-wing populist resurgence against local energy transitions to merely represent ruptures and instabilities that are inherent in the transformation process toward sustainability. They, thereby omit the wider connections with the political-economic dynamics that exist. Further research has shed light on the populist struggles between renewable and fossil fuel industries (Knuth 2019), between different pathways and infrastructures of energy transitions (Batel and Devine-Wright 2018), or between the maintenance and decommissioning of fossil fuel infrastructures (Kojola 2019). In more detail, Batel, Valquaresma, and Alba (2024) explored the experienced territorial manifestations of renewable energy projects and far-right populist rhetoric in relation to the implications of the deployment of renewable energy in peripheral locations in Portugal.

As is often the case with right-wing populism in general, ' energy populism ' entails a specific rural dimension. Populist contestations over the locations of renewable energy infrastructures do not only often take place in rural regions (Dech  ezelles and Scotti 2022) but also focus on the ' values of rurality. ' Furthermore, infrastructural decline, deindustrialization, and the enclosure of land and natural resources in rural areas can serve as leverage points for rural populism. In that sense, the antienvironmental and anticlimate rhetoric of rural energy populism can be understood as a form of ideological backlash, responding to real and perceived threats to the economic position of rural regions ' left behind ' in global competition ( \_ Zuk 2025). Therefore, rural energy transitions are only one trigger for rightwing populism and they must be viewed in relation to additional issues that underlie and drive change in rural areas.

The main characteristics of right-wing populism such as the rejection of the established media, political institutions, forms of participation, and science can also be found in conflicts related to the realization of energy transitions (Haas et al. 2025). At the same time, rural energy transitions entail a potential for progressive change. Current efforts aimed at a decentralization of energy supply provide opportunities for new institutional solutions that provide ways for increased public participation and social equity within energy transitions (Becker and Naumann 2017), also in rural areas. For example, community energy projects facilitate energy democracy, aim to generate local benefits and deliver distributional justice (e.g., Creamer et al. 2019; Fichera et al. 2025). In summary, as pointed out by McCarthy (2025), rurally inflected populism has become an important element of contemporary politics that needs to be carefully considered when analyzing conflict dynamics related to rural energy transitions.

## From Rural Energy Transitions Toward a Right to the Countryside -and Back Again

As the three examples illustrate, energy transitions, in terms of the utilization and provision of renewable energy sources, and rural change are inextricably linked. The expansion of renewables has become increasingly entangled with specific problems, hardships, and wider challenges, but also hopes and desires within rural areas and for rural areas. The realization of energy transitions in rural areas has tangible consequences for their socioeconomic development. In turn, as land-use change, rural poverty and marginalization, and rural populism exemplify, rural change impinges on the possibilities and introduces barriers to the transformation of energy systems.

Therefore, we advocate for a more thorough contextualization and holistic understanding of rural energy transitions, one that situates them within general trends of rural change.

At first, such an understanding necessitates an extension of the empirical focus from energy to additional sectors in transition. Most prominently, this would need to involve the agricultural sector but also the transportation sector and efforts for their sustainable transformation, initiatives for sustainable water and waste management, the maintenance of social infrastructures, and the realization of housing justice. Such an extended focus would enable us to look at interlinkages and commonalities, along with contradictions, between different sectors of infrastructure provision and their transformations. There are numerous examples of such cross-sectoral linkages, such as the introduction of electric mobility, the energy retrofitting of affordable housing, and the use of wastewater and biofuels to generate heat and electricity.

Second, there is a need to connect different conceptual approaches to the establishment and formation of just and sustainable infrastructure futures; for example, the right to energy (Walker 2015), water

(Farhana and Loftus 2012), transportation (Sanchez and Brenman 2007), or housing (Rolnik 2014). Obviously, justice and sustainability have to be achieved through all of these (and many other) sectors and cannot be limited to one or only some of them. In turn, there is the need to elaborate the rural dimensions of these approaches, which brings us to our final point.

Third, various rights, forms of infrastructural justice, sustainability, democracy, and civic participation must become part of a ' right to the countryside ' (Barraclough 2013) to allow a ' good ' (Shucksmith 2018) or ' postcapitalist ' countryside (Gallent et al. 2025) that caters to its inhabitants, their livelihoods, and the their needs. Consequently, such rural futures question established paradigms of growth, the maximization of profit as well as the fierce competition between different rural places and regions. Like the extensive debates on the ' right to the city, ' its institutional forms, practical implementation, and contradictions, the ' right to the countryside ' requires further conceptual elaboration and practical operationalization. In that sense, the right to the countryside comprises not only just and sustainable energy provision, but also just and sustainable land use, housing, social infrastructures, and much more. A right to the countryside will not be exclusive for rural areas but needs to be considered in its entanglement and metabolism with urban regions as well (Charmes 2025). Likewise, it needs to be implemented locally but also incorporates further relations reaching beyond regions and nationstates that coconstitute rural spaces of crisis, conflict, and hope. Therefore, a right to the countryside entails new social and energetic spaces that are rural but at the same time ' glocal ' as well as ' rurban. '

As we briefly illustrated with this contribution, social change might start with the transition of energy infrastructures, but it goes far beyond it. Hence, rural energy transitions should be understood as an energy-related process rather than an energy-centered process. In a similar vein, they are addressing rural peculiarities but must go beyond them to become truly transformational. /H17039
