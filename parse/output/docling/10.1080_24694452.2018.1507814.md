Francis J. Magilligan,  L. Allan James, † Scott A. Lecce, ‡ James T. Dietrich, § and John A. Kupfer †

Department of Geography, Dartmouth College † Department of Geography, University of South Carolina ‡ Department of Geography Planning and Environment, East Carolina University § Department of Geography, University of Northern Iowa

The extreme rainfall of October 2015 in South Carolina generated numerous dam failures and spawned the flood of record at most U.S. Geological Survey stream gauges. Detailed field sampling and systematic image analysis are used to document the immediate and sustained geomorphic adjustments at four failed dams within the urbanized Gills Creek watershed. That urban focus is augmented with a similar analysis at five failed dams in more rural settings where less urban infrastructure exists. We also document the magnitude and type of geomorphic adjustments throughout the Gills Creek watershed unrelated to dam failures. Despite the extreme rainfall and associated flooding, the geomorphic effects were limited and localized, manifesting primarily at dam failures but not progressing significantly downstream, especially along Gills Creek, where the combination of intact dams, frequent urban roughness elements, thick floodplain vegetation, and numerous wetlands explains the lack of significant morphologic adjustments or major deposition. Dam failures in rural settings showed greater overbank deposition but the geomorphic effects were still limited to dam proximal locations because of the thick vegetal cover below the dam. Because these dams in rural and urban settings were constructed to maximize waterfront property, low gradient sites were selected; therefore, the geomorphic effects were unlike planned dam removals where massive headcutting, knickpoint migration, and sediment evacuation tend to occur. The most significant effect of the dam failures was the shift from an initial braided channel to a single thread channel within the emptied reservoirs, which occurred quickly, often within the first few months. Key Words: breached dams, floods, floodplains, infrastructure, urban hydrology.

D ams permeate the U.S. landscape, with more than 80,000 dams registered in the National Inventory of Dams (NID) and an additional 2 million more existing dams that fail to meet the height or volume requirements to be included in the NID database (S. V. Smith et al. 2002). Many of the nation ' s dams are obsolete, aging, and pose a great risk to society in the event of their failure (Doyle et al. 2008; Doyle and Havlick 2009), especially in urban areas where population densities are high and other at-risk infrastructure, such as roads, bridges, and culverts, commonly occur (Blanton and Marcus 2009). For example, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (ACOE) classifies more than 30 percent of its inventoried dams as having ' high ' or ' significant ' hazard potential, although their metric is more reflective of the potential for disastrous downstream social and economic effects than the actual structural integrity of the dams. Because of the aging of this dam infrastructure (where the average age of a dam in the NID is fifty-two years), the American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE) recently gave the condition of the nation ' s dams a ' report card ' grade of D (Herrmann 2013). To combat some of the liability and environmental concerns associated with dams, more than 1,300 dams have been removed over the past several decades (Bellmore et al. 2015; O ' Connor, Duda, and Grant 2015; Bellmore et al. 2017).

Dams fail for many reasons, but failures are usually associated with high precipitation storm totals or elevated streamflow that exceeds the dam ' s structural integrity (MacDonald and Langridge-Monopolis 1984; Costa and Schuster 1988; Evans et al. 2000;

O ' Connor and Beebee 2009; Walder et al. 2015). Some of the more profound natural disasters in the United States are associated with dam failures, including the infamous Johnstown Flood of 1889, where more than 2,200 people died, or the Rapid City Flood of 1972, where more than 200 lives were lost. Moreover, in the eastern United States, where recent decadal climatic trends reveal increases in both annual and extreme precipitation events (Milly, Dunne, and Vecchia 2005; Melillo, Richmond, and Yohe 2014; Huang et al. 2017), the potential for dam failure is high, yet poorly understood.

A robust literature on the structural and engineering dimensions of dam breaches encompasses the structural attributes of dams and associated breach hydraulics (MacDonald and Langridge-Monopolis 1984; Walder and O ' Connor 1997; Pierce, Thornton, and Abt 2010; Walder et al. 2015). This study, however, focuses on the geomorphic dimensions of dam breaches in relation to broader regional flood characteristics. Specifically, we examine dam failures in central South Carolina that occurred in response to an extreme rainfall event in October 2015. The resulting flooding generated immediate geomorphic and infrastructure effects; Richland County, for example, experienced nineteen documented dam breaches or failures during the storm, often along streams where multiple dam failures occurred (Figure 1). Parts of this landscape near the City of Columbia are highly altered, with high population densities and multiple recreational and flood control dams. These boundary conditions (numerous ponds and dams within urbanized watersheds, extensive impervious surface areas, increasing extreme rainfall, and dams of unknown origin or engineering specifications) provide a lens to better understand and quantify the geomorphic signature of rare events in anthropogenically altered landscapes.

To best contextualize the immediate and initial geomorphic adjustments of urban and rural watersheds to dam breaching or failure, we frame our analyses around the geomorphic literature on the effectiveness of extreme events (Gomez et al. 1997; Magilligan et al. 1998; Surian and Rinaldi 2003; Lecce et al. 2004; Gartner et al. 2015; Magilligan, Buraas, and Renshaw 2015; Surian et al. 2016; Lisenby, Croke, and Fryirs 2018). We combine that conceptual framework with the dam removal literature that empirically and theoretically represents an important proxy for dam failures, especially in its attention to differentiating dam-proximal from damdistal geomorphic impacts. In particular, the dam removal literature illustrates some important ways to query the geomorphic signature of dam failures, especially in highlighting the spatial component and the mechanisms of adjustment. Depending on the style (e.g., sediment removed or stored on site) and rate ( ' blow-n-go ' vs. staged) of removal, the geomorphic response and subsequent adjustments can be divided into distinct response zones: reaches immediately downstream of the removed or breached dam; in the immediate drained reservoir; and upstream of the former impoundment or reservoir (Sawaske and Freyberg 2012; Grant and Lewis 2015; Foley, Bellmore, et al. 2017). The character of downstream responses is governed by the caliber and strength of bed and bank materials and the duration and stream power of the dam burst and subsequent flows. The magnitude and extent of the upstream effects depend on the presence of a postremoval knickpoint, its associated rate of postremoval upstream migration, the sedimentological characteristics of the reservoir fill, and the magnitude of postremoval hydrologic events (Major et al. 2012; Sawaske and Freyberg 2012; Gartner, Magilligan, and Renshaw 2015; Grant and Lewis 2015; Magilligan et al. 2016).

To better comprehend the geomorphic effects of the October 2015 flood -in addition to the impacts of dam failures -we also extend this analysis to stream reaches quite removed from the immediate dam proximal locations to better isolate the ' natural ' fluvial response to this extreme precipitation event. Using the fluvial response to the flood and the associated dam breaks, the objectives are to (1) quantify the type and extent of geomorphic responses to regional flooding and dam breaks; (2) document the immediate and initial (Year 1) geomorphic adjustments in each of the response zones, and (3) compare the magnitude and style of geomorphic adjustments in dam proximal locations relative to the type of geomorphic responses at locations more distant from the dam. With this detailed focus on nine dam breaks, these results will contribute broadly to the dam removal literature where postremoval assessments are rare (Bellmore et al. 2017), especially in the southeastern United States (Foley, Magilligan, et al. 2017).

## Background

## Study Area

This study focuses on a set of tributaries to the Congaree River in central South Carolina that contains dams that failed in response to increased streamflows generated by the extreme precipitation events of 1 -5 October 2015 (Figure 2). The Congaree River is formed by the confluence of the Broad and Saluda rivers in Columbia, South Carolina, and flows for just 78 km before joining the Wateree River to form the Santee River, draining an area of 18,100 km 2 . The tributaries examined in this study originate in the Sandhills physiographic region, an upland composed of Cretaceous and Eocene sands with highly permeable soils (Swezey et al. 2016). They flow through gently sloping Pliocene surfaces and onto Quaternary alluvium of the Congaree lowland at the inland edge of the Coastal Plain ecoregion. Hillslopes are steeper (  3 percent) in the Cretaceous and Eocene surfaces associated with the Gills Creek watershed and upper reaches of the other tributaries and gentler in the Pliocene and Quaternary surfaces along the Congaree River floodplain (Figure 3). Local relief is generally on the order of 50 m or less.

Land use and land cover in the study area is dominated by pine and hardwood forests interspersed with developed and agricultural land. Hardwooddominated forests occur on narrow floodplains along medium-gradient streams and a mix of forest types, including cypress -tupelo ( Taxodium -Nyssa ) swamps, are prevalent on the broader, low-gradient floodplain created where the Congaree River flows onto the Coastal Plain. The most densely developed areas are associated with the City of Columbia (estimated 2016 population: 134,309) and its metropolitan area (estimated 2016 population: 817,488; data from U.S. Census Bureau 2018), which lie on the western edge of the study area. We focused on tributaries from seven subwatersheds (twelve-digit U.S. Geological Survey [USGS] hydrologic unit codes) that represent a gradient from more urbanized conditions in the northwest (Jackson Creek and Lower Gills Creek, both of which contain &gt; 65 percent developed area according to 2011 National Land Cover Database data; Homer et al. 2015) to moderately developed subwatersheds such as Upper Gills Greek and Mill Creek (20 -30 percent developed) to rural subwatersheds in the southeast with less than 10 percent developed area (Lower Colonels Creek, Cedar Creek, and Tom ' s Creek). Given the high permeability of sandy soils and dense forest vegetation, the hydrologic regime under unaltered conditions is characterized by high base flows and subdued flood peaks (Hung et al. 2018). Channels in rural areas, therefore, are adjusted to relatively small floods and have relatively low banks, small cross sections, and sandy beds. In Gills Creek, high base flows and moderated flood peaks are further fostered by numerous ponds, but channels are highly urbanized in some areas and engineered extensively between the ponds (Figure 4).

The greatest concentration of field observation locations came from the Gills Creek watershed (Table 1), which is located at the northern end of the large tributaries draining to the Congaree River. Above the Congaree floodplain, the Gills Creek watershed is an urbanized area with an average impervious cover of  19 percent and a floodplain containing abundant woody vegetation and wetlands (Table 2), important first-order controls on altering the flood hydrograph. Although the Gills Creek watershed is the most heavily developed of the systems examined in this study, there is considerable variation in the extent and potential effects of human infrastructure within the basin. For example, much of the upper main branch of Gills Creek is on Fort Jackson, where there are few ponds or road crossings, whereas a large number of ponds and roughness elements are present in lower Gills Creek below Fort Jackson and throughout the reaches of Jackson Creek, which has the dominant drainage area of the basin.

## The October 2015 Precipitation Event

Torrential precipitation from 1 to 5 October 2015 resulted from the nexus of a stalled frontal boundary located along the Atlantic Coast, circulation around a stalled upper level low over southern Alabama and Georgia, and the slow movement of Hurricane Joaquin, which intensified over the southern Bahamas from a Category 3 to Category 4 hurricane with sustained winds over 250 km hr   1 (Mizzell, Malsick, and Tyler 2016). The combined systems funneled a persistent stream of warm, moisture-laden tropical air from the Atlantic across South Carolina for three days, generating more than 50 cm of rain in some places (Feaster, Shelton, and Robbins 2015). Statewide, many sites set all-time twentyfour-, forty-eight-, and seventy-two-hour rainfall records, including North Myrtle Beach, Charleston, and Columbia (Federal Emergency Management Agency [FEMA] 2016). Initial estimates of damage losses by the South Carolina Emergency Management Department were nearly $1.5 billion (National Weather Service 2016).

The record rains that led to the dam failures examined in this study were both long-lasting and intense. Early on 4 October, the Richland County Emergency Services Gills Creek rain gauge at Forest Acres recorded hourly rainfall intensities of 4.5 cm (2:00 -3:00 a.m.), 9.5 cm (3:00 -4:00 a.m.), 7.6 cm (4:00 -5:00 a.m.), and 5.4 cm (5:00 -6:00 a.m.), for a record four-hour total of 26.9 cm (Mizzell, Malsick, and Tyler 2016). From 2 October through 5 October, a total of 54.6 cm (21.5 in.) of rain was recorded at the Gills Creek weather station. A regional analysis of rainfall recurrence intervals based on National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration Atlas 14 that was already adjusted for the drainage areas of the dams considered in this study indicated that forty-eight-hour rainfalls at all of the sites exceeded the 1,000-year return period (FEMA 2016). The resulting flooding was exacerbated by high antecedent moisture conditions caused by an additional 12.5 cm of rain that fell from 24 to 29 September, saturating the ground and filling lakes, ponds, creeks, and rivers in advance of the October event.

Gauges that did operate through the event, however, indicated record flow stages at several sites.

The best records of river discharge for urban and periurban areas come from the Gills Creek watershed (e.g., Musser et al. 2016), where the City of Columbia and the USGS maintained five stream gauges at four locations on Gills Creek that recorded rainfall and flow stages through 4 October. Both USGS gauges were damaged and failed during the peak flow (Figure 6), but a USGS postflood survey analysis of surveyed high-water marks at the Devine Street gauge indicated that the flood peaked at 5.98 m, which exceeded the highest previously recorded stage (Feaster, Shelton, and Robbins 2015). The USGS and City of Columbia also measured discharges at Rocky Branch Creek in downtown Columbia at Whaley Street (where rainfall intensities and totals were less) and recorded the second highest stage on record at 3.72 m on 4 October. The USGS streamflow gauge on Cedar Creek, which has a long record of stage and discharge, is the most central to the rural southern tributaries of this study but failed prior to the peak of the flood. Based on models of floodplain inundation at Congaree National Park (cf. Kupfer, Meitzen, and Gao 2015), however, where the gauge is located, flood stage at the Cedar Creek gauge during the event would have been at least as high as the stage of the Congaree River, which experienced its highest discharge and flood peak since 1936.

Statewide, the October storm caused the failure of at least fifty-one state-regulated, one federally regulated, and numerous unregulated dams (South Carolina Department of Health and Environmental Conservation [SC DHEC] 2017). At least nineteen dams failed in Richland County (see Figure 7 for examples), eighteen of which were on tributaries entering the Congaree River from the north. Five of the failed dams previously had been classified by the SC DHEC Division of Dams and Reservoirs Safety as having a high hazard potential, which means that they occur in places where their failure is likely to cause loss of life, serious infrastructure damage, or both. Many of the dams that held were severely damaged and lost functionality, including lowering of lake levels and closing of roads. The Gills Creek watershed alone had twenty-three dams that were regulated by SC DHEC and an unknown number of unregulated dams and ponds (HDR Engineering, Inc. of the Carolinas 2016; Figure 1). Seventeen of the twenty-three regulated dams were classified as having high hazard potential; of those, eleven were overtopped, three failed, and five others were seriously damaged (HDR Engineering, Inc. of the Carolinas 2016).

## Methods

## Field Methods

Fieldwork was conducted to determine the spatial distribution of erosional and depositional features and to characterize geomorphic changes after dam failures. Immediately after the flood, we conducted a rapid assessment of damage by visiting and identifying as many sites as possible (depending on road failures and access issues) for future follow-up. During the next several weeks and months, we visited nine dam-break sites to evaluate the development of knickpoints, collect sediment samples, document the thickness of sediment deposits, and measure maximum clast diameters. We measured the depth of knickpoints, calculated the distance of headward migration above the dam using a Global Positioning System or autolevel, and assessed the textures of fine-grain flood sediments in proximal and distal locations downstream from dam failures. Deposit thickness was measured with an Oakfield soil auger or by digging soil pits, and depths to root crowns of riparian vegetation were measured to determine the thickness of the deposits. At two sites (Forest Drive and Ulmers Pond Dam) where coarse material was deposited, we measured the b -axis of the coarsest clasts (about thirty to forty per site) along the deposit and used flood debris to estimate water depths over pond and floodplain surfaces.

We supplemented field-collected data with postflood aerial imagery and digital elevation models (DEMs) produced by unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) surveys. Based on field surveys and failures reported by the USGS, twenty-three dam-breach sites were investigated using satellite and aerial imagery. ## Results

The overall response to the extreme precipitation and subsequent flooding was spatially and geomorphologically complex and complicated by the presence of the remaining intact dams (which stored sediment and, to a lesser degree, water) and the occurrence of multiple dam failures (which provided or flushed upstream sediment). In the aggregate, the effects of the flooding were highly localized across all sampling sites and across all basins. Moreover, where major geomorphic changes occurred, as in the former reservoirs, the fluvial response and recovery revealed rapid adjustments to the newly reconnected flux of water and sediment. This section presents the geomorphic impacts within a watershed scale context of Gills Creek, including the effects resulting directly from dam failures as well as effects along the main stem unrelated to a dam failure. An array of individual dam failures across Richland County augment these watershed scale results.

## Geomorphic Impacts at the Watershed Scale

Field evidence and aerial imagery from the Gills Creek watershed indicate the generally substantial but highly localized geomorphic impacts and, in some instances, the complete absence of any geomorphic signal despite the extreme rainfall and associated flooding. In particular, detailed mapping and image analysis indicates negligible channel widening or lateral migration along Gills Creek or along its major tributaries. The most common geomorphic response was overbank deposition, but this was often confined to channel proximal locations or concentrated as sand splays. The pattern of overbank sedimentation was irregular throughout the basin because it was partly controlled by the presence of an intact reservoir or, in some instances, the presence of other urban infrastructure. Of the multiple sites visited immediately after the flood and in subsequent visits, only two sites had any substantial overbank sedimentation (i.e., below the Decker Road and Forest Drive sites). Many floodplain locations had less than 15mm of silt that was obscured by vegetation within a few months. At the Decker Road site on the upper end of Cary ' s Lake, the mean depth of deposition on a high, channel proximal levee was 8 cm, and deeper deposits occurred in four sand splays orthogonal to the channel. Cary ' s Lake Dam failed during the storm, and the sand splays suggest that the channel spilled onto the lake bed after the lake drained. Gills Creek overtopped Decker Road during the flood, so flows through and over the bridge that generated the splays must have been substantial during the peak discharge.

The only other site of observed major deposition was the Forest Drive site, which was located  300- m downstream from Forest Lake Dam and represents locally derived and locally deposited materials. This site had a series of sand splays (Figure 10) deposited across the left floodplain with maximum heights up to 0.9 m, widths up to 8 m, and extents up to 25 m obliquely from the channel. The SfM orthophoto map and DEM of a representative sand splay show the geometry, proximity to the channel, and tapered geometry for a representative sand splay for which a volume of 58 m 3 was computed from the DEM. Geomorphic and sedimentological evidence indicates that the splays were derived from local construction materials at a severely eroded bridge abutment on the downstream right bank approximately 40 m upstream. The splays are composed primarily of wellsorted medium sands but are capped irregularly with a veneer of angular to subangular fine-coarse gravels. Measurements of the coarsest gravel clasts across the splays capture the tail of the distribution as a metric of flood power. Grain sizes ranged from a mean of 58 mm in channel-proximal locations to 38 mm distally. The angularity and crystalline mineralogy of the clasts suggest that they were imported material and not transported a great distance.

The splays represent a form of natural levee construction that is driven by bedload rather than overbank suspension processes. Ritter and Blakely (1986) described a sand and gravel lobe 1 to 2 m thick and 30 to 100 m wide deposited downstream of a bridge on the Gasconade River floodplain in Missouri by 1982 floods. The Gasconade lobe had a large scour hole at its head that removed the natural levee and encouraged subsequent flows and sediment transport up onto the floodplain. The Forest Drive splays are depositional features lacking scour holes, crevasses, or other erosional features, and abundant bed material apparently ceased to be delivered during later phases of the flood. The splays are located near narrow breaks in the riparian vegetation where minimal roughness influenced overbank flows. They shifted slightly downstream after deposition as demonstrated by their final positions approximately 4 m below the vegetation breaks and by their subtle cross section asymmetry with relatively gentle stoss sides and steep slip-off faces, similar to tapered transverse dunes. Upstream sides of the splays have an abrupt transition from freshly deposited splay sand to undisturbed floodplain silts, whereas the downstream sides have a thin veneer of sand derived from the splays overlying the original floodplain. Apparently, the splays were constructed during the rising limb of the flood by bank failure upstream that generated abundant inchannel sediment and bed aggradation. This encouraged bed material transport from the channel to the floodplain, but flow velocities from the channel to the floodplain declined later during the flood as the channel bed scoured and the floodplain became inundated. At peak flow stages, flood water overtopped and scoured the road embankment east of the bridge and substantial down-valley flow velocities occurred on the floodplain that sculpted the splays.

In general, field reconnaissance at sites immediately below intact dams rarely showed geomorphic effects. Although Forest Lake Dam did not fail, the flood overtopped its crest. Local residents estimated flow depths of approximately 0.5 m over the crest and Google imagery reveals highly turbid water in the reservoir and turbulent flow over the dam releasing highly turbid floodwaters. Sediment might have been contributed locally from Eightmile Creek, which enters Gills Creek below Forest Lake and would have been deposited above the Forest Drive bridge where deep ponding and flood damages occurred. The overbank deposition along the reach below the bridge attenuated greatly with distance. At the Quail Lane site, 800 m downstream from the Forest Drive site and immediately above intact Lake Katherine, we observed minimal overbank deposition, on the order of 1 to 5 mm. Moreover, immediately below Lake Katherine at the Kilbourne Road site, channel-proximal trees had root crowns conspicuously lacking overbank sedimentation, and there was no evidence of flood-related effects (Figure 11).

## Geomorphic Effects at Failed Dams

The effects at the failed dam sites were profound (see examples in Figure 7), but they were more significant for the infrastructure (e.g., the dam, the spillway, and roads) than for the geomorphology. For many of the major dam failures along Gills Creek (Lower Rocky Creek Dam and North Lake Dam and badly damaged Spring Lake Dam), these failures debouched directly into reservoirs above intact impoundments (Figure 1) that had the potential to prevent a broad spatial signature by storing water and sediment. For example, the Lower Rocky Creek Dam, which routed sediment from both Lower and Upper Rocky Ford Lakes, is only 150m upstream from Forest Lake Dam, which did not fail.

Lakes and dams were less a part of a connected mosaic of artificial dams (typical of Gills Creek) at the four rural dam-break sites outside of the Gills Creek watershed (Ulmers Pond(s), Murray Pond, Westons Pond, and Clarkson Pond). At these rural sites, a more extensive sedimentological signature generally occurred because of greater accommodation space for the catastrophically released pulse of water and sediment. This robust sedimentological signature was typical of Murray Pond and Westons Pond, where stored sediment from the reservoir, in conjunction with sediment from the earthen fill dams, contributed to the downstream overbank sedimentation. At Murray Pond, channel-parallel longitudinal bars in dam-proximal locations grade to more continuous overbank sedimentation 200 m below the dam where Road 601 generated a backwater effect. These bars range in height from 0.2 to 0.4 m and 4 to 6 m in length.

The magnitude and extent of the geomorphic signature of a dam failure was perhaps best pronounced below Westons Pond, where the sustained sediment availability and lack of downstream infrastructure contributed to spatially extensive sedimentation. As with the pattern at Murray Pond, the initial sedimentation occurred as channel parallel bars; however, at the dam-proximal sites at Westons Pond, ramps linked to the channel bed also provided a source of coarse material ( M ¼ 47cm) that capped the bars. Shovel pits at bar crests revealed sedimentation on the order of 0.8 to 1.0 m. Deposition transitioned from streamlined, channel-parallel bars to more dispersed overbank deposition with depths thinning to 10cm at a distance of approximately 225 m below the former dam and becoming thinner and more discontinuous for the next approximately 100 m (Figure 12).

Two of the lowermost ponds at the Ulmers Pond complex failed, including the largest (maximum storage ¼ 118,000 m 3 ) of the five that drained catastrophically when both outlets failed. Sediment transported from Ulmers Pond into the failed reservoir downstream was deposited in a zone of angular, imbricated boulders (mean boulder size ¼ 260mm) and poorly sorted sands that extended 100 to 125 m downstream from the Ulmers Pond dam, which then graded into isolated discontinuous bars (Figure 13). We adduce from the imbricated boulders, more typical of a fluvial deposit, that the larger upper dam failed after the smaller unnamed downstream pond drained. The size of the boulders reflects not only the energy associated with the dam break but also the size of the material used to ' anchor ' the dam. Coarser material might have been moved by the immediate outburst flood, but the imbricated boulders correspond to the size of the boulders used to construct the dam, and their angularity and crystalline mineralogy are not associated with an upstream source.

## Geomorphic Recovery at Failed Dams

In addition to documenting the immediate impacts at each of the failed dams, we followed the trajectory of geomorphic adjustments at both urban and rural settings within the first year. One of the most striking immediate responses at each of the failed dams was the immediate emergence of braided streams within the former reservoir (Figure 14). We attribute the development of braided streams to either the channel responding to the influx of sandy sediments from reconnected upstream sediment supplies, the mobilization of sandy sediments accumulated on the floor of the former reservoir, or some combination of the two. The braided planform, however, was ephemeral. All reservoirs that fully drained (sixteen of twenty-three sites) developed a single-thread channel within a year of the flood, with ten of the sites developing a single-thread channel within the first ten days (Figure 15). Three of the remaining sites (Cary ' s Lake, Murray Pond, and Lower Rocky Creek) required between 100 and 223days to shift to a single threaded channel. The shift to a single-thread channel is conditioned by the geometry of the former reservoir (e.g., size and aspect ratio) and the presence of a well-defined predam channel (Figure 14). Reservoirs with aspect ratios greater than 4.0 develop single-thread channels in fewer than 75 days at seven of seven sites. At the three sites where single-thread channels required more than 200 days to develop, the aspect ratio varied between 0.9 and 3.4. Historic imagery showed the predam stream channel at fourteen of the sixteen sites; of these fourteen sites, eleven (76 percent) reoccupied the predam channel in the postflood period.

## Discussion

## Geomorphic Signature

Despite record-setting precipitation and the numerous dams that failed within Richland County, the geomorphic effects were highly localized and somewhat muted. At several sites (e.g., Kilbourne Road site), we did not detect any geomorphic evidence of the flood. This is not to say that the flood had few impacts but, in general, the impacts were largely restricted to damage to infrastructure (roads, culverts, and dams) with little overbank sedimentation or channel widening. The presence of frequent roughness elements and urban infrastructure (Tables 3 and 4; Figures 4 and 9) and intact dams helped mitigate the geomorphic impacts in the Gills Creek watershed. These frequent roughness elements like road and railroad crossings might act as ' bottlenecks ' at extreme flows and dissipate energy periodically. The Southeast has some of the highest densities of streets, roads, and railroads in the United States (Blanton and Marcus 2009), and our calculations for Gills Creek (Table 3) correspond with the regional numbers determined by Blanton and Marcus (2009), highlighting the significant role of urban infrastructure. The frequent intersections of roads and railroad crossings (Figure 9, Tables 3 and 4) within and along the Gills Creek floodplain, along with the periodic occurrence of intact dams (Figure 4), can minimize energy and thus bank erosion and can further disconnect the floodplain from the channel, thus limiting deposition.

For some of the sites, the failed dams drained directly into intact reservoirs, but even at sites with considerable distance to the next downstream reservoir, the spatial extent of the geomorphic signature was limited. For example, at Semmes Lake on Fort Jackson, aerial imagery along Wildcat Creek reveals a broad sandy deposit within the first 0.5 km of the dam break, but little of that sediment was transported to the Kilbourne Road site approximately 3km downstream, which lacked evidence of scouring or deposition (Figure 11). The presence of thick vegetation and wetlands (Table 2) helped dissipate the flow or provide the accommodation space for sediment deposition. Similarly, attenuated deposits occurred between the Forest Drive site and the Quail Lane site above Lake Katherine, with overbank depths diminishing from a maximum depth of 0.5m to less than 2 mm over a distance of 1 km. Moreover, field reconnaissance and detailed image analysis show undetectable channel erosion throughout the Gills Creek watershed. Unfortunately, the USGS did not measure sediment loads during the flood, but all of the imagery reveals highly turbid flows throughout the region. The lack of erosion might be related to the rivers already being at capacity and no longer having the available energy to generate sustained erosion.

The robust literature on urban hydrology and geomorphology suggests that major geomorphic changes should have occurred (Hammer 1972; Hollis 1975; Chin and Gregory 2005; Cole et al. 2017; Miller and Hess 2017), yet our results point to only modest adjustments. The combination of sporadic intact dams, frequent urban roughness elements, thick floodplain vegetation, wetlands, and rivers being at capacity could explain the lack of significant channel morphologic adjustments or major deposition. Because of the mosaic of dams along the main stem of Gills Creek, there are very few long, continuous sections that are unaffected by dams, reservoirs, or other structures, so there might have been little opportunity for unabated geomorphic response (Figures 4 and 9). The lack of major geomorphic changes on undammed tributaries (e.g., Eightmile Creek), however, where the flows could have generated greater responses, is perhaps more surprising. For these and other sites within Gills Creek, the rapid time to peak might help to explain the lack of geomorphic response. Using geomorphic evidence from dam breaks in the Western United States, Costa and O ' Connor (1995) showed that the geomorphic impacts of large floods -even those with peak unit stream powers above erosion thresholds (Magilligan 1992) -might not generate significant geomorphic effects because the cumulative time above that threshold might not have been sufficient. Recently, based on detailed field sampling at the watershed scale, Magilligan, Buraas, and Renshaw (2015) documented the profound sedimentological impacts associated with the catastrophic Hurricane Irene but the limited impact on channel erosion (cf. Buraas et al. 2014), a finding that they adduced to the rapid time to peak of this extreme flood. The lack of geomorphic adjustments seen in this study, in combination with the roughness of urban infrastructure, fully charged rivers, and ubiquitous dams, might similarly be related to the rapid time to peak of the October 2015 flood.

## Geomorphic Effects at Failed Dams as Proxy for Removed Dams

Dam removals have become increasingly common as a technique for restoring the ecological and geomorphic function of river systems, yet the literature documenting the initial and sustained geomorphic adjustments at planned dam removals is especially limited in eco-geomorphic scope. Although the dam failures analyzed here were not planned, they serve as an important proxy for the type and magnitude of geomorphic responses for planned removals, especially in an understudied region like the southeastern United States (Foley, Bellmore, et al. 2017). In particular, some of the larger scientific questions orbiting the dam removal literature concern the timing of sediment evacuation from the former reservoir, the downstream transport extent of sediment, the extent of knickpoint migration, and the timing of overall channel recovery, especially within the former impoundment (Sawaske and Freyberg 2012; Grant and Lewis 2015; Foley, Magilligan, et al. 2017; Major et al. 2017). Detailed results from an array of failed dams show very similar adjustments to planned dam removals, especially that these geomorphic and ecological responses can happen quickly. For example, the shift from a braided planform to a single-thread meandering channel in drained reservoirs happens very quickly, certainly well within the first year and frequently within the first several months of the breaching (Figures 14 and 15).

Several differences, however, exist between planned dam removals and the breached dams typical of these recreational and urban settings. Most of the constructed dams in Richland County were built to generate waterfront property; therefore, the site selection criteria differ from those in settings where the dam was constructed to generate power. For these recreational settings, low-gradient sections, which generally correspond to former reservoirs with low aspect ratios, would generate maximum waterfront property. This contrasts with the steep gradient sections that would be favored for industrial power production, typical of historic mill dams. For the failed dams investigated herein, field sites and image analysis did not detect any significant knickpoint migration or subsequent headcutting. In many instances, a new channel did not form following breaching or removal (typical of major knickpoint formation); rather, the channel development occurred by the postremoval channel reoccupying the predam channel (Figure 14) and by downstream migration of the transition to a single-threaded channel.

Previous work on dam removal indicates that more than 50 percent of sediment stored in the reservoir is evacuated within the first year -irrespective of large postremoval floods -and is conditioned more by the composition of the sediment and the rate of knickpoint retreat. These studies lack predam topographic information and bathymetric data for the impoundments, so it is impossible to quantify the exact volume of sediment evacuated. In these low-gradient reaches where major base-level changes did not occur following the breaching or removal, where vegetation quickly colonized the former impoundment, and where knickpoint migration was insignificant, however, there was little opportunity to evacuate whatever sediment was stored in these former impoundments.

Several different conceptual models have been proffered to explain postremoval channel evolution (Doyle, Stanley, and Harbor 2002; Pizzuto 2002). Because of the deep incision following base-level changes, the most commonly invoked model to portray channel changes in the former impoundment is the channel evolution model (Simon and Hupp 1986) where the initial incisional phase is followed by progressive lateral migration and floodplain formation inset into the valley fill. That model is less applicable to the settings examined in this study where headcutting and deep incision failed to occur and, instead, the postfailure channel shifts from an initial braided channel planform and reoccupies the predam single-thread channel.

Moreover, unlike most planned dam removals where downstream sediment deposition might extend for 10 1 to 10 2 km (Grant and Lewis 2015), most of the nine field sites lacked spatially extensive sedimentological evidence. For the sites along Gills Creek, the frequent occurrence of downstream dams or other infrastructure limited the longitudinal extent of sediment transport (Figure 9). The roughness of this urban infrastructure and abundant floodplain vegetation and woody wetlands combine to contain the downstream sediment flux. Even in instances where urban roughness elements were absent, such as Westons Pond, the thick woody vegetation across the floodplain in this rural setting limited the transport to within a few hundred meters. Lacking bathymetric data or sediment discharge data, it is difficult to ascertain sediment sources, but the geomorphic evidence (e.g., an absence of massive headcutting) suggests that, unlike planned dam removals, very little of the sediment supplied to the flow was derived from the impoundment.

## Conclusions

The extreme precipitation and subsequent flooding across central and coastal South Carolina in October 2015 generated profound impacts on infrastructure, including the failure of numerous dams. Detailed fieldwork and image analysis, however, reveal very limited and localized geomorphic impacts. Furthermore, the postfailure shift in channel planform within the empty reservoir from a braided channel to a single-thread meandering channel occurs rapidly, typically within the first year and often within the first several months. The geomorphic literature is replete with evidence that large floods might not produce profound geomorphic effects, with explanations ranging from the role of diminished channel slope in large alluvial rivers (Dury 1973), supply-limited conditions (Magilligan et al. 1998), to diminshed shear stresses in unconfined systems (G. H. S. Smith et al. 2010). Our results suggest, however, that in human-altered systems, the effect of urbanization might, in some circumstances, contribute to the general geomorphic ' ineffectiveness ' of an extreme event in part due to the overall resistance of the system (Phillips 2009) or where local contingencies might occur to help explain the modest geomorphic impacts. For example, the geomorphic impacts would have, no doubt, been more pronounced if the Forest Lake or Lake Katherine dams had also failed.

The other important boundary condition controlling the flood ' s overall ineffectiveness is the selection criteria and purpose of regional dams. As recreational dams are built to maximize waterfront acreage, these dams occupied gently sloping river reaches, so the channel response differed in many ways compared to existing conceptual models used to ' predict ' the magnitude and spatial extent of geomorphic impacts following planned dam removals where rapid and sustained headcutting occurs following removal. In instances where channel slopes are low, impoundments have low aspect ratios, and where reservoirs lack thick deposits of accumulated sediment, the geomorphic response to dam failure might be muted.

Our spatial and temporal results have important management implications. Although muted geomorphic impacts manifested, they were greatly overshadowed by the ubiquitous impacts on urban infrastructure and urban and rural impoundments. With many of the existing dams in the state located in high population centers, they pose a significant risk in the event of their failure. As recent historical analyses document and climate change models predict, extreme precipitation events have been, and will continue to be, increasing significantly in the eastern United States, thus, this extreme flooding might become the new normal, ultimately requiring urban and regional planners to develop mitigation strategies to confront the possibility of increased flooding and dam failures.
