## ' We Spray So We Can Live ' : Agrochemical Kinship, Mystery Kidney Disease, and Struggles for Health in Dry Zone Sri Lanka

Nari Senanayake

Department of Geography, University of Kentucky, USA

In March 2015, Sri Lanka ' s then-President Maithripala Sirisena launched the Toxic Free Nation Movement as a long-term solution to a mysterious form of kidney disease (CKDu) now endemic in the island ' s dry zone. As part of this strategy, in 2016 the movement worked with farmers in north-central Sri Lanka to cultivate indigenous rice varieties without agrochemicals. Yet, within a year, 80 percent of farmers who experimented with indigenous and organic rice farming had switched back to some form of agrochemically intensive cultivation. In this article, I examine farmers ' narratives of why this happened, demonstrating how the movement ' s conceptualization of agricultural harm often missed the forms of accounting most salient for residents themselves. Instead, through their testimonies, residents track how polyvalent relationships with agrarian toxicity mediate (1) vulnerabilities to simple reproduction squeezes, (2) reliance on grain fungibility, and (3) strong but bittersweet attachments to dry zone agrarian landscapes. As a consequence, I document how residents respatialize their knotted relationships to agrarian toxicity to include moments of what I call ' agrichemical kinship. ' I argue that this optic helps us grasp the ways in which agrochemicals simultaneously erode and enable modes of social reproduction against a backdrop of rural stagnation. Following feminist scholars of toxicity, this article not only reveals intimate, yet undertheorized, connections between the field of toxic geographies and the concept of social reproduction but also dashes hopes of any simple equation between banning agrichemical inputs and enacting health in the wake of CKDu. Key Words: agrochemical kinship, dry zone Sri Lanka, mystery kidney disease, social reproduction, toxic geographies.

T errance grew up in Sri Pura, the son of firstgeneration settlers who moved to the area in the 1960s. He, like many of the residents in the region, lives in a state-sponsored agrarian settlement scheme designed to revitalize rice cultivation in Sri Lanka ' s dry zone. Located on the border of the North, North-Central, and Eastern Provinces, Sri Pura is predominately populated by Sinhala Buddhist settlers who engage in smallholder rice (paddy) farming. Crucially, the rollout of dry zone colonization largely overlapped with the Green Revolution, including the adoption of high-yielding seeds, chemical fertilizers, and pesticides. As a consequence, agrarian livelihoods in the area have long been tied to chemically dependent forms of agriculture. More recently, this corner of north-central Sri Lanka has been thrust onto the national stage as ground zero for an epidemic of mysterious kidney disease. Although the causes of this illness are hotly contested, hypotheses linking it to the use of agrochemicals have captivated popular imagination, finding expression in Sri Lankan villages and global media outlets alike (see Chatterjee 2012; Bandarage 2013a, 2013b; Jayasumana 2014). Reflecting on the complex entanglements between rice cultivation, agrochemicals, and health, Terrance recounted a recent argument he had in the waiting room of a new kidney disease clinic:

While I was waiting to be called at clinic one afternoon, I overheard two doctors chastise another kidney disease patient. They asked him if he was ' still spraying poisons? ' When he replied ' Yes, ' they demanded that he immediately ' stop poisoning other people ' by continuing to spray agrochemicals on his rice fields. Angered by hearing this exchange, I turned to the doctors and asked them directly: ' If we don ' t use agrochemicals, then how can we produce enough food? If we stop using chemicals, where do you think you will get food? I have tried cultivating deshiya [indigenous] rice varieties without agrochemicals but there are times when there is no one to buy the harvest. If you really want us to produce without toxins, then we need to have buyers for this rice. I have multiple telephone numbers of people who promised to come and purchase this grain … we call and call, and I am still waiting. …' But my questions fell on deaf ears. The doctors didn ' t respond. In fact, they didn ' t speak to me or acknowledge me at all after that.

This encounter captures the multidimensional nature of violence operating in Sri Lanka ' s north-central dry zone, where chronic kidney disease of unknown etiology (CKDu) is now endemic. First, the very existence of a new kidney disease ward is indicative of the violence that this disease inflicts on bodies. At present, more than 52,000 patients are being treated for renal failure in government hospitals ( ' Sri Lanka Renal Registry ' 2021) with approximately 5,000 deaths attributed to kidney disease annually (Balachandran 2018). Second, the narrative of blame exchanged through this encounter reveals a discursive violence directed at residents who fail to live up to the scripts written for and about them. Critically, this account depoliticizes environmental contamination and positions residents as at risk or, in the case of farmers, as producing risk for others because of individual decisions. Finally, this fraught encounter reveals an epistemic violence: a violence that dismisses local or informal accounts of toxicity and implies that the relationships between health and the dry zone environment -or, in this case, between health and agrochemical use -are a onesize-fits-all kind of relationship (also see Davies [2019] for a discussion of epistemic violence and toxicity). In fact, interactions between health and the agrarian environment are multiple, mediated by key political economic and ecological contexts, and they are also often contradictory.

In this article, I open a window onto residents ' complex and polyvalent relationships with agrarian toxicity in the shadow of both CKDu and ongoing attempts to improve health in disease hotspots. I focus specifically on farmers ' encounters with the Toxic Free Nation Movement or Vasa Visa Nethi Ratak, a recent initiative that seeks to reinvent the relationship between health and agrarian environments by reintroducing indigenous rice varieties to the dry zone and also preventing the use of agrochemicals in the farming sector. Launched in 2015 by the Presidential Taskforce on Kidney Disease Prevention, this movement positions itself as part of a long-term solution to kidney disease ( ' A Toxic Free Nation: A Wholesome Agro-Culture, A Healthy Populace ' 2015). Through Vasa Visa Nethi Ratak ' s ambitious agenda, agrochemical harm has acquired greater visibility as a potential cause of kidney damage in both public health discussions and media circles, despite intense and ongoing scientific speculation about the etiology and prevalence of CKDu (Bandara et al. 2008; Chandrajith et al. 2011; Bandarage 2013a, 2013b). Emphasizing the need for ' toxic-free ' and ' wholesome ' agriculture, Vasa Visa Nethi Ratak worked with smallholder rice farmers in the agricultural settlement scheme of Sri Pura to cultivate 1,000 acres of indigenous ( deshiya ) paddy varieties without chemical inputs over the main farming season of 2015 -2016. Yet, by the summer of 2017, 80 percent of farmers who experimented with deshiya or organic rice cultivation in this region had switched back to agrochemically intensive forms of cultivation. Terrance, for instance, embittered by the absence of stable and profitable markets to absorb his deshiya rice harvest, returned to cultivating improved rice varieties with chemical inputs within six months. By focusing on farmers ' individual decision making, the Toxic Free Nation Movement promotes a form of precautionary cultivation that short-circuits inquiry into the social and political -economic factors that tie residents to agrochemicals in complex ways.

Challenging the Toxic Free Nation Movement ' s conceptualization of harm, Terrance ' s story instead reveals a more relational, contingent relationship to agrochemicals, which is attentive to how residents ' patterns of chemical dependence index polyvalent forms of value and traverse distinctions between productive and reproductive spheres. Indeed, in contrast to the movement ' s conceptualization of agrochemicals as purely ' villainous objects with violent effects ' (Balayannis and Garnett 2020, 1), this article documents how residents also recognize moments of agrochemical kinship that enable everyday life and cultural longevity in the context of a stagnant rural economy. Put simply, the concept of agrochemical kinship forces us to grapple with how relationships between agrochemicals, harm, and health manifest in situated ways.

My argument unfolds in four parts. I begin by locating the concept of agrochemical kinship as a response to the Toxic Free Nation Movement ' s narrow conceptualization of agrochemical harm as well as recent scholarly work on everyday life in toxic spaces. I extend this optic by building on existing work by feminist scholars of toxicity to reveal intimate, yet undertheorized connections between agrochemical dependence and social reproduction. Specifically, I argue that the concept of social reproduction is analytically important to the literature on toxic geographies because it directs attention to the polyvalent uses and values of agrochemicals in daily life. Simply put, it helps reveal the myriad ways in which toxicity mediates forms of sociality that are necessary for everyday survival. Following this discussion, I situate my analysis in historical context. I highlight both the enduring cultural significance of rice cultivation and the stagnation of rural economies that not only limits the types of employment open to residents but also intensifies the productivity demands of rice cultivation. I then turn to my ethnographic material to flesh out the scope and meaning of agrochemical kinship. Against a backdrop of life-negating structural forces that lock residents into chemically dependent rice farming, I carefully describe how agrochemicals enable myriad modes of social reproduction. Although well intentioned, I argue that how the Toxic Free Nation Movement ' s programs count and categorize agrochemical harm often miss the forms of accounting most salient for residents themselves, which track vulnerabilities to the simple reproduction squeeze and changes in grain fungibility that facilitate household resilience and encompass strong but bittersweet attachments to rice farming in the region. I conclude by arguing that these knotted relationships to agrarian toxicity require a fundamental rethinking of the spatiotemporality of risk that is experienced by dry zone residents.

The findings presented in this article draw from fifteen months of ethnographic research (from 2013 -2017) in the agricultural settlement scheme of Sri Pura. 1 As part of this fieldwork, I conducted ethnographic interviews with thirty-eight residents in the study site. Many of my interlocutors were interviewed during the summers of 2013 and 2015 and several times during 2016 and 2017. In these conversations, I paid particular attention to how interviewees understood agrarian toxicity and agrochemical dependence and how perceptions and agricultural practices changed over time. All interviews were conducted in Sinhala and I transcribed and translated the interview transcripts. 2 Transcripts were coded using a mix of inductive and a priori codes to help capture residents ' complex relationships to agrochemicals in this setting. In addition, as part of my larger project, I interviewed eighteen local government officials, thirty-four activists, fourteen academics, and ten medical practitioners. These conversations help contextualize the findings of this article. Based on preliminary analysis of interview data, I developed a structured survey of 136 households. Surveys used a nonprobability sampling design, which establishes trends and themes in villagers ' experiences, behaviors, and understandings (McGuirk and O ' Neil 2010). These surveys included families with and without a family member with kidney disease and were conducted in a sample of three Grama Niladhari (G.N.) divisions (Sri Lanka ' s lowest administrative unit) in Sri Pura. Surveys focused on (1) settlement histories, (2) demographic and household information, (3) livelihood portfolios, (4) water consumption practices, (5) disease profiles (including rates of co-disease burden), (6) dietary choices, and (7) cultivation practices. For this article, I analyzed survey data for patterns in the uptake of toxic-free agriculture and used these data to generate descriptive statistics to frame and contextualize the key themes that emerged from my ethnographic analysis. Because data collection unfolded over several ethnographic moments in 2013, 2015, and 2016 and 2017, I was able to document key shifts in cultivation practices, the emergence of new sets of relations between residents and the agrarian environment, and residents ' perspectives about health, agrarian change, and toxicity.

## Agrochemical Kinship

This article develops the concept of agrochemical kinship to explicate how patterns of chemical dependence in the wake of CKDu are tied to residents ' struggles to sustain everyday life and cultural longevity or to accomplish social reproduction. My argument is informed by a recent turn in the literature on everyday life in toxic spaces that emphasizes the deeply relational and situated character of our relationships with chemicals (Chen 2012; Agard-Jones 2013; Murphy 2017a, 2017b; Romero et al. 2017; Todd 2017; Valdivia 2018; Balayannis and Garnett 2020; Geissler and Prince 2020; Nading 2020). Although important, I argue that these accounts of how toxicity simultaneously erodes and enables life are incomplete without a corresponding analysis of social reproduction -or how the ' lively practices of everyday life and the myriad efforts to secure these ' (Katz 2009, 697) are conditioned by webs of chemical dependence. Before elaborating on these arguments, I first highlight several limits of the Toxic Free Nation Movement ' s conceptualization of agrichemical harm.

## ' A Wholesome Agroculture, a Healthy Citizenry, a Toxin-Free Nation '

As part of their work in Sri Pura, the Toxic Free Nation Movement sought to reintroduce indigenous rice varieties to the dry zone and also prevent the use of agrochemicals in the farming sector ( ' A Toxic Free Nation: A Wholesome Agro-Culture, A Healthy Populace ' 2015). This included distributing indigenous rice seedlings and holding workshops on organic cultivation techniques and consciousnessraising events about agrochemical harm, particularly in the context of kidney disease. In this moment of heightened anxiety about environmental risks, the most urgent warnings targeted the use of agrochemicals in rice cultivation, despite scientific uncertainty about the role that agrochemicals actually play in kidney disease development. Drawn into contested debates about CKDu ' s etiology, agrochemicals ' identity as neurotoxins and possible conduits for heavy metals like cadmium or arsenic captivated both local and national attention (Bandarage 2013a, 2013b; Ranawaka 2013; Jayasumana 2014; Ji 2015).

Recent headlines on the movement ' s work make these connections apparent: ' Killing fields: Agrochemicals and kidney disease ' (Bandarage 2013a) and ' Agro-chemicals: The silent killer in Sri Lanka ' (Ranawaka 2013). These stories position Sri Lanka ' s trajectory of agricultural modernization as the cause of the kidney disease epidemic and, in doing so, encode long-standing narratives about how agrochemicals shape human health (see Carson 2002). In the context of Sri Lanka, these headlines are often paired with another proclamation: The solution to modern agriculture is a return to a more ' wholesome, ' traditional, and indigenous form of cultivation (see N. De Silva 2013a, 2013b; ' Major drive to detoxify Lanka begins today ' 2016; Gunatillake 2018). These two axioms mirror the structure of the Toxic Free Nation Movement ' s three-year plan and are frequently reproduced by segments of the media, as well as some doctors and scientists. Although these narratives index the deeply problematic legacies of the Green Revolution, they also reproduce modernist ideas of purity and flatten complicated historical and socioeconomic realities that continue to tie residents to agrochemical use in this setting.

Equally problematic is how the movement conceptualizes toxicity as a discrete event that can be spatialized in a bag of chemical fertilizer or bottle of pesticides, rather than as fluid, contextual, and a social relation. For instance, through their planning documents, promotional materials, and media coverage, the Toxic Free Nation Movement focuses on ' systematically eradicating ' the ' unrestrained application of agro-toxins for weed and pest control ' by providing farmers with organic and natural alternatives and educating them about agroecological farming practices ( ' A Toxic Free Nation: A Wholesome Agro-Culture, A Healthy Populace ' 2015, 25 -26). Crucially, within the Toxic Free Nation Movement ' s framework, agrochemical harm is seen as resulting from farmers ' individual cultivation practices. As a representative of the movement explained during one of our conversations,

We don ' t provide any financial assistance in our programs. Money won ' t provide lasting solutions; instead, we focus on providing farmers with the knowledge that it is possible to cultivate toxic-free and that it is possible to once again engage in natural or ecological farming. We provide trainings to farmers to help them make this switch … but it is up to farmers to choose these practices.

This framing tends to neglect larger drivers of structural vulnerability in the dry zone and emphasizes neoliberal concepts of personal responsibility for managing exposure to toxic harms. The solutions that this movement proposes are accordingly, geared toward providing access to indigenous seed, organic inputs, and knowledge of chemical risks. Their flagship policy is a ban on agrochemical use. Thus, at its core, the Toxic Free Nation Movement promotes a form of ' precautionary cultivation ' that can be critiqued as a ' liberal band aid that diverts attention from more trenchant, collective interventions aimed at protecting populations made structurally vulnerable to toxic trespasses because of their poverty, racialized minority, immigrant or undocumented status; or Indigenous ties to place, as well as combinations of these factors ' (Lee 2020, 13).

Agrochemicals are thus increasingly addressed within a production-centric paradigm rather than a multidimensional one. This approach runs the risk, however, of performing ' epistemological enclosure ' and erases important forms of accounting that are salient to residents of toxic spaces (Tuck 2009; Davies and Polese 2015; Stawkowski 2016; Valdivia 2018). For example, programs that ban agrochemical use and position continued chemical dependence as evidence of flawed individual decisions obscure the myriad ways in which agrarian toxicity enables forms of social reproduction particularly in the context of limited economic and social opportunities. As I detail in what follows, residents ' testimonies articulate how livelihood struggles and anxieties about cultural reproduction are central to their decisions to either take up or abandon toxic-free agriculture. These testimonies demonstrate how agrochemical dependence traverses the artificial separations of ' productive ' and ' reproductive ' spheres in agrarian societies, even as these chemicals tie residents to a status of precarity and poverty. These complex relationships to agrarian toxicity are ill-matched to the movement ' s narrow focus on seeds and inputs and the norms of purity and containment that such interventions support.

## Beyond Narrow Conceptualization of Harm: Chemical Kinship and Social Reproduction

At the crux of theorizing a more relational and situated account of agrochemical dependence in the dry zone is the field of toxic geographies. Within this subdiscipline, geographers have turned to theorizations of violence (Davies 2018, 2019; Nunn 2018), environmental justice (Mah and Wang 2019; Davies and Mah 2020), and intimacy (Bagelman and Wiebe 2017; Tironi 2018; Vasudevan 2021) to center residents ' narratives, perceptions, and judgments of polluted environments. Central to this literature is a focus on ' the longstanding relationships and emergent social forms that arise from chemical exposures and dependencies ' (Shapiro and Kirksey 2017, 484; see also Agard-Jones 2013; Murphy 2017a, 2017b). As a consequence, this scholarship indexes the ' mass patterning ' of human life by chemicals, recognizing that there is no simple way to extricate ourselves from the chemical entanglements that have altered the conditions of all life (Calkins 2019, 44; see also Murphy 2017a). As such, the focus in much of this work is on documenting the contradictory and complex ' economic, personal, political, and sentimental relationships that have emerged … from [persisting within] shared and shifting chemical ecologies ' (Shapiro and Kirksey 2017, 482 -84).

With the rise of these perspectives, the concept of chemical kinship holds much promise for understanding complex ' chemosocialities, ' thus extending scholarship in the field of toxic geographies. By tracing the emergence of ' deep affective ties ' with nonhuman presences and entities, work in this vein relocates the idiom of kin to objects and sites that seldom figure in accounts of kinship (Kafer 2019, 5; see also Chen 2012; Haraway 2016). In doing so, this literature helps reconceptualize what relations can sustain life in this new ' age of toxicity ' (Walker 2011). For example, Agard-Jones (2016) used the concept of chemical kinship to signal bonds that are created through toxic exposures. These bonds can traverse territorial boundaries, axes of social difference, and family bloodlines to generate ' communities of chemical injury ' (see also Petryna 2013). Crucially, for Agard-Jones (2016), linking these territorially dispersed and socially differentiated communities through forms of chemical kinship recalibrates questions of accountability and justice. Specifically, her work forces us to grapple with the myriad ways in which chemical entanglements function simultaneously as hazard and protection or as harm and possibility. Highlighting the sometimes protective potential of chemical kinship, Roberts (2017) explored ' both the pleasures and the perils of [toxic] permeability, porosity, and penetration in contemporary Mexico City … [as well as] how persistence might exist despite permeability and porosity -and also because of them ' (597, italics added). Reflecting on the polyvalent relationships to toxicity that emerge in Colonia Perif  erico, Roberts described toxic entanglement as a form of ' protective porosity ' that helps prevent violent forms of police and public health surveillance. In doing so, she illustrated how toxicity sustains life and social relationships at collective levels, even as it might make residents sick. Fleshing out the concept of chemical kinship in a recent article, Balayannis and Garnett (2020) argued that by approaching chemicals in terms of their relations, this optic ' involves a tentativeness towards making normative claims about chemicals because, like kin, these materials are never entirely good or bad; at once, they can both be enabling and harmful ' (1). Together, this work pushes back against accounts of toxicity that ' reduce chemicals to their violent effects, [while] overlooking the material ways they participate in the structuring of social and political life ' (Balayannis and Garnett 2020, 2). In doing so, they create space for more complicated notions of agency, complicity, and resistance in the field of toxic geographies, particularly in contexts where possibilities for a dignified life and strategies for happiness are paradoxically tied to remaining in toxic spaces.

In this article, I flesh out these kinds of messy, fleshy, and paradoxical kinship entanglements with agrochemicals in Sri Lanka ' s dry zone. Specifically, I seek to build on the potentials of this scholarship by more explicitly engaging with the feminist concept of social reproduction and by extending the optic of chemical kinship to new sites and agrarian actors. Given that moments of chemical kinship are often entangled with daily and long-term practices of survival as well as polyvalent forms of value, I contend that struggles for social reproduction are central to theorizing a more relational and situated account of agrochemical dependence in the dry zone.

Broadly defined as the ' activities and attitudes, behaviours and emotions, responsibilities and relationships directly involved in the maintenance of life on a daily basis, and intergenerationally, ' social reproduction has generated a vibrant discussion in the discipline of geography (Laslett and Brenner 1989, 382 -83). Katz (2001) dialectically linked the concept to circuits and crises of production, conceptualizing social reproduction as ' life ' s work ' or the ' social practices through which people reproduce themselves on a daily and generational basis [to renew] … the social relations and the material basis of capitalism ' (709; see also Mitchell, Marston, and Katz 2004; Bakker and Silvey 2008; Strauss and Meehan 2015). Fraser (2016) elaborated on the mutually constitutive and fraught relationship of social reproduction to capitalism, observing that on the one hand, ' social reproduction is a condition of possibility for sustained capital accumulation; on the other hand, capitalism ' s orientation to unlimited accumulation tends to destabilize the very processes of social reproduction on which it relies ' (100).

Owing a significant intellectual debt to this lineage of feminist scholarship, literature that integrates agrarian studies and social reproduction has become increasingly wide-ranging in its ambit, taking up questions on in situ displacement or social dispossession (Makki 2014; Paprocki 2016), shifts in gendered labor burdens (Nichols 2016; Nyantakyi-Frimpong and Bezner Kerr 2017), spatial reproductive squeezes (Ghertner 2020), translocal householding (Gidwani and Ramamurthy 2018), and polyvalent forms of value (Li 2014; Narotzky and Besnier 2014; Chung 2017), as well as collective appropriations of space or practices of commoning (Gibson-Graham 2006; Ghertner 2020).

Across this literature, scholars unsettle distinctions between ' work ' and ' nonwork ' as distinct spheres of activity in many agrarian settings. By highlighting the instability of these conceptual categories, critical agrarian scholars map heterogenous labor practices and social relations that transgress neat productive -reproductive binaries. The thrust of this scholarship is to foreground the ' socially necessary, but often unwaged and invisible, forms of labour ' that are required for the reproduction of agrarian livelihoods and landscapes across intergenerational timescales (Winders and Smith 2019, 871). In doing so, this work at once pushes back against the ' elision of unpaid work from theories of value ' at the same time that it broadens conceptualizations of value in ways that ' don ' t rely on monetization and traditional definitions of productive activity ' (Strauss and Meehan 2015, 3; see also McClintock 2018). As a consequence, social reproduction is analytically important because it brings into sharp relief the forms of accounting most salient in daily and generational struggles for sustenance and resilience. Put simply, it directs attention to the multiple uses and values of land, labor, and grain in daily life, particularly for agrarian societies.

Although these interventions serve as an important springboard for geographic work, few studies examine how the functional relationship between social reproduction and capital accumulation in agrarian settings is mediated by relationships of toxicity. To help bridge this gap, I pair the concept of chemical kinship with work on social reproduction to make two important interventions. First, I document how agrochemical dependence both enables and erodes important modes of social reproduction in the context of simple commodity production -the most common form of cultivation in Sri Lanka ' s rice fields. An analysis of social reproduction is necessary because agrochemical dependence does not stop at the field gate but rather hemorrhages into sites of social connection, household resilience, and cultural longevity in the dry zone. Reading agrochemical relations through the concept of social reproduction thus leads to an understanding of these chemicals as more than just sources of toxic pollution or inputs for agrarian production but also as operating within dense networks of sociality that are often necessary for everyday and intergenerational survival.

Second, this conceptual pairing encourages us to grapple with how agrochemicals index polyvalent values for residents of agrarian societies. Specifically, by focusing on how agrochemicals are enrolled in the spaces and practices of social reproduction, this approach holds the potential to recenter the conceptions of health and well-being that dry zone residents identify as significant to their lives. In sum, this conceptual framework allows us to move beyond categorizing residents ' return to chemical inputs as evidence of how they are duped or lacking agency. Instead, this article responds to Tuck ' s (2009) call to shift analyses away from documenting damage and toward foregrounding complex personhood that highlights the values that residents themselves place in agrochemicals, values that are not yet fully understood by existing research or public health interventions (see also Galt 2013; Kimura and Katano 2014). Accordingly, this article zooms in on moments of agrochemical kinship to reveal how struggles for social reproduction tie residents to agrarian toxicity in complex and multidimensional ways.

## History and Context: Land, Identity, and Livelihood in Dry Zone Agrarian Systems

In the dry zone, patterns of agrochemical kinship emerge within three contexts: (1) the enduring cultural significance of rice cultivation, (2) stagnating agrarian economies, and (3) agrochemical-dependent ecologies. As part of a larger program of ' colonization ' rolled out between the 1950s and the 1970s, dry zone settlers were allocated between two and three acres of land for cultivation (see Peebles [2006] for a history of settlement programs). Under this system, rice was to be ' grown wherever suitable ' and the ' sale, fragmentation, or mortgage of paddy lands were restricted without official consent ' (K. M. De Silva 1981, 305). As a consequence, farming in the area is characterized by smallholdings, family labor regimes, rice or paddy cultivation for local markets or subsistence, and peasant -proprietor tenurial regimes, all of which help reproduce the paddy ' s unique role in household economies.

State-sponsored agricultural settlements not only transformed demographic patterns in the dry zone but also, crucially, transformed traditional paddy ecologies. Indeed, in Sri Lanka, the rollout of colonization schemes largely overlapped with the adoption of Green Revolution technologies such as highyielding seeds, chemical fertilizers, and pesticides. According to Zubair (2005), the Green Revolution changed the practice of agriculture in the region ' from an organic multi-culture with indigenous crops aided by buffaloes, to a chemically intensive mechanized monoculture ' (164). An analysis of agricultural census data from 1940 through 2000 indicates that the uptake of chemical fertilizers in paddy farming only really took off in the late 1960s despite the introduction of a generous fertilizer subsidy scheme in 1952 (Bansil 1971; Ekanayake 2009). Although the subsidy scheme has continued in one form or another to this day (with a brief suspension in the 1990s), dependence on chemical inputs has substantially increased over this period, from an average of 41 pounds per acre in 1968 (Bansil 1971), to new heights of 152 pounds in 1997 and 250 pounds in 2004 (Ekanayake 2009). 3

The story and patterns of pesticide use in the north-central dry zone follow a similar trajectory. According to the Sri Lankan Department of Agriculture (2006), synthetic pesticides were first introduced in the late 1940s and formed the cornerstone of malaria eradication campaigns during this period. By the early 1960s more synthetic insecticides such as aldrin and endrin were imported to the island for the control of pests in farmers ' fields. According to Bandarage (2013b), however, pesticide use in rice farming only became widespread after key economic policy shifts favoring import liberalization and structural adjustment in 1977. She claimed that ' between 1983 and 1997, the quantities of formulated pesticides (insecticides, herbicides, and fungicides) imported increased tenfold ' (Bandarage 2013b, 6). 4 This trend was confirmed by Sumith (2005), who drew on statistics from the Office of the Registrar of Pesticides to argue that ' the continuous dependence on pesticides had brought a dramatic increase of imports … from 2309 metric tons in 1980 to 5120 metric tons in 2003 ' (3).

The increased use of agrochemicals in paddy farming fundamentally restructured north-central landscapes, systems of production, and relations of expertise. In particular, chemically intensive agriculture gave rise to key ecological and social disruptions in the region, entrenching mutually reinforcing systems of dependence, particularly between farmers and the knowledge of chemical manufacturers and markets for chemical inputs. Parallel systems of dependence emerged between crop productivity and the effects and availability of fertilizers or pesticides (also see Yapa [1998] for a detailed discussion of these transformations in the Sri Lankan case). To summarize, dry zone paddy farming unfolds in chemically dependent ecologies that render organic and deshiya cultivation costly in terms of yield and income. Through discussions with farmers and activists, I learned that on average it takes about three seasons to cultivate deshiya rice varieties without significant yield losses (this is on top of the natural yield difference that exists between deshiya and high yielding varieties seeds).

For most residents in Sri Pura, however, the ability to wait for paddy soils to biologically regenerate themselves is a luxury they cannot afford. Instead, most residents derive either an important source of livelihood or identity from rice cultivation and live season to season. According to recent statistics compiled by the Sri Pura Divisional Secretariat Division (2015), 45 percent of families rely solely on cultivating paddy as ' peasant proprietors ' for their main source of income and a further 10 percent relied on seasonal wage labor in rice fields to sustain their families. 5 Even residents who have additional sources of income identify as part-time farmers and routinely rely on paddy cultivation to cover key household expenses. This is, in part, because ownership of paddy lands disqualifies residents from receiving poverty welfare benefits and grants them access to farm subsidies (Rs. 5,000/acre during the course of my fieldwork). As many of my interlocutors explained, ' We cannot survive cultivating paddy alone, but without paddy, few can survive. '

Indeed, the struggles of many farm families cannot be disaggregated from the more generalized structures that truncate and devalue life itself in these spaces, such as the consolidation of rice value changes, deskilling, and mounting levels of debt. In fact, chronic indebtedness among Sri Pura residents is endemic and reproduced through high costs of production, rising costs of living, and seasonal droughts, which all profoundly shape paddy production systems in the area. Among my survey respondents, 73 percent reported household debts that curtailed their production and consumption decisions. Moreover, because of the extraordinarily high rates of rainfall variability in the area, farmers cannot always cultivate two seasons a year, and often must cover their household expenditure for twelve months with the income generated from one season of cultivation, other income-generating activities, and credit raised against the next season ' s harvest. 6 As a consequence, losses are not easily absorbed, and past debt obligations continue to restrict the profitability of paddy cultivation long after the rains return or the price of grain rises.

Furthermore, farmers ' abilities to hold onto their paddy harvest are severely circumscribed by these debt relations and conventional rice value chains that marginalize farmers ' fields as a site of profit. In Sri Lanka, markets for improved rice varieties are dominated by a small number of private buyers and distributors who have extensive granaries and milling capacity. Although the Sri Lankan government created a Paddy Marketing Board (PMB) in 1971 to purchase the products of local farmers at an assured price (currently at Rs. 38 per kilogram), during the course of my fieldwork in Sri Pura the PMB only purchased about 8 percent of the total harvest. As many farmers explained, to qualify for the assured price, farmers ' harvests must meet strict requirements for moisture content and number of empty husks, and impurities (varietal mixing) of the paddy must not exceed certain levels. Moreover, according to the majority of farmers surveyed during my fieldwork, the PMB purchasing programs are hamstrung by frequent delays and implementation lags. 7 Because many residents face intense pressures to sell and sell quickly to settle loans taken out for cultivation and to settle debts raised to cover household consumption, they cannot afford to wait. Together these factors compel many farmers who are often already burdened with debt and are risk averse to sell their products to private buyers at rates well below the assured price (usually between Rs. 28 -29 per kilogram). These private buyers purchase paddy of different qualities on a sliding price scale. During the harvest, their huge lorries pepper the landscape, allowing them to purchase wet paddy, in bulk, straight from farmers ' fields at the very moment when supply is highest and price lowest. Throughout the dry zone, stocks of improved paddy varieties are transferred rapidly from farmers ' fields to a handful of large private granaries and mills. Because unmilled paddy can be stored for two years and milled rice for three months, these distributors can either flood the market with stored paddy or hold back stock to manipulate prices. Collectively, these systems have helped perpetuate cycles of indebtedness and stagnation in rural economies, despite increases in rice yields as a result of Green Revolution technologies.

Within these patterns of structural vulnerability, the cultural significance of rice cultivation also continues to tie dry zone households to agrarian livelihoods. Although the settlement scheme of Sri Pura was created as part of a post-independence agrarian development agenda, the dry zone has a long history as a rice-producing region. In fact, this region sustained substantial agricultural economies at different points in Sri Lanka ' s history, including two ancient hydraulic kingdoms: the first spanning more than a millennium (380 BCE -993 CE) and the second, 300 years (993 -1293 CE). According to historians, these kingdoms were not only regional centers of rice production but also instrumental in preserving the Theravada Buddhist tradition in South Asia, particularly during the period of Hindu revival in India in the fourth and fifth centuries (Peebles 2006). The avowed goal of the postindependence resettlement policy was to restore the productive capacity of the dry zone, but most historians agree that this program of ' renewal ' also powerfully resonated with a particular interpretation of Sinhalese Buddhist history and a broader nationalist attempt to revive ' past glories. ' As Moore (1989) argued, ' The leading actors … [saw] themselves as fulfilling an historic mission which involves the recreation of an authentic, traditional village, family-farming and rice-based Sinhalese society ' (188). Unsurprisingly, the identity of the dry zone farmer and settler is profoundly shaped by this ideologically constructed image of the region. More than 98 percent of residents in Sri Pura self-define as Sinhala Buddhist, an artifact of both this long history of racialized state building and the continued ethnic polarization within border settlements during the country ' s thirty-year-long civil war (Sri Pura Divisional Secretariat 2015). 8

Rice is also a staple grain, consumed for at least two, often three, meals per day in this area. Indeed, 75 percent of my survey respondents in Sri Pura kept a share of their harvest for household consumption, and in this way paddy cultivation continues to shape both the region ' s identity and local subsistence practices. It also helps reduce the cost of food in a context where residents face declining purchasing power for household consumption. It is within this particular context that Vasa Visa Nethi Ratak ' s schemes to transform dry zone rice cultivation into a healthy, indigenous, and organic sector unfold. In the remainder of this article, I integrate semistructured interviews, household surveys, and key ethnographic vignettes to flesh out the scope and meaning of agrochemical kinship.

## Analysis

As the preceding overview makes clear, the history of this region has created strong path dependencies that tie residents to rice cultivation -and agrochemicals -in complex ways. In this section I discuss how experiences of agrarian toxicity and attempts to mitigate it are informed by (1) legacies of loss, (2) simple reproductive squeezes, (3) grain fungibility, and (4) bittersweet attachments to rice farming identities. Together these mechanisms speak to how agrochemicals enable forms of sociality that are necessary for daily and cultural reproduction even as they proliferate toxic pollution in dry zone soils and bodies.

## Vasa Visa Nethi Ratak and Legacies of Loss

Although it is predated by various other initiatives that ban agrichemicals, Vasa Visa Nethi Ratak departs from these actors by explicitly positioning itself as the long-term solution to the problem of kidney disease. In fact, institutionally, it is not housed under the Ministry of Agriculture but instead it falls under the Presidential Taskforce for Kidney Disease Prevention. Its pilot sites, Sri Pura among them, are predominately areas of CKDu endemism.

Yet, despite these good intentions, my household survey data reveal that attempts to repatriate native rice varieties unfolded on the ground in highly paradoxical ways. In fact, according to my survey of 136 farming households in Sri Pura, more than 80 percent of farmers who had experimented with deshiya or organic cultivation reverted back to cultivating with agrochemicals and improved rice varieties. One of my interlocutors, Ajith, a rice farmer who has been cultivating for more than twenty years, shed some light on how and why this situation came about. He observed:

Initially the intention [of the movement] was to encourage people in this area to grow some indigenous or organic rice for their domestic consumption, particularly in the context of all the health problems that we face here. But there has since been a distortion of that goal. … Last season they came to the area and distributed a lot of seed paddy. They promised us that they would buy our harvest for a price premium. So a lot of us took it up. But after the harvest there was no one to buy the produce. … I still have gunnysacks of deshi paddy in my home that we cannot sell.

Vasa Visa Nethi Ratak had planned to directly connect farmers to urban consumers, but at the time of writing, this market network had yet to materialize. Instead, only 6 percent of farmers in my survey sample were able to sell directly to consumers in metropolitan centers. In practice, farmers must rely on highly personalized and often ephemeral networks, which are spatially concentrated in government supply chains (co-ops), niche metropolitan markets, health care infrastructure (i.e., stores in both Western and ayurvedic hospitals), temples, or ad hoc nongovernmental organization projects.

As a result, many residents who took up organic and indigenous rice cultivation recounted bitter legacies and experiences of loss. Take, for instance, the testimony of Priyanka, a farmer leader who worked closely with the movement and, as a result, unwittingly became enrolled as an intermediary in these deeply ephemeral markets:

Last maha season, the yield of our crop was only purchased seven months after the harvest and only for 50 rupees per/kg [which was barely enough to break even]. So, for those seven months farmers were waiting anxiously. They couldn ' t even afford to pay the laborers that they hired [to help with the harvest. Many of these laborers are members of their community who they have to see everyday]. … We received ten to fifteen calls a day from farmers anxious to sell their harvest, begging us to help them sell their paddy. … It was impossible to stay at home during that time, because of the calls.

Here, Priyanka called attention to the pressures that farmers in Sri Pura face to recover their debts as well as the long temporal delays they experienced in securing markets for deshiya paddy. As many of my interlocutors explained, these factors severely eroded household resilience for many families and shaped their decision to switch back to improved varieties and chemical inputs in the next season.

More broadly, to grasp why Vasa Visa Nethi Ratak ' s programs intensified residents ' experiences of debt and desperation, three key dynamics must be understood: First, through my conversations with residents across Sri Pura, I learned that Vasa Visa Nethi Ratak was only able to temporarily increase the acreage under deshiya rice varieties (to 1,000 acres at its peak) by distributing free deshiya rice seedlings and soliciting cultivation. Second, as many of my interlocutors were quick to point out, because deshiya rice varieties typically produce lower yields -on average the harvest of indigenous rice varieties is one third to half less than that of improved ones -they must be sold at a price premium to generate enough income for households to break even. As the preceding testimonies indicate, the acreage under deshiya varieties expanded so rapidly that it outstripped the development of a market capable of absorbing this grain. Indeed, as my data reveal, those willing to pay a price premium for indigenous rice are far and few between and are limited mostly to niche markets. Finally, the surge in supply of traditional varieties outstripped demand for them, which significantly eroded the price premium of deshiya products. This made it highly unprofitable to cultivate deshiya rice varieties given the difference in yield. As a consequence, farmers had to bear the cost of the movement ' s inflated rhetoric and overstated promises.

It would be easy, then, to account for rapid abandonment of indigenous and organic agriculture in this context as an outcome of dwindling profit margins. As the preceding testimonies indicate, residents expressed significant concern about their livelihoods in justifying their return to agrochemicals. Less obvious, however, is how these legacies of loss produced far-reaching consequences for rural social reproduction. Indeed, reducing residents ' struggles to simple trade-offs between health and economy would miss important dimensions of how this loss was experienced quite forcefully as a squeezing or tightening of the conditions necessary for maintaining life on a daily basis and intergenerationally, as well as how it was refracted by strong but bittersweet attachments to dry zone agrarian systems and rice farming identities. Put differently, it would miss the role that agrochemicals play in enabling forms of social reproduction, while undoubtedly, proliferating toxic risks in the long run. It is to a discussion of these very dynamics that I now turn.

## The Simple Reproductive Squeeze

I first met Albert and Mala in 2013. Like many second-generation settlers in Sri Pura, they encroached government land to build a homestead. As such, they do not own any ' colony ' or paddy plots, and they have no deeds to their names at all. Instead, when they cultivate, they do so on lands leased from Albert ' s parents and they pay a quarter of their paddy harvest as rent. Albert and Mala ' s main source of livelihood is from sharecropping, but they also grow some coconut and pepper for local vendors in their home garden. In addition, once a week Mala sells baked goods at Sri Pura ' s open market. The couple have three children, and although the older two children are married, they continue to live on their land. They are stuck in place because of the limited off-farm employment opportunities for young families in this area. Albert and Mala ' s youngest son has left the region to work in the hotel industry in the island ' s capital and occasionally sends money home.

Three months before our first meeting, Albert had been referred from the main district hospital in Anuradhapura to the nearest kidney disease clinic. In 2015, he began receiving a Rs. 3,000 per month (US$16) kidney disease stipend from the government, which helps cover health-related and livelihood expenses, although the money does not stretch far. ' The income we get from this land is barely sufficient to cover the expenses of the two of us. It is not enough to maintain the whole family, ' he told to me in 2015. To make matters worse, during my last visit to his home in 2017, the family had not been able to cultivate during the last season because of drought. Instead, they were scraping by on poverty welfare benefits, Albert ' s disease stipend, and credit with local stores. As I sat with Albert under the shade of his garden, we recollected our first meeting, almost four years earlier: Albert had been keen for me to admire his pepper vines. On the way to the garden, I had seen the skeleton of a kitchen being built in their backyard. Mala had told me earlier in the day with a mix of pride and gratitude that her youngest son sent money home for them to build the new kitchen. Bright red clay bricks lay in piles waiting to be set in place. Fast forward four years, and I find many of these bricks are missing, probably repurposed for more urgent and immediate needs. The skeleton of the kitchen remains, as do Mala ' s aspirations for its construction. ' Here, ' she tells me again, ' is where the hearth will be. '

Mala and Albert ' s narrative of endurance and loss highlights dry zone residents ' vulnerability to the simple reproduction squeeze. First elaborated by Bernstein (1981), the concept of simple reproductive squeezes has been used widely by critical agrarian scholars to understand how the dispossessory and exploitative effects of agrarian capitalism are experienced within the realm of social reproduction. Wilson (2010) elaborated on this definition, arguing that the simple reproduction squeeze is not … simply a price-cost problem experienced by a capitalist farm through a drop in revenue or profits. Rather the squeeze is also caused by declining productivity, market uncertainty, and rising household costs and it is experienced by peasant farmers in the realm of social reproduction -of sustaining the household -that of feeding, clothes, schooling and healing of the family. (77 -78)

In this context, many of my interlocutors recognize the role that agrochemicals play in loosening reproductive squeezes and guaranteeing household reproduction in the short term, even as they pose challenges for the economic and ecological viability of rice cultivation -as well as human health -over the long term.

For example, residents who derive their income solely from cultivation managed cultivation challenges such as the drops in productivity or price produced through deshiya and organic farming by either cutting personal consumption to subsidize the costs of paddy cultivation or taking out loans to cover household consumption in the short term. Both measures temporally disperse reproductive crises but cannot resolve them. As Albert explained:

People often ask why we can ' t earn enough income to support ourselves. But … the effects [of these losses] carry onto the next season even after the rains come or after we return to agrochemicals use, because there is a lack of seed, a lack of money to plough the fields and so we cannot get out of this poverty. … We always have to borrow money again not only to cultivate, but to eat. And because of this we are afraid to change our practices again.

Albert ' s remarks draw attention to the strong unity between reproductive and productive activities for many households in Sri Pura. More poignantly, however, this testimony epitomizes how cultivation losses produced by the uptake and adoption of deshiya seed or organic inputs were acutely experienced as dispossessions in the realm of social reproduction. That is, for many residents, toxic-free cultivation tightened the conditions necessary for maintaining life on a daily basis by eroding their ability to cover expenses related to foodstuffs, household goods, school fees, and health services. In this context, the large-scale return to agrochemicals in the dry zone signals how daily life -and struggles for a life worth living -is enabled through complex patterns of chemical kinship. As one resident poignantly explained:

Today, people are willing to sacrifice both kidneys because they can ' t actually maintain their families. … Because of their economic hardship they have to get at least 350 to 400 bushels from their fields [this calculation is based on a three-acre land allocation]. This means they have to spray. Otherwise, they have no other way to live.

## Grain Fungibility and Pre-existing Modes of Social Reproduction

Residents ' accounts also signal the critical roles that improved rice varieties and agrochemicals play in facilitating household resilience during periods of seasonal drought. Here the ability to derive plural forms of value from rice grains is an essential source of stability for farm households. In this region, paddy is simultaneously a means of subsistence, a means of production, and a commodity, and it shifts between these states constantly in day-to-day life. At stake in cultivation is a secure household food supply, an important source of livelihood, and the means for future cultivation. Consider the following testimony of Jayantha, a second-generation rice farmer in Sri Pura who cultivates his parents ' plots:

Farmers in this area depend heavily on the income generated from paddy cultivation. So, whenever a farmer has need of money, to buy home supplies or cover their child ' s educational expenses, they need to be able to sell part of their stock quickly in order to generate a cash income. In Sri Lanka, you can do this with [improved varieties of] paddy. But you can ' t do this if you grow any other crop. If you have a stock of [improved] rice at home, all you need to do is take it to the market, there are plenty of buyers. This is how people sustain their household budgets and manage periods of high expenses. But if you grow pumpkins you can ' t stock and sell them whenever you need to. If you grow pumpkins on that scale, there is very little you can do with your harvest. Perhaps in the end you can use them to play ball on the road. But that ' s about it. … I mean you can ' t eat them all either. For one day maybe you can consume half a pumpkin at most?

Through this comparison, Jayantha hints at one of the biggest problems with deshiya rice cultivation: Residents cannot sell it when they need to cover household expenses.

Another one of my interlocutors, Kamala, explained her family ' s predicament like this:

Say I have to buy some additional groceries for my family and need to exchange my [ deshiya ] rice for money, there is no guarantee I will be able to. I mean sometimes there are buyers that come at harvest time, but after that it can be difficult to sell it.

Together these testimonies offer insight into how toxic-free cultivation forecloses what I call the fungibility of paddy in the dry zone. Fungibility refers to a grain ' s ability to move back and forth between states as food, seed, and commodity (see Ghertner [2020] for a similar discussion of land fungibility). This property forms an important source of household resilience for many dry zone residents, who conceptualize stores of paddy as savings in the bank, precisely because they can be exchanged for cash at any time.

In conventional paddy farming, the fungibility of rice is enabled by the large and stable markets for improved rice varieties that can absorb any amount of grain at any time. In contrast, the buyers of organic and deshiya rice varieties are ephemeral, spatially concentrated in niche metropolitan markets, and dominated by different branches of the state, local, and international nongovernmental organizations, and various ad hoc projects, the majority of which mediate deshiya rice markets as part of philanthropic intervention. As argued earlier, a widely recognized consequence of limited access to markets was that farmers who cultivated ' toxic-free ' deshiya rice were stuck with stores of paddy that they ultimately could not sell.

Less understood, however, is how toxic-free rice cultivation altered people ' s relationships to grain fungibility and, in turn, impaired key modes of social reproduction in the context of drought. In fact, experimentation with deshiya rice cultivation was followed by the worst regional drought in forty years (Mosbergen 2017; Perera 2018; Sandeepika 2018). Because of this context, the erosion of grain fungibility in the wake of toxic-free cultivation destabilized household economies across the dry zone. Unsurprisingly then, the return en masse to agrochemical use across rice fields in Sri Pura helped resurrect a crucial form of chemical kinship that underpins forms of everyday agrarian resilience even as it proliferates toxic risks in day-to-day life. Residents ' negotiation of toxicity is thus profoundly shaped by the polyvalent values of rice and the fragmentation of pre-existing modes of social reproduction. As I describe next, it is also shaped by their deep attachments to an identity that is rooted in rice cultivation.

## Agrochemicals and Cultural Reproduction

When I spoke with residents about their complex relationships to agrochemicals, they often evoked strong but bittersweet attachments to dry zone rice farming landscapes and identities. As alluded to earlier, in the dry zone, local identities are profoundly shaped by ideologically constructed narratives of the region as the rice belt, the frontier, and the heartland of Sinhala Buddhist civilization. More poignantly, residents ' testimonies epitomize one of the key paradoxes of rice cultivation in the dry zone. Although my interviews about loss -in terms of both yield and income -were often infused with nostalgia about this long history of racialized state building, residents are also keenly aware of how conventional systems of agriculture produce social and chemical dangers.

As many of the farmers whom I worked with observed, it is very difficult for them to generate an economically viable livelihood through farming (either deshiya or high-yielding seed) without chemical inputs or incurring debts. Alex, a male farmer from Sri Pura, highlighted how the onset of drought brings this dilemma into sharp relief:

In this area, we are not used to buying rice from the store. But with these losses and now with the absence of rain, people have to get used to it and we find that very difficult. We are used to consuming rice from our own harvests, we take pride in eating what we ourselves have nurtured and grown. … In this country, there are common sayings: that the ' farmer deserves to be a king, ' that he is the ' true Bhoomiputra of the nation '-[the sons of the land and frontiersmen in the conquest of the dry zone in the nation ' s war against hunger] … but the reality is that farmers cannot even cultivate the next season without going into debt.

This testimony illustrates how the losses of yield and income -due to deshiya cultivation, drought, or disease -translated directly into a loss of autonomy over local foodways, which in turn amounted to a loss of cultural identity and significance in a broader national context.

It is precisely this identity-building capacity of rice cultivation at multiple scales that helps explain why residents intensified their dependence on agrochemicals in the wake of CKDu. At the heart of Alex ' s testimony is a recognition that stagnating agrarian economies together with the failure of deshiya cultivation push residents to social and political margins:

We are given less inputs and when we try to sell our harvest, we have to sell our outputs for very low prices, if we can sell them at all. We ' re squeezed from both sides. This is the economic situation of most farmers … there is no place, no status, no recognition of the farmer in this country today.

As a consequence, the large-scale return to agrochemical inputs in the wake of CKDu provides a window onto broader issues affecting dry zone farming communities that include the precarity of both their way of life and cultural identity. Specifically, residents ' testimonies signal the myriad ways in which agrochemicals continue to underwrite the cultural significance of rice farming because of their role in generating national food security. Indeed, as historians of the dry zone are quick to point out, although improved varieties of seed were developed on the island as early as the 1940s, the large-scale uptake of high-yielding rice varieties and chemical fertilizers only really gained momentum in 1967 as part of the ' Food Drive Programme. ' 9

This history was repeatedly evoked by residents when I asked them why they returned to agrochemical use. Consider the following testimony of Sena, a second-generation rice farmer, who echoed many of the themes in Alex ' s earlier observations.

So the way I see it, rice farmers deserve more recognition because they are the primary contributors to food production in this country. There is no respect, no value for the profession. But if we don ' t cultivate rice, then how will you eat? The government would ultimately have to import grain from foreign countries because the whole nation cannot be weaned from its ages-old taste for rice.

Central to Sena ' s account is the way that dry zone residents are situated within networks of agrochemical kinship that traverse territorial scales and are deeply implicated in reproducing national foodways. At stake in residents ' knotted relationships with agrochemicals are narratives of hope about the longevity and cultural significance of rice farming identities as well as painful experiences of loss and marginalization, as the status of farmers in dry zone Sri Lanka become increasingly precarious. In all cases, residents ' accounts were filled with nostalgia about their sense of self, community, and purpose within a broader nationalist imaginary. Their testimonies index fraught relationships of social reproduction to agrochemicals: On the one hand, agrochemical inputs reinforce multiscalar attachments to identity and livelihood that enable residents to project their hopes for a dignified life into the future. On the other hand, circuits of agrarian toxicity erode ecological and bodily health in the long term and, by doing so, destabilize the very processes of social reproduction on which life in the dry zone depends.

## Discussion and Conclusion: Respatializing Health and Pathology

The testimonies recounted here signal how dry zone residents embody a much broader spatialization of toxicity and kinship than what is encoded in the Toxic Free Nation Movement ' s programs. Although these largely vertical, top-down campaigns against kidney disease are important and valuable, they attempt to spatially contain pollution in discrete and bounded environments, such as in a bag of fertilizer or a bottle of pesticide. As this article demonstrates, though, residents ' complex struggles for health are more often than not linked to broader concerns about the reproduction of place, self, and dignity, particularly under precarious socioeconomic conditions.

Through their words and practices, residents respatialize toxicity beyond these discrete environments and call attention to how agrochemicals simultaneously erode and sustain life. On the one hand, chemical relations emerge in residents ' testimonies as signifiers of a larger, pathological system of cultivation. In articulating these critiques, residents readily acknowledge that agrochemical intensification not only reproduces the precarity of dry zone agrarian livelihoods in the long run but also routinely exposes community members to the attritional violence of long-term, low-dose toxic harms. Yet, at the same time, this text took pains to demonstrate that the shift to ' toxic-free ' agriculture generated cultivation deficits that were acutely experienced as dispossessions in the realm of social reproduction and refracted by strong but bittersweet attachments to rice farming landscapes and identities. In the dry zone, these in situ displacements were made possible through the strong unity of reproductive and productive spheres of labor as well as the ideological role that rice farming plays in a broader nationalist imaginary. Put differently, in the context of simple reproduction squeezes, dependencies on the fungibility of rice, and strong attachments to dry zone rice farming identities, this article demonstrated how agrochemical inputs enable moments of kinship, stability, and connection for many residents.

These findings create space for different kinds of theory and intervention. In particular, by bringing work on chemical kinship and social reproduction to bear on the experience of living within contaminated spaces, the article sheds new light on the fraught and complex encounters with toxicity in rural settings. Specifically, the complex patterns of agrochemical kinship unfolding in the dry zone resonate with recent work by geographers on how toxic harms can simultaneously erode and sustain forms of life as well as how ' life forged in ongoing chemical violence is also life open to becoming something else ' (Murphy 2017a, 500). This article extends this work by investigating how knotted patterns of chemical dependence in rural agrarian settings are profoundly shaped by struggles for social reproduction at multiple temporal and social scales. So, at the same time as this article critiques toxic infrastructures and structural violence, focusing on chemical kinship and social reproduction reveals that residents ' relationships to agrochemicals cannot be articulated solely through the lens of pathology.

The problem with discussing agrochemicals as purely ' villainous objects with violent effects ' is that it often leads us to manage harm via ' separation, containment, clean up ' (Balayannis and Garnett 2020; Lee 2020). As the stories recounted here demonstrate, though, these ' solutions ' inadvertently create new forms of harm because they obscure the myriad ways that agrochemicals also enable household and cultural resilience in the context of rural stagnation and decline. As Roberts (2017) concluded, such ' linear thinking shuts down the possibility of tracing how chemicals might be engaged in looping relations with bodies situated in their specific worlds ' (614, italics added).

More broadly, narrow, production-centered approaches to agrarian toxicity can entrench forms of epistemic violence -violence exerted against or through knowledge -that marginalize the accounting of harm that is most salient for residents themselves. If agrochemical dependence is only understood through the lenses of avoidance or pathology, residents who returned en masse to agrochemical use are stripped of the ability to explain their patterns of dependence as ones that are tied to larger struggles for social reproduction. It is imperative, therefore, to connect an analysis of the more generalized structures that truncate and devalue life itself in these spaces, such as mounting levels of debt, the waning cultural significance of farming, and rural stagnation, to moments of agrochemical kinship. These moments of kinship are important because they offer sites for rethinking our relationships to agrochemicals without abstracting them from larger systems of violence.

As a consequence, my work demonstrates that residents ' knotted relationships with agrochemicals have profound consequences for how we conceptualize health improvement schemes in such contexts. Indeed, residents ' testimonies direct attention to how the Toxic Free Nation Movement ' resonate[s] with public health campaigns worldwide that call on people, as individuals, to remove themselves from forms of sociality necessary to their very survival ' (Roberts 2017, 612). In doing so, my interlocutors ' commentaries dash hopes of any simple equation of banning agrochemical inputs and enacting health in the wake of CKDu. Instead, they point to the need for interventions that grapple with the multiplicity, complexity, and contradictions of relationships between health and agrochemicals. Ultimately, until the lens of policy intervention is widened to encompass more provincialized, historically contingent, and geographically situated accounts of health -environment relationships, attempts to ' cultivate health ' in the wake of CKDu will continue to produce paradoxical effects, new processes of differentiation, and new sources of harm, as has largely been the case so far.
