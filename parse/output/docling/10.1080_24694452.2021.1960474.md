## Un/Making Assets: The Institutional Limits to Financialization

Kelly Kay  and Renee Tapp †

Department of Geography, University of California, Los Angeles, USA † Department of Urban Planning and Policy, University of Illinois at Chicago, USA

This article contributes to contemporary debates within the financialization and assetization literatures by examining the sale of tax credits in U.S. property markets over the last twenty years. Whereas the vast majority of research on financial assets focuses on the income-generating aspects of property, this article considers a more holistic approach to assets. Through detailed empirical analysis of syndication in historic tax credit and conservation easement markets, we demonstrate how assets are not just sources of income or cash flow but also -critically -sources of expense reduction. Drawing from relevant U.S. tax law and related legal provisions, we trace three interlocking components in the assetization process: enabling and disabling legislation, the structures and typologies of property ownership, and the actors that make deals happen. By starting with asset creation, rather than assumed geographies of investment, we bridge the rural -urban divide to reveal new geographies of finance sourced directly from the state. Although the tax sheltering dimensions of assets are unique in this regard, the state retains the capacity to unmake assets that lack ' economic substance. ' As such, this article offers a new direction forward -a legal-institutionalist framework -that foregrounds the state as setting and enforcing the limits to financialization. Key Words: economic substance, finance, fiscal geographies, syndication, tax.

P roperty -both urban and rural -continues to be one of the most important sources of wealth generation well into the twenty-first century (Piketty 2014). Amassing profits from land and buildings depends not only on the reach of investors and changing modes of production but also on a constant stream of assets and new sources of revenue. The ability for property-based asset ownership to reshape the economy has largely been enabled by institutional changes that redefine the financial qualities of assets and determine who is an eligible asset holder (Adkins, Cooper, and Konings 2020). Over the last three decades in the United States, the tax system has lowered the individual barriers to land ownership and more efficiently distributed the array of financial benefits of a single property to a wide group of investors through syndications. Crowdsourcing property ownership with syndications has also increasingly become the channel whereby investors tap into a new financial wellspring (the state) and associated assets (tax benefits). These practices appear in tandem across rural and urban geographies, as syndication has gone mainstream for both large and small investors.

Whereas profits sourced from financial, rather than productive, channels characterize the financialized economy (Krippner 2005), scholars increasingly argue that ' the commodity is an anachronistic lens through which to understand the character of present-day economic restructuring ' (Adkins, Cooper, and Konings 2020, 15). In its place, critical human geographers and social scientists have mobilized ' assetization ' to understand the transformation of property into financial assets (Ward and Swyngedouw 2018; Aalbers 2020; Langley 2021). Recent research in this vein focuses on the incomegenerating and price-appreciating features of property (Leyshon and Thrift 2007; Muniesa et al. 2017; Fields 2018; Birch and Muniesa 2020). Although cash flows are indeed important, economic geographers working on tax demonstrate that the expensereducing qualities of assets can matter more than income yields to investors who are not interested in owning and managing property but still want to lock in the financial benefits of land and buildings (Kay 2016; Tapp 2020). Scholarship in this register emphasizes the fiscal and legal mechanisms of finance (Knuth and Potts 2016; Potts 2020) and centers tax law as the core state institution responsible for emitting expense-reducing assets (Tapp and Kay 2019). Despite growing evidence of the state ' s capacity to produce investable assets from property (Ouma, Johnson, and Bigger 2018; Ouma 2020), what seems to be missing are more direct questions as to where, why, and how the state restricts assetization, particularly -as in the case of syndication -when the state itself is the source of the asset. In overlooking the state ' s ability to redraw the boundaries of assets, critical human geographers miss an important opportunity to investigate how the state might restrict or limit financialization.

This article develops a theory of assetization that brings into focus expense reduction (tax benefits) as a critical dimension of financial assets and examines the state ' s role in not only enabling and participating in financialization but also in setting and enforcing the limits to asset creation. To do this, we examine a parallel process in rural and urban geographies -syndication -through empirical case studies. By situating the making and unmaking of assets in two markets alongside each other, this article deploys a ' cross-case comparison ' that is ' essential ' for building a comprehensive understanding of financialization (Ouma, Johnson, and Bigger 2018, 507; see also Christophers 2015). Although notable recent work on financial assets has conceptually spanned the rural -urban binary (see Fairbairn 2014; Fields 2018; Ouma 2020), few studies have conducted comparative research across both geographies. Our analysis begins by spatializing tax and financial data -rather than assuming pregiven geographies -to show how the process of syndication produces similar accumulation outcomes (expense reduction) in both urban and rural locales. We argue that if ' law is the cloth from which capital is cut ' (Pistor 2019, 5) and syndication is a quick ticket to asset ownership, then the state ultimately plays an outsized role in charting the new geographies of finance.

The article proceeds as follows: First, we develop a conceptual framework that theorizes the financialization of real property as a process that is fundamentally animated by the law, embedded in state practices, and manifest in distinct geographies. This ' legal-institutionalist ' approach places the emerging body of literature on assetization into conversation with fiscal and legal geographies to broaden the conception of an asset beyond something that simply generates a future-oriented income stream. Next, we give a brief overview of syndication before describing methods of data collection and analysis. The article then turns to our examination of real property syndication, focusing on the cases of historic tax credits and conservation easements to show how deals are legally structured and how site-specific actors come together to create markets in often unexpected places. Although our research draws from examples in the United States -a legal-institutionalist approach requires place-specific attention to national and subnational regulations and institutions -many of our findings are likely to resonate with other empirical cases as well. After establishing the process by which these assets are made, the next section considers the economic substance doctrine as the state ' s primary mechanism to ' unmake ' syndicated assets. Finally, we conclude by exploring four broader implications a legal-institutionalist framework has for financialization scholarship in human geography.

## A Legal-Institutionalist Framework for Constructing Assets

We propose a legal-institutionalist perspective on assetization that begins by recognizing that financial markets and their component parts -assets -are first and foremost ' constructs of law ' (Pistor 2013). This type of approach focuses on the ' institutional conditions and legal mechanisms that transform real estate into a liquid asset ' (Gotham 2006, 233). It understands institutions to include ' property rights, governance structures, conceptions of control, and rules of exchange ' that structure economies, politics, and society writ large (Fligstein 1996, 657) and capital as the outcome of ' two ingredients: an asset and the legal code ' (Pistor 2019, 2). Particular institutions of law (i.e., contract, corporate, and tax law) coupled with legal forms of ownership emit assets with critical attributes that create wealth. In the United States, property is coded into financial assets from the built and physical environment with ' an amalgam of recording regulations, banking laws, zoning laws, subdivision regulations, private deed restrictions, land-use regulations, building codes, insurance laws, and property tax law ' (Gotham 2006, 236). Because ' law is a matter of state power ' (Deakin et al. 2017, 189), the assetization of property cannot exist without a state to back and enforce a legal system (Pistor 2019; Potts 2020). It is in this sense that assetization can be understood to be a legally constructed, ' essentially hybrid ' place between the state and market and the public and private (Pistor 2013; see also Bryant and Spies-Butcher 2020).

Tax law is a core legal institution where investors minimize expenses and maximize gains (Tapp and Kay 2019). The most significant asset targeted by these practices is property, where alterations to the tax code bestow financially beneficial attributes onto land and buildings that extend beyond income generation (Gunnoe 2014; Kay 2017; Wijburg 2019; Knuth 2020; Tapp 2020). For example, asset depreciation on buildings is a noncash expense that reduces taxable income and, in practice, functions as an economic subsidy for new buildings. Likewise, although land cannot be depreciated (Fairbairn 2014), the heavy debt service used to finance land and buildings can be written off to amplify returns to investors (Appelbaum and Batt 2014). Furthermore, tax credits and deductions offer property owners incentives that can be monetized and circulated on secondary markets (Kay 2016; Tapp 2019). The abundance of tax benefits has made investors quick to classify assets as anything that ' can be thought of as something that, in the future, can generate cash flow, reduce expenses, or improve sales, regardless of whether it ' s manufacturing equipment or a patent ' (Barrone 2020, 1, italics added). Such legal nuance helps move property from a spatially fixed investment class to a more liquid, multidimensional, and bundled financial asset where rights unravel into discrete investable streams for the highest bidder.

Monetizing the various dimensions of real property brings many parties to the table and entails significant negotiation over the separation of control and ownership (Lapavitsas 2013; Ashwood et al. 2020). Asset holders are notoriously ' not interested in sharing their spoils ' (Pistor 2019, 206), leading them to selectively use legal vehicles to partition and sequester the various financial attributes of property, while ensuring that they maintain priority positions in the hierarchy of rights. Pass-through entities that allow fractional ownership -limited liability companies (LLCs), limited partnerships, and S corporations 1 -have become especially useful in legally parceling portions of an asset ' s profits among a group of investors (Gunnoe and Gellert 2011; Van Loon and Aalbers 2017; Tapp 2020). Whereas property was once ' unlike other financial assets ' because of its ' indivisibility ' (Coakley 1994, 700), these innovations in the legal system ' rationalized buildings and property into legible and tradeable commodities ' by splitting a single asset into small, tradable units (Revington and August 2020, 858). Fragmented ownership, divisible financial attributes, and transferrable profits are now standard legal features in the assetization of land and buildings.

Considering assets to be legal constructs is a reminder that finance is ' not an ethereal force that imposes its will on the built environment from above ' (Weber 2015, 65) -it ' lands ' where it is directed. The process of selling partnership interests, shares, and securities in a specific building or piece of land is embedded in a ' web of legal relations ' where brokers, lawyers, and accountants manage ' knowledge about the law and how to use the modules of the code to create private wealth ' (Pistor 2019, 165). Finance clusters where intermediaries congregate and spreads outward from there (Pike and Pollard 2009; Hall and Leyshon 2013). Property is often transacted along personal lines that connect local institutions and professional networks (Ashton, Doussard, and Weber 2016; Guironnet, Attuyer, and Halbert 2016; Ouma 2016; Sanfelici and Halbert 2019). Without these networks, actors ' cannot come to consensus about the meaning of abstractions, such as risk and value, involved in the exchange of complex assets and instruments. … In order for spatially intensive yields to flow from property to investors, an extensive set of ongoing, locally situated relationships must first exist ' (Weber 2015, 65). Where much of the financialization literature in geography tends to ' ignore the supporting actors ' (Weber 2015, 31), focusing on the collective behavior of legal and financial intermediaries allows us to better understand asset creation.

We draw on these interlocking components in the construction of assets -enabling and disabling legislation, the structures and typologies of property ownership, and the actors that make deals happen -to illustrate the centrality of the state in financial markets for property. In the sections that follow, we analyze the ways in which syndication turns land and buildings into financial assets and trace the power the state possesses to curtail markets and rein in financialization. First, though, we describe the mechanics of syndication to frame the parallel phenomenon in rural and urban geographies.

## A Brief History of Real Estate Syndication

Syndication is a form of group asset ownership. Investors pool their capital to buy or build real property and divide the financial benefits of land ownership -cash flows, tax benefits, and appreciation -among themselves. Despite being considered ' one of the most complex areas of federal tax law ' (Brueggman and Fisher 2016, 604), syndication ' s primary appeal to property developers is its ' unique way to raise capital at low cost ' (Downs 1985, 94). Maximizing equity investment lowers the debt needed to finance a project and is especially beneficial to properties that do not generate conventional cash flows. For investors, the draw of syndication is as an expense-reducing asset where ' the syndicator customarily seeks to maximize not only the investor ' s yield but also the nontaxability of his investment, to furnish ' tax shelter ' to the investor ' (Berger 1960, 745). Markets for syndications increase sources of real estate capital and broaden the ownership base in property by selling tax shelters.

Syndicators -also called promoters or sponsors -are responsible for sourcing the capital to finance a deal. On their own, syndicators lack the funds to cover a property ' s acquisition. Instead, they appeal to personal relationships -family, friends, colleagues -or they advertise in newspapers and magazines, crowdfunding platforms, and invitation-only events to sell interests in real property (Langley and Leyshon 2017; Saez and Zucman 2019; see Figure 3 for an example). The former -public solicitation -transforms simple partnership interests into bona fide securities, placing them under the regulatory scrutiny of the U.S. Securities Act of 1933 and the oversight of the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). Buying securities through syndication is a common real estate practice and was pioneered by Lawrence Wien in the 1930s. Wien famously helped real estate mogul Harry Helmsley build his literal and metaphorical empire with syndication: Helmsley acquired the Empire State Building by forming a syndicate that sold 3,300 ownership shares for $10,000 each (Maurer 2017). Syndicators help extend the reach of finance into local property markets (Gotham 2006), making it possible for amateur and experienced investors alike to acquire cash flows and tax benefits from property they have never seen.

Modern forms of property ownership have opened syndication up to smaller investors. If historically syndications were formed as corporations and real estate investment trusts for ' the very rich, who deal themselves enormous tax breaks ' (Benzer 1972), then today limited partnerships, LLCs, and joint ventures are the ' preferred vehicle(s) for syndication ' for a more diverse set of investors (Selig 2017, 404). Contemporary forms of property ownership shield investors from the double taxation corporations face and from personal liability if the deal sours, at the same time increasing liquidity in an otherwise illiquid asset. They also cloak the deal in anonymity: transactions bought and sold by LLCs mask investors ' identities and camouflage the allocation and distribution of income, risk, and profit among the entity ' s members (Hamill 1998; Loewenstein 2012). Moreover, structuring real property transactions with noncorporate legal entities like LLCs makes it possible to splinter off exact benefit streams to each investor in a syndicated group. Syndications, as legal creations, take different forms depending on the prevailing federal tax code and how far syndicators are willing to bend the law for investors.

We consider two distinct examples where syndication is used to purchase properties and provide investors with the asset of expense reduction. These examples highlight the public -private nature of finance (Pistor 2013), as private capital is put forward to purchase specific properties that will be used to generate tax credits and deductions from public sources. After briefly discussing our methods of data collection and analysis, the case studies examine (1) the legal forms and entities syndication takes, (2) the actors involved in the transactions, and (3) the geographies syndication produces. We follow the cases by exploring (4) the specific government regulations and case law that enables and disables important features of these markets.

## Research Methods

This article bridges two long-term research projects on historic tax credit and conservation easement markets to analyze the institutional origins and limits to the construction and circulation of financial assets. Empirical findings in this article draw on qualitative and quantitative data, including semistructured interviews, participant observation at industry conferences and webinars, and two original data sets described herein. Our approach develops what Kass (2020) called ' critical financial analysis, ' where ' fluency in public finance can enable geographers to better understand the discourse around fiscal issues and illuminate the politics around seemingly technical and (sometimes) benign changes in financial norms and practices ' (108). The methodology we adopt offers but one way to skirt the challenges of generating knowledge about financial transactions (see Ashwood et al. [2020] for another good example) and serves to spatialize the balance sheet, pointing to new geographies of assets beyond the rural -urban divide.

Data for the historic tax credit market come from (1) a quantitative database obtained through a Freedom of Information Act Request from the National Park Service of all federal historic tax credit projects in the United States from 2001 to 2018 ( n ¼ 13,598); (2) forty-three semistructured in-person interviews conducted with investors, developers, accountants, lawyers, preservation consultants, and regulators between September 2016 and November 2017; and (3) 105 hours of participant observation at national historic tax credit industry conferences, workshops, and webinars held in June 2016, September 2016, August 2017, and April 2020. The quantitative data set included the building name, location, historic district (if any), estimated qualified rehabilitation expenses, final qualified rehabilitation expenses (i.e., the final amount the tax credit was derived from), and the year the tax credit was awarded. Notably, it does not include the name of the property owner or developer or the names of the investors. This information is not collected by the Technical Preservation Services, which oversee the credit and is not publicly accessible as per the U.S. Internal Revenue Service ' s (IRS) mandate. Semistructured interviews with self-identified active participants in the tax credit industry provided insights into this opaque market. Discussions focused on how tax credit deals were legally structured and syndicated market responses to government reregulation and investor demand for deals. Conferences, workshops, and webinars provided information on how to navigate the changing regulatory landscape, identify investment partners, and structure syndications, as well as technical details including how to finance tax credit deals, estimate returns, and appraise the values of historic buildings. Quantitative data, semistructured interviews, and participant observation were supplemented by secondary materials including case law around historic tax credit syndication, industry reports, and policy documents.

Empirical data on conservation easement syndication draws from (1) more than fifty semistructured interviews conducted between 2014 and 2019 with tax professionals, appraisers, attorneys, journalists, landowners, land trusts, conservation easement tax credit brokers, state and federal employees, and investment managers; (2) seventy-five hours of participant observation at industry conferences and webinars; and (3) a novel quantitative data set ( n ¼ 269) developed by the authors. The data set was created by taking publicly available raw data from a 2017 Brookings Institution report (Looney 2017b) on conservation easement syndication and combining it with LLCs listed in ' request for information ' letters sent by the Senate Finance Committee to fourteen conservation easement syndicators suspected of fraudulent overvaluation. This list was then extended to include a second group of LLCs sourced from a Chicago-based law firm investigating conservation easement-related securities fraud. The SEC Form D filings for each of the 269 LLCs were used to determine the location of incorporation and addresses of principal place of business for all LLCs suspected of engaging in abusive syndicated easement deals. Principal place of business data were used to create an ArcGIS shapefile that was analyzed for spatial trends. Secondary data, including a review of relevant case law challenges to conservation easement syndication, policy documents, and industry reports from land trusts, were used to contextualize the unmaking of assets in the conservation easement market.

## Case Studies

We begin our case studies by focusing on the ways in which syndication generates expense-reducing financial assets. Because syndication often operates at the ' edges of the law, ' it requires (1) carefully structured deals and (2) a close-knit community of professionals and investors who understand the projects at hand and trust one another (see Roberts [1995]; Wainwright [2011]; Aalbers [2018] for an international perspective). These relationships, along with tax arbitrage between different subnational jurisdictions, produces (3) a domestic geography of tax shelters, often hidden in unlikely places. We attend to each of these three features in the case studies that follow.

## Historic Tax Credits

Historic tax credits are the primary way to protect and preserve socially and architecturally significant buildings across the United States. They are used to finance the material and labor costs of redeveloping historically preserved structures listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Presently, more than 45,000 buildings have been redeveloped with the credit (Technical Preservation Services 2020). For a historically designated building to be eligible for credits, it must pass the IRS ' s ' substantial rehabilitation test ' and demonstrate that the cost of the redevelopment is greater than the preredevelopment cost of the building (26 CFR 1.48-12). Preservation consultants work with architects and developers to estimate the material and labor costs of redevelopment, which, given the age of the structure and the complexity of adaptive reuse, almost always exceed the building ' s purchase price. Developers use the estimated rehabilitation costs to sell the credit to investors through syndications; the majority of these partnerships are structured as LLCs (Tapp 2020). At its core, it is the ability to show that a building is ' uneconomical ' that gives the credit its value to developers, and it is the use of the credit for offsetting expenses that makes a project profitable for investors.

When historic tax credit syndication emerged in the 1980s, it existed primarily to shelter private wealth for individuals. Between 1981 and 1986, nearly 70 percent of the historic tax credits awarded flowed to wealthy individuals and households, many of whom relied on syndicators to transact investments on their behalf (General Accounting Office 1986). Syndicators specialized in packaging and selling pro rata partnership interests in ' activities with respect to which the individual participant had little or no experience ' (U.S. Department of the Treasury 1999, 19). With ' cookie-cutter deals that were mass-marketed to the taxpaying public ' (U.S. Department of the Treasury 1999, 81), syndicators amassed ample funds that allowed them to ' badger ' developers into redeveloping older buildings in urban areas (Waite 1988). Throughout the 1980s, as tax credit syndication became the ' de facto urban policy ' (Ryberg-Webster 2015), it was obvious that the ' market fundamentals ' were deeply skewed. Households supplied equity markets with their savings (Beitel 2000) and the middlemen -syndicators -drove the demand. Passive loss rules enacted by the Tax Reform Act of 1986 quieted the market by making the majority of individual investors ineligible for historic tax credits. Over the last two decades, the revival of the historic tax credit has come under the guidance of big accounting firms and corporate investors who maneuvered into the market and advocated structurally complex syndications ' designed to completely eliminate all risk ' in these real estate deals (Jacobs 2013, 1).

As a result, pockets of historic redevelopment appear across urban America that have little to do with the actual numbers of historic buildings located in a city. Figure 1 shows the twenty-five cities with the greatest number of historic tax credit projects completed between 2001 and 2018. With the exception of Chicago and New York -both of which rank far down the list -historic tax credits projects cluster in secondary and tertiary cities. The volume of transactions done in places like Missouri and Ohio is partially an outcrop of an increased supply of equity as statewide legislation has replicated the federal financial benefits of historic redevelopment at the state scale (Tapp 2019). Today ' s syndicators repopulate their client base with developers, high-net-worth individuals, and corporations ' with predictable tax liabilities ' (Keightley, Marples, and Sherlock 2019, 3). This includes many banks and financial institutions such as Bank of America, First National Bank of Canada, J.P. Morgan Chase, Old National Bank, and US Bancorp, as well as businesses like the Sherwin-Williams Company (Tapp 2020).

The availability of capital alone does not make a property market. Syndicators make markets by opening closed doors to their clients. Because historic tax credits cannot be bought and sold on an open market (Tapp 2019), these spaces remain inaccessible to investors ' unless you happen to be a client of one of the law or accounting firms or top banks actively engaged in the market ' (Tiller 2019). For a fee, boutique syndication shops, national accounting companies, specialized departments within U.S. banking giants, historic preservation nonprofits, law offices, insurance agents, and financial planners will ' identify, evaluate, and manage tax credit equity investments ' for socially unconnected investors (Keightley, Marples, and Sherlock 2019, 2). Likability and trust form the basis of these relationships and all but guarantee repeated transactions. One developer located in a Midwest city described their relationship with a local banker who would syndicate tax credits:

Even in an era of digital economies, tax credit deals continue to be forged by word-of-mouth referrals between local real estate actors (see Berger 1960 for a historical perspective). However, these insular networks can also act as a bubble, where recent bad practices, like the ' unfortunate turn [to] the ' sale of tax credits, '' test the limits of the law (Jacobs 2013, 1).

## Conservation Easements

Since the 1980s, conservation easements have become a popular means of legitimately protecting culturally and environmentally important rural and exurban land under threat of conversion or development. At present, nearly 28 million acres of land in the United States is under conservation easement (National Conservation Easement Database 2020). Easements are generally used to protect critical habitat, open space, working farms, and ranches by removing the development potential of a property while allowing the underlying land and remaining property rights to stay in the initial owner ' s hands. Land is typically valued using a ' before and after ' method, where a qualified real estate appraiser will value the property under its current use and then estimate its value if it were converted to its ' highest and best use, ' generally extraction or real estate development (Kay 2016). The easement is donated to a nonprofit or other qualified holding agency, and in return the landowner receives the value of the easement (highest and best use minus current value) in tax benefits (Lindstrom 2012).

Historically, most conservation easements were donated by individual landowners who intended to continue to hold the land after the donation was complete. Struggling ranchers, for example, might be tempted to sell their property to an exurban real estate developer but could be incentivized to keep the land in its current use with the tax incentives they would receive by placing an easement on their property. When the U.S. Internal Revenue Code was amended to permanently allow the donation of partial interests in property for tax benefits in 1980, this was the intent (Small 2000). Although most conservation easements still tend to follow this format, a ' cottage sub-industry evolved within the financial services market over the past decade … these securities offerings are premised on a real estate investment transaction that involves the acquisition by an investor group of an interest in environmentally sensitive real estate that is also developable or marketable as commercial real estate ' (Updike and Mick 2015, 293). Over the last decade, syndicated partnerships have claimed more than $20 billion in charitable deductions (Looney 2017a). In 2016 alone, 248 entities claimed $6 billion in tax deductions (Looney 2017a). Overly optimistic, if not explicitly fraudulent, appraisals are largely behind the boom of syndicated conservation easements. Investors now seek out land with the highest gap between current and possible use specifically to yield tax benefits that could be shared by the members of the LLCs formed to purchase these properties. The property acquisition is structured so that the purchase price is hidden from public records, making it possible for appraisals to produce easement values that are many times higher than the purchase price of the entire property (Updike and Mick 2015). Some of the most egregious transactions earn investors an average of $9 in credits for every $1 invested (Looney 2017a).

Syndicated conservation easements -particularly those derived from grossly inflated appraisals -are concentrated in the southeastern United States. Although ' it ' s impossible to identify the precise birthplace of the syndicated conservation easement, it ' s safe to say it became an industry in Georgia ' (Elkind 2017, 77). Between 2010 and 2012, 36 percent of all deductions nationwide for conservation easements were taken by taxpayers in Georgia (Looney 2017a). Yet, in 2020, only 2 percent of the land area in the entire state is under easement, syndicated or otherwise (National Conservation Easement Database 2020), indicating that those deductions were likely taken on relatively few, relatively small properties. Additionally, a comprehensive review of SEC filings for LLCs that are suspected of being formed for the purposes of conservation easement syndication transactions indicates that 66 percent of the LLCs (178 total), including all of those being investigated by the Senate Finance Committee, are registered in Georgia or list it as their primary place of business (see Figure 2). For those that are registered or operating elsewhere, nearly all locate their primary place of business in the southeastern United States, including neighboring Alabama, where 20 percent of the LLCs are registered. Many of the LLCs operating in the Southeast are incorporated in Delaware, where scholars speculate that the state ' s legal climate -a concentration of corporate law experts and a state consensus to ensure corporate law precedent is up to date -means that legal issues are handled in a concise and timely manner for investors and syndicators (Fisch 1999).

Georgia ' s sprawling suburban development (to provide credible development threat), lack of regulatory hurdles around rezoning and extractive property uses, and state-level tax incentives, along with cooperative conservation professionals and the agglomeration of consultants, have made it the epicenter of syndicated conservation easements. The latter have been especially important in selling syndicated deals locally. Investors are located through advertisements such as the one shown in Figure 3 and through word-of-mouth outreach. For example, one accountant working in coastal Georgia targets Southeastern Conference college football coaches -who typically have high W-2 amounts and a dearth of tax- deductible expenses -to buy into syndicated conservation easement deals as a means of erasing their tax liabilities (Lee 2019). By targeting investors with regional ties, syndicators pump their local networks with promises of deductions orders of magnitude larger than the initial investment.

If bringing investors together with deals is the job of syndicators, then crafting hyperinflated returns is the job of an appraiser who willingly overvalues land. One appraiser confirmed during a webinar on abusive syndicated conservation easement transactions that ' appraisers are always complicit in this; you have to have a really aggressive appraisal for this to work ' (personal communication, 3 May 2019). These appraisers operate within a relatively contained geography because their subjective valuations are drawn from local real estate comparisons (Weber 2015). A U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) complaint to stop rampant easement overvaluation was filed in the Atlanta District Court and named a set of parties who comprise a large subset of the local epistemic community required to undertake a syndicated deal ( US v. Zak et al. 2018). Zak, the named defendant, worked as a broker and syndicator, and other named parties in the complaint were licensed appraisers, accountants, and investment managers. The DOJ filing details the complex intertwining of the parties through multiple overlapping LLCs and partnerships, hinting at their close-knit and longstanding working relationships, much of which took place between Rome, Georgia, and Atlanta, Georgia, cities that are just under seventy miles apart.

Geographic clustering and proximity, which is crucial in markets whose participants value closeness and trust to discuss sensitive information in person (Hudson 1998; Wainwright 2011), is even more important in cases of questionable legality and overvaluation like conservation easement syndication.

Excessively appraised property values have become a source of conflict within the established conservation community. Because conservation easement tax benefits are premised on the charitable donation of partial interest in property, they require the cooperation of nonprofit conservation organizations to accept, monitor, and enforce them. The Land Trust Alliance, the umbrella organization and primary national institution that advocates for and accredits the land trust (private conservation) community, however, has taken a hard stance against syndicators, who they frame as ' bad apples ' that could ruin the tax incentives for the whole community (Lindstrom 2015). For this reason, the Land Trust Alliance has urged member land trusts to carefully scrutinize all land deals involving syndication and to refuse to sign IRS Form 8283 (the form required to claim the deduction) on any easement that is suspected to be ' fraudulent ' or overvalued. Without a land trust or nonprofit willing to accept the donation, a conservation easement fails to become a legitimate asset for investors.

As a result of the Land Trust Alliance ' s refusal to participate, new land trusts have been formed to accept the donation of easements from syndicates. These groups appeared quickly, far outside the established conservation community, and with direct links to financial markets. The Atlantic Coast Conservancy (Georgia) and Pelican Coast Conservancy (Alabama) are two examples of these groups, both of which bill themselves on their Websites as ' providing a rapid response ' (Atlantic Coast Conservancy 2021) to lands that were ' previously underserved ' (Pelican Coast Conservancy 2021) by their respective states ' extant land trust communities. More to the point, the Atlantic Coast Conservancy was started in 2009 by a conservation biologist and two ex-Wachovia bankers looking to salvage their real estate investments ravaged by the financial crisis. When the trio found they were unable to locate an established land trust willing to accept easements with hyperinflated appraisals, they formed their own (Elkind 2017). These developments are a good example of how far syndication has turned the logic of conservation on its head and has pushed easements to the edges of law.

## Economic Substance and the ' Unmaking of Assets '

The ' inherent incompleteness ' and malleability of law, including tax law, has meant that the state is often playing catch-up to the ways in which syndication stretches the limits of legislative intent of tax programs (Pistor 2019, 210). Regulatory lag has long allowed investors to ' exploit this incoherence by structuring transactions that produce tax benefits out of thin air ' (Bankman 2000, 29). Much like the corporate tax shelters that flourished in the 1990s, conservation easement and historic tax credit transactions have become ' engineered products [that] are split off from the underlying economics of a deal ' (Novack and Saunders 1998, 200). As the number of tax sheltering schemes grows in volume and expands in geography, the state must decide how far to allow syndications to drain its coffers (see U.S. Department of the Treasury 1999).

The U.S. federal state has a number of regulatory options to kill tax sheltering schemes, but recent case law indicates that it is leaning toward enforcement that grasps the actual economic reality of these deals. In particular, the economic substance doctrine has become the primary tool to ' unmake ' assets in conservation easement and historic tax credit markets. Formally added to the U.S. Internal Revenue Code in 2010, economic substance ' has played a particularly important role in the government ' s fight against corporate tax shelters ' (Bankman 2000, 6). Transactions are deemed to have economic substance only if ' (a) the transaction changes in a meaningful way (apart from Federal income tax effects) the taxpayer's economic position, and (b) the taxpayer has a substantial purpose (apart from Federal income tax effects) for entering into such transaction ' (26 U.S.C.A. §7701(o)(1)). That is, the economic substance doctrine ensures that transactions demonstrate a reasonable expectation of profit beyond the reduction of tax liability. Determining the economic substance of transactions, however, is not straightforward, because ' the line between aggressive, yet permissible, tax planning and improper, and sometimes criminal, tax evasion, like many lines in the law, is not at all clear or even straight ' (Glassman 2006, 669). Fraudulent transactions often appear as ' a deal done by very smart people that, absent tax considerations, would be very stupid ' (U.S. Department of the Treasury 1999, v). To ' sniff out ' tax shelters, economic substance arguments rely on tying the motives to the effect of a transaction, ' the latter of which incorporates other common law doctrines such as sham transaction, economic profit, and substance over form ' (Bankman 2000, 12). Economic substance is not only the threshold that the IRS uses to trigger an audit or litigation but it also exposes transactions to a number of other criteria that the state can ultimately use to rescind benefits.

Legal challenges to syndicated partnerships caught the relatively staid historic tax credit market off guard. For nearly forty years, the IRS took a handsoff approach to enforcing economic substance in syndications. In the wake of mounting pressure from the U.S. Department of the Treasury to crack down on ' corporate tax shelters ' as ' an unacceptable and growing level of tax avoidance behavior ' (U.S. Department of the Treasury 1999, 2), however, the IRS began an ' assault on historic tax credit partnerships ' starting in 2007 with Historic Boardwalk LLC v. Commissioner of the Internal Revenue Service (Jacobs 2013, 7). The transaction in question occurred in 2000 when the New Jersey Sports and Exposition Authority -a nonprofit with no tax liability -sold historic tax credits through a syndicated partnership structured with the Pitney Bowes Corporation to finance the rehabilitation of East Hall, the venue for the annual Miss America pageant. The IRS argued that the deal was a sham and lacked economic substance because the partnership eliminated Pitney Bowes ' s commercial risk and narrowly defined profitability to only the credits themselves. Many actors in the market agreed with the IRS. One investor in the historic tax credit market summarized the case in this way: ' I think Boardwalk was an example of where we got too creative, too cute, too clever with how we minimized the economics to the investor and made it be just about the tax credits ' (personal communication, 2 November 2016). The Third Circuit Court sided with the IRS and revoked the credits, ruling on the form of the transaction rather than the economic substance to find that Pitney Bowes was not a bona fide partner in the deal (U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit 2012, 85). Without fully closing the loophole, Historic Boardwalk demonstrates that the state can apply another powerful instrument in unmaking assets: introducing legal uncertainty to a market.

Fear continues to loom over the historic tax credit market, sustained by the threat of audits. An investor described the impact as follows: ' Boardwalk spooked and scared the hell out of everybody, with how we structure these deals. It ' s an economic partnership at the end of the day and we have to stay within the guidelines of tax law ' (personal communication, 2 November 2016). Indeed, many actors in the market felt like ' the IRS is playing a game of ' gotcha '' (Jacobs 2013, 17). The fact that audits lead to litigation that would dissolve the financial benefits of the credit has proved especially worrisome for investors who expressed concern that even ' well-connected people are under audit ' (personal communication, 22 September 2016). Although the IRS has yet to publicly issue additional findings, many syndicated deals have turned to a new financial intermediary to provide a third-party assessment of the fee structure of a tax credit deal. These ' reasonableness opinions, ' as an investor remarked, can be used to ' object against an IRS audit ' (personal communication, 22 September 2016). Reasonableness opinions might create a false sense of security, however, because these reports are derived from triangulating information about fees from comparable local redevelopment projects (i.e., comps). This insular market activity, once again, opens tax credits up to the ' worst ' practices of a self-referential network.

Ironically, reviving syndication has necessitated a recalibration of deal structuring that has made historic tax credit transactions more susceptible to the very thing investors are attempting to avoid: taxable income. In 2013, the IRS issued ' safe harbor ' guidelines (Rev. Proc. 2014-12) that outlined a series of requirements that, if all elements were met, would keep a partnership safe from audit. Many professionals honed in on the criteria around bona fide partnerships and interpreted this to mean that syndicated deals must be structured with downside risk or upside potential. In practice, this has led investors to substantiate their partnerships with cash flows. Baking preferred returns into deals tethers the tax-advantaged transaction back to the incomegenerating aspects of the asset. The state ' s efforts to reset tax credit assets highlight the multitude of financial attributes of property, particularly when income-generation operates as a punitive measure for investors looking to slash their expenses.

Questions of economic substance had never been important in conservation easements until fairly recently because tax credits tended to be claimed by individual landowners on their own family lands, rather than by syndicators on investment properties. This changed in 2019 when the IRS added syndicated conservation easement transactions to its ' dirty dozen ' list of abusive tax avoidance scams for the year (IRS 2019). It specifically sought to flag pass-through ownership entities (i.e., LLCs) selling interests in deals where a ' purported appraisal …

greatly inflates the value of the conservation easement based on unreasonable conclusions about the development potential of the real property ' (IRS 2017, 2). In the first case brought against conservation easement syndication, Coal Property Holdings LLC v. Commissioner of the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) (2019), the court ' s ruling disallowed the entire tax deduction. It did not do so, however, on the basis of economic substance, endeavoring instead to prove that the deduction did not meet perpetuity requirements for conservation easement donations. This was likely an intentional choice, because the IRS has increasingly gone after deductions ' for technical failures to comply with the Code and Regulations. When the government wins this latter type of case, the consequence is the loss of the entire deduction, not just a reduction ' (Lindstrom 2011, 483). Technicalities are but one way to unmake conservation easements that lack economic substance.

Ongoing speculation in the conservation community ventures that economic substance could be the basis of scrutiny in future tax court cases. Small (2019), a conservation and historic preservation tax attorney and former IRS employee who authored the federal income tax regulations for conservation easement donations, explained:

For more than a few decades now, the tax code has had rules that essentially say that if there is no ' economic substance ' to a transaction, that is, if the ONLY reason someone enters into the transaction is to generate tax deductions, those deductions can be denied. Or, put another way, if the only reason a deal is put together is to sell income tax deductions, and there really is no other economic reason why an investor would enter into such a deal, if there is no risk of loss and no chance of gain, then there is no economic substance to the deal and the deduction is denied.

Likewise, Lindstrom, a conservation attorney and legal scholar, cited several cases of related common law doctrines -sham transaction and step transaction -to contextualize the potential future use of economic substance to disallow syndicated easement transactions. He referenced Klauer v. Commissioner of the Internal Revenue Service as one example in which the IRS Commissioner pursued the step transaction doctrine ' to ensure that the tax consequences of a transaction turn on its substance rather than its form ' ( Klauer v. Commissioner 2010). Although the tax credit was not disallowed, the questioning of its validity on the grounds of form versus substance lays legal precedent and echoes a 2018 complaint issued by DOJ ' s Public Affairs Office against a group of conservation easement syndicators and promoters in Georgia. Pointedly, the complaint stated that ' these syndicates lack economic substance and are shams ' (DOJ 2018).

To tackle the more flagrant abuses within conservation easement syndication, the IRS and U.S. Congress indicated that they will also flag the substance of transactions over questions of time and overvaluation. A 2017 IRS notice (Notice 2017-10) and the proposed legislation regulating syndicated easements (Charitable Conservation Easement Program Integrity Act of 2019 [S. 170/H.R. 1992]) further draw distinctions between transactions with and without substance. Both raise concerns over deductions that are worth more than 250 percent of the initial property investment, and the Act suggests banning the donation of an easement on land that has been owned for less than three years. With a number of syndicated conservation easement tax cases on the docket, the IRS has offered the defendants a one-time opportunity to settle out of court, as long as they accept the full disallowance of their tax benefits and pay back all owed taxes, plus interest and maximum possible penalties (usually 40 percent; IRS 2020).

The state ' s current efforts to limit historic tax credit and conservation easement syndication involve punitive actions that slow down and encumber -rather than completely eliminate -expensereducing assets with holding periods, cash flows, and uncertainty. Focusing on barren economic transactions, questionable ownership forms, and egregious valuations, the economic substance doctrine makes it easier for the IRS, DOJ, SEC, and related enforcement actors to unmake assets that are predicated on tax sheltering and other forms of expense reduction that depend on the federal tax system. This is true across a range of geographies and types of syndicated investment vehicles. Recent regulatory clampdown evidences the claim that ' despite all the talk about the retreat of the state in a globalized economy, and the growing power of footloose finance, the state, in all its manifestations and across juridical scales, remains a central figure in the regulation of all sorts of flows critical to rendering [land] investable ' (Ouma 2020, 11). The state is not only alive and well, but in its dual capacity as profit source and market regulator, it ultimately determines the limits to financialization.

## Conclusion

In this article, we used syndicated property investment and the economic substance doctrine to highlight two sides of the same coin -the role of the state in both making and unmaking new forms of assets premised on expense reduction. Spanning across urban and rural geographies, historic tax credit and conservation easement markets illustrate multiple interlocking components in the construction of assets. In particular, we focused our analysis around four similarities in the assetization process: (1) the legal ownership forms syndication takes, (2) the communities of professionals who are willing to work together at the fringes of this legality, (3) the contorted geographies of finance that expense reduction produces, and (4) the specific interventions the government takes to limit the scope of assets. Syndicated historic tax credits flourish in secondary and tertiary U.S. cities where tight-knit communities of investors and practitioners cluster. Although in the rural and exurban context, the combination of willing actors and development pressure in the southeastern United States contributes to the explosion of conservation easement transactions with questionable ecological, social, or recreational values. Across both cases, a lack of economic substance has been grounds for ' unmaking ' the valuable asset of expense reduction. To theorize this process, we presented a legal-institutionalist approach to financialization, using a deeply place-based analysis of U.S. laws and institutions. This framework begins with the state as the arbiter of markets and assets and, in doing so repositions the federal state as a powerful, rather than a weak actor, with regard to finance capital and assetization. Beyond its applicability to our cases, we see four general contributions that a legalinstitutionalist approach to financialization can bring to the field of geography.

First, we demonstrated that a legal-institutionalist framework necessitates a deep dive into vast state data, including but by no means limited to laws, court cases, and financial filings. Combined with qualitative interviews from professionals in the field, detailed case studies developed in this fashion offer context-specific and country-specific insights into the intersection of the state, finance, and property.

Rather than seeing empirical specificity as a limitation, our study offers but one example of a growing global body of innovative geographic research into property and finance (see Ashwood et al. 2020; Kass 2020).

Second, and related, by using a legal-institutionalist approach to spatialize various forms of publicly available financial data, we ultimately challenged our a priori assumptions about where assets are created and where capital tends to pool. Our findings indicate that there are more similarities between investment strategies in St. Louis, Missouri, and rural Rome, Georgia, than there are between St. Louis and New York City. This is not to deny that there are many important differences between rural and urban geographies but instead suggests that a legal-institutionalist approach that starts with the creation of assets is useful in revealing continuities between geographies that are often pursued empirically by separate groups of researchers, despite conceptual overlap (see Fairbairn 2014; Fields 2018; Ouma 2020).

Third, a legal-institutionalist perspective traces the origins of assets through law and, in doing so, illustrates the ways in which assets are bundled and can unravel to reveal a multitude of financial benefits. Syndication rewards corporate and individual investors who either lack the financial capacity or desire to own and manage property with the tax benefits of property ownership. Syndicated properties can still generate income, however, and so investors in these schemes find themselves pulling profit from the same parcel of land or same building as other investors who are reaping cash flows. Even though structuring and selling these deals is no small task, it is common practice in rural and urban property markets. We attended to the specific fiscal and legal mechanisms and professional intermediaries that craft syndications across two markets, but avenues for future work could trace the multiple dimensions of a single financial asset across an array of investors to better understand the interplay between the law, property ownership, and debt and equity markets. This offers one way to examine the expansiveness of ' the asset economy ' (Adkins et al. 2020).

Finally, after thirty years of writing on neoliberalism and austerity, it is assumed by scholars that places like the United States are very weak federal states and that capital is largely in the metaphorical driver ' s seat. Yet, as Pistor (2019) reminded us, the state has the capacity to ' roll back ' capital.

Common law doctrines like economic substance offer but one example of what she called ' persistent incrementalism ' where the state, via the law, pushes back the overreach of financial markets (Pistor 2019, 229). This has the potential to radically reframe the ways in which critical geographers have thought about financialization by asking them to center the state and law in their analyses, transforming understandings of where power lies, where profit and assets are derived from, and how exploitative financialization might be limited.
