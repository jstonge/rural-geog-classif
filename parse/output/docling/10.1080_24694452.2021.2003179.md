## Urban Flight and Rural Rights in a Pandemic: Exploring Narratives of Place, Displacement, and ' the Right to Be Rural ' in the Context of COVID-19

<!-- image -->

S. Ashleigh Weeden,  Jean Hardy, † and Karen Foster ‡

 School of Environmental Design &amp; Rural Development, University of Guelph, Canada † Department of Media &amp; Information, Michigan State University, USA ‡ Department of Sociology &amp; Social Anthropology, Dalhousie University, Canada

The COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated many preexisting challenges facing rural communities and brought tensions in rural -urban relations closer to the surface. This article offers an explorative contribution to discussions in critical geography by comparing media narratives surrounding urban flight to rural places during the COVID-19 pandemic. Comparing field sites in rural Michigan (United States), Ontario (Canada), and the ' Atlantic bubble ' (Canada), we use an emerging theorization of the ' right to be rural ' to explore how urban flight and rural displacement are tied to concepts of community, identity, and safety. This approach is grounded in the political economy of rurality and emphasizes the power relations, inequalities, and historical contingencies that structure the experiences of full-time and part-time rural residents during the pandemic. Our exploratory discussion surfaces critical tensions in the geographically and socioeconomically uneven implications of the pandemic, including the ' anxious economic acquiescence ' experienced in many tourism-dependent rural regions and both the ' hard ' and ' soft ' ways in which rural regions responded to increased demands for access. We argue that the political economy of rural -urban relations is critical to understanding the social processes that will shape the ' right to be rural ' during and after COVID-19. Key Words: Canada, COVID-19, displacement, rural, rights.

A sthe novel coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic spread across North America in spring 2020, it simultaneously sharpened rural -urban divides and highlighted rural -urban linkages (cf. Weeden 2020b; Maclaren and Philip 2021). Governments across all jurisdictions wrestled with whether and how to require people to stay apart, or stay home, to keep everyone safe. Public health interventions involved limiting travel and movement at international, interregional, and interhousehold scales. North American jurisdictions used a blend of ' hard ' (i.e., punishable with a fine or arrest) and ' soft ' restrictions, the latter appealing to individual responsibility and relying on social pressure. Rural regions with low virus case counts early in the pandemic faced a rising tide of people with means looking to escape from urban ' hot spots ' (Petersen 2020). Although some local tourism initiatives tepidly welcomed the tide, encouraging ' staycations ' in nearby communities, many rural individuals and communities pleaded with people to stay home

(Petersen 2020). The dissonance between such conflicting responses produces poignant commentary about how economic class, geography, and rights frameworks have collided during the pandemic.

With little certainty about when and how abruptly the COVID-19 pandemic will end (even as effective vaccines spread across the Global West), it is important to examine its impact on rural -urban dynamics, as pandemic-driven waves of urban flight threaten to displace existing rural people and communities and redraw the map of where people live. We draw on the political economy of rurality to contribute to a growing body of critical conversations in geography about place, power, and policy in contemporary rural life -the intersection of which is increasingly framed as the ' right to be rural ' and constitutes the lens through which we explore media coverage of rural -urban dynamics during the pandemic (Foster 2018a, 2018b; Foster and Main 2020; Weeden 2020a, 2020b, 2020c; Foster and Jarman 2021). By examining key media narratives from our respective field sites in Michigan, Ontario, and Atlantic Canada (see Figures 1 and 2), we explore the power relations, inequalities, and historical contingencies that structure the experiences of both full-time and part-time rural residents during the pandemic. We argue that inherent tensions in accessing or acquiring a ' right to be rural ' are central to regional COVID-19 experiences, conflicts, and inequalities.

## The Right to Be Rural?

French sociologist Henri Lefebvre ' s 1968 ' right to the city ' proposed the reclamation of urban life from capitalist, individual entitlements by putting community experiences at the heart of civic decision making (Lefebvre 1996; Harvey 2003, 2008). Civic movements around the globe subsequently picked up the ' right to the city ' as a resistance cry against gentrification, privatization, and the encroachment of neoliberal capitalism into civic life. Rural scholars (Barraclough 2013), including ourselves (Foster 2018a, 2018b; Foster and Main 2020; Weeden 2020a, 2020b, 2020c; Foster and Jarman 2021), have asked whether there is also a ' right to the rural. '

The question arises from significant political and economic changes that have restructured rural communities over the last several decades (Halseth et al. 2010). Rural places have been, and continue to be, shaped by legacies of colonialism; histories of extractive and productivist economies (Innis 1956); neoliberal restructuring of global, national, and regional economies (Woods 2005); and globalization and urbanization (Woods 2005, 2007). Rural life has thus been animated by endogenous and exogenous forces, often outside local control, pushing many communities to the periphery of broader social, economic, and political agendas (cf. Woods 2010, 2011; Canadian Rural Revitalization Foundation 2015). The resulting erosion of public services, institutions, and amenities in rural communities has made rural living more difficult, challenging the belief, sometimes encoded in law, that people are free to live wherever they choose (cf. Canadian Rural Revitalization Foundation 2015; Foster and Jarman 2021).

Adopting the rights-based lens we call the right to be rural means focusing on whether the conditions that make being rural -staying there, living comfortably there, having equal access to necessary goods and services -are present or possibly challenged (Foster and Jarman 2021). Here, we explore shifts and controversies around rural -urban dynamics, rural tourism, urban flight, and rural displacement during the COVID-19 pandemic through this lens (cf. Foster 2018a, 2018b; Foster and Jarman 2021). In doing so, we open critical new lines of placeand rights-based inquiry into the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic.

## Exploring Place-Based Narratives of Rural Displacement in Michigan, Ontario, and Atlantic Canada

In rural communities, as everywhere, place shapes the relationships between institutions, individuals, and environment, which in turn produce local identities, cultures, and economies (cf. Reimer and Markey 2008; Halseth, Markey, and Bruce 2009;

Baldacchino, Vodden, and Gibson 2015). The role of place in rural communities is itself fundamentally shaped by low population densities and long distances from other communities, particularly urban centers. Based on these observations, we compare place-based narratives and experiences across our respective field locations in Michigan, Ontario, and the ' Atlantic bubble ' in Canada (which included the provinces of Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, and Prince Edward Island) to show how these rural regions have managed the competing demands of the pandemic, economic uncertainty, and the potential of urban flight -driven displacements.

These cases were selected based on criteria proposed by Yin (2014) and Stake (2000) and supported by Ragin ' s (2007) argument that comparative case research creates opportunities to focus on phenomena that ' are of interest because they are infrequent ' (68). All have significantly rural populations, have faced similar social and economic restructuring processes in the last thirty to fifty years, and have experienced similar tensions between yearround residents and seasonal residents and tourists during the pandemic (Rich 2020). The cases that follow are drawn from local and regional media sources identified using a purposive search strategy (Patton 2015) on news and media databases (e.g., NewsBank, Nexus Uni, Google News) with the terms ' rural  AND COVID-19 ' or ' touris  AND coronavirus, ' filtered to our regions. We employed a grounded theory approach to thematic analysis of the resulting sources (cf. Heydarian 2016). Although media sources represent a particular kind of public discourse, they are nevertheless helpful for analyzing emerging trends and are particularly useful for observing unfolding political and policy agendas (Saraisky 2016). Our exploratory analysis is informed by our own positionalities as researchers with experience in the rural regions we write about (cf. Naples and Sachs 2009). We are limited by the emergent nature of the issue and the novelty of the pandemic as a driver in rural -urban dynamics and displacements. Further, due to the scale and scope of this special issue, we present our sources summarized in our overarching narrative rather than relying on extensive direct quotation.

In Michigan and Ontario, we explore how very different approaches to public health managed to produce similar journeys from firm ' STAY AWAY '

messages to what we have termed ' anxious economic acquiescence. ' Through the Atlantic bubble, we demonstrate how it has guarded against disease, but also displacement, with hard boundaries that create explicit processes for claiming a right to be rural (or a right to be Atlantic). Yet, across all three cases, the prioritization of economic concerns and an overreliance on outside investment and resource extraction via tourism put rural communities in a catch-22: They could be economically secure by opening up or they could be safe from COVID-19 by shutting down but not both. That impossible position is popularly conceptualized in terms of urban versus rural individuals, but we argue that it is more structural than that, and our cases show that it cannot be addressed solely through individualizing appeals to personal responsibility.

## From ' STAY AWAY ' to Anxious Economic Acquiescence: COVID-19 and the Political Economy of ' Up North ' in Michigan

Michigan ' s rural economy relies on the seasonal movement of people from metropolitan areas to the natural respite of ' Up North. ' The impact of COVID19, including a brief interruption in this seasonal movement in the spring and early summer of 2020, and the anxious economic acquiescence that followed are best understood through regional histories of displacement by settler colonialism, natural resource extraction, and now the tourism and recreation economies.

Natural resource extraction economies were essential to settler colonial development of the northern frontiers in Michigan. The economic boom provided by logging and mining in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries did not last, however; many parts of rural Michigan witnessed massive depopulation in the twentieth century, the effects of which are seen today. An overreliance on tourism and other facets of the service economy have become the new export economies (Shapiro 2013). The path from natural resource extraction to tourism, and the growing reliance on outside investment, has been exacerbated by the sharp decline of state and federal funding to rural regions over the past forty years (Randall 2020). Shifts from public to private investment in rural development are key to understanding challenges in accessing a right to be rural and the effects of COVID-19 in the same communities.

As COVID-19 cases surged in cities across the United States in March 2020, rural communities in northern Michigan and the Upper Peninsula of Michigan 1 seemed naturally protected; the region ' s remoteness and low population density led to very few cases in the first months of the pandemic. By 28 April 2020, five counties in the Upper Peninsula -equivalent to the footprint of Connecticut or twice that of Wuhan, China (with a fraction of the population) -had zero reported cases (Mack 2020). Paired with economic lockdown policies that worked in favor of rural economies reopening, these conditions encouraged a summer of staycations, with people vacationing domestically in rural places rather than in cities or international destinations. Traffic across the Mackinac Bridge, which connects the more populated and urban Lower Peninsula of Michigan with the much more rural and remote Upper Peninsula, saw backups for miles as people swarmed north (Werly 2020). In fact, local estimates across rural Michigan said that the summer 2020 tourism season was comparable to the previous year and, in some places, even more successful (Beck 2020a, 2020b).

As tourism numbers declined as temperatures dropped in the fall, however, people whose livelihoods were reliant on the summertime influx of urban outsiders were threatened by a new migrant: COVID-19 (Brewster 2020; Roelofs 2020). Due to the anxious economic acquiesence that led rural places to allow seasonal visitors, this relatively geographically isolated, and therefore naturally protected, region became a hotbed of transmission in late 2020. For example, the Upper Peninsula of Michigan had fewer than 200 cases (in a population of just over 310,000) as of 1 July 2020 but more than 10,000 cases by the Thanksgiving holiday. Many rural businesses quoted in media coverage of the pandemic seemed resigned to opening themselves up to the risk of the virus to keep their businesses open and ensure that their region, so reliant on outside investment, would maintain itself. Such anxious economic acquiescence reveals the difficulties of maintaining a right to be rural when rural life becomes a commodity.

## Conflict, Confusion, and COVID-19 in Ontario ' s Cottage Country

Ontario ' s cottage country is variously considered as Georgian Bay and the surrounding region (e.g., Muskoka), or along the coastline of Lake Huron (particularly the Saugeen/Bruce Peninsula), or the rural areas of eastern Ontario (e.g., Prince Edward County or the Kawartha Lakes; see Figure 1). The term cottage country positions rural landscapes as amenity-rich, yet sparsely populated spaces far away from major cities, deemphasizing notions of rural communities as places where people live and work more permanently. It also replaces the truth and trauma of colonialism with a romanticized reality only accessible to a small percentage of people (Stevens 2018). Such discrepancies between the ideal and real cottage country are increasingly bound up with displacement experiences for both year-round residents and working-class seasonal visitors, as demand for leisure property grows among those with means (Bingley 2020; Kalinowski 2020).

The same glossy narratives that created the idea of cottage country were found throughout media coverage of COVID-19 in the spring of 2020. For many, a cottage was the perfect place to escape the pandemic (Cotnam 2020; Poling 2020). Public health experts and community leaders, however, urged people to stay home (Paikin 2020a, 2020b; Weeden 2020b). Many rural Ontario communities wrestled with the same conflict between protecting public health and economic acquiescence as their counterparts in Michigan (Flanagan 2020; Kenny 2020). 2 Year-round residents were concerned that visitors would bring the virus with them and overwhelm already limited local resources, from groceries to emergency health care (Furst et al. 2020; Paikin 2020a, 2020b; Peterborough Public Health 2020). A lack of clarity or legal ramifications led to confusion and conflict, however, as Premier Doug Ford visited his own cottage while telling others to stay home (Cotnam 2020; Humphreys 2020), property owners threatened to withhold property tax payments if they were not allowed to access their seasonal properties (Hodgins 2020), and First Nations that leased land to seasonal property owners closed their borders (Davis 2020; Simon 2020).

As the first wave of the pandemic dissipated across Ontario, messages to stay away ceded to ' We can ' t stop you ' and eventually ' You ' re welcome here (with reservations) ' (Federation of Ontario Cottagers ' Associations 2020). As local leaders prioritized mitigating the economic consequences of a missed tourism season, cottage country dynamics seemed to return almost to normal across much of Ontario in summer 2020, mirroring the anxious economic acquiescence noted in Michigan (Hanslep 2020). By September, however, stories surfaced about even small gatherings at cottages becoming superspreader events (Blewett 2020).

A second wave of rising COVID-19 case counts and pandemic restrictions loomed in spring 2021, bringing new potential displacements. Real estate demand and prices in cottage country ballooned (Bingley 2020; Kalinowski 2020) and grassroots initiatives like the ' Ninety Minutes from Toronto ' project map highlighted smaller and rural communities that might appeal to those looking to escape the city (Williams 2020). The question remains: Do rural people get a say in what happens next?

## Bursting the Bubble? Regional Collaboration to Fight COVID-19 in Atlantic Canada

Atlantic Canada is one of the most rural parts of Canada (40 to 50 percent of the population lives outside a major urban center). Notwithstanding recent growth in the region ' s major cities (e.g., Halifax, Nova Scotia), Atlantic Canada has experienced a cycle of economic booms and busts tied to the rise and fall of primary resource sectors -contributing to a stubborn narrative that treats the region ' s economy as a perpetual problem to be solved (Foster and Main 2020). Prior to COVID-19, tourism was a $5 billion industry in Atlantic Canada, and it remains a key sector for investment and development (e.g., the Atlantic Growth Strategy launched in 2016). Foster and Main (2020) argued, though, that many rural residents in Atlantic Canada view tourism ' not as the best choice, but as the last hope for communities where primary industry is believed to be dead and impossible to revive ' (76). The COVID-19 pandemic revealed how tourism-led development created a new wave of ' single-industry towns ' dependent on visitors.

On 3 July 2020, the four Atlantic premiers agreed to allow unrestricted travel among residents of their provinces and restricted travel (i.e., a fourteen-day quarantine) into the region by anyone else. The resulting Atlantic bubble (see Figure 2) helped maintain some of the lowest numbers of COVID-19 cases in Canada. In November 2020, when the second wave arrived in New Brunswick and Nova Scotia, the bubble burst with other provinces removing themselves (Groff 2020; Jerrett, Farnell, and Brown 2020). Although each province maintained its own specific rules about travel during the pandemic (Vibert 2020) and there was still considerable economic loss (including 171,000 jobs), the Atlantic bubble might have supported a quicker economic rebound by allowing life to continue more normally than in places where the risk of transmission was higher (Gordon 2020a, 2020b).

In contrast to the anxious economic acquiescence in Michigan and Ontario, the Atlantic bubble mitigated some of the economic harm of restricting the regional economy while guarding against outside threats to community health. In some ways, this counters the experiences of rural displacement noted elsewhere; Atlantic year-round residents and localized actors pushed would-be visitors, including many seasonal property owners, to the periphery.

Although the bubble was largely supported by people across the Atlantic region and people within the bubble successfully intermingled for months (CBC News -Newfoundland &amp; Labrador 2020; Cleary 2020), it was not without challengers and challenges. People on the islands of Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland were nervous about giving up the ' natural ' immunity afforded by their hard borders (Cleary 2020). ' Plate shaming ' (Battis and Jones 2020) became a concern, as cars with license plates from outside the bubble were targeted with hostility by people who perceived them as potential virus vectors. Within each Atlantic province, rural anxieties continue to surface from time to time, particularly when case counts increase in more populated areas. When residents of Halifax Regional Municipality (HRM), the most densely populated area in Nova Scotia, were given unique restrictions and urged not to leave HRM for any nonessential reason in November 2020, institutions and businesses in surrounding rural communities screened visitors and turned away people from HRM, and rural residents were sternly instructed not to come to HRM for Christmas shopping, an annual tradition for many (Shaw 2020). In spring 2021, as a third wave descended on Nova Scotia, the province ' s chief medical officer of health controversially told HRM residents with rural cottages to check their ' privilege ' before complaining about travel restrictions; people were dying (Lethbridge 2021).

## Insiders and Outsiders: Whose Rights Count?

The COVID-19 pandemic has presented new challenges and opportunities for the application of rights-based frameworks to rural -urban dynamics. Mobility rights are enshrined in the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. The U.S. Supreme Court has consistently upheld the right to movement among states (including the right to be treated as a welcome visitor rather than a hostile stranger). During the pandemic, these, along with property rights, have underpinned demands by seasonal property owners and vacationers to access rural communities. Seasonal residents do have social and economic ties to their seasonal home communities, and the pandemic has forced the issue of whether these contributions confer ' insider access ' to rural regions or not.

The things that make rural places unique and attractive also make them vulnerable to commodification and exploitation. As we discussed earlier, the anxious economic acquiescence in response to such demands in Ontario and Michigan emerges at the intersection of rural economic history and the policy failures of governments unable to handle the pandemic. Fear of a virus is displaced by the fear of economic collapse. Atlantic Canada ' s protective measures might have served as a countersuit to these forces by clearly defining who had a right to access ' the bubble ' (without quarantine) and who did not, determining clear (although controversial) measures for how one might acquire that right, and prioritizing local and community needs. The fact that it was contested by some people outside Atlantic Canada -even challenged in the Newfoundland and Labrador Supreme Court -underscores the relationality of rights, their political character, and the competing definitions of freedom currently circulating, despite charter and constitutional rights (cf. The Saltwire Network 2020).

## Summary and Future Research

Using the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic as a unique case, this article is an exploratory contribution to discussions in critical geography that surfaces new lines of inquiry into contemporary rural -urban dynamics, yielding insights that challenge current conceptions of who and how the right to be rural is acquired or accessed. The complexities of the pandemic shed light on the relationship between mobility rights and the political economy of the rural, when rural places must balance opening themselves up to the spread of a virus with the possibility of severe economic decline. The media narratives presented reveal concepts of community, identity, and safety at the center of this balancing act. This exploration invites future research into how such narratives might evolve as the pandemic continues and (hopefully) eventually ends and the pandemic ' s ultimate impact on rural economies and rural -urban dynamics.
