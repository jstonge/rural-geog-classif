## Accessible Green Spaces? Spatial Disparities in Residential Green Space among People with Disabilities in the United States

Sandy Wong,  Johnathan Rush, † Franklin Bailey,  and Allan C. Just †

<!-- image -->

<!-- image -->

 Department of Geography, Florida State University, USA

† Department of Environmental Medicine &amp; Public Health, Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, USA

This article presents new quantitative results on the distribution of residential green space for people with disabilities in the United States, building on and bridging scholarly research in two distinct domains: one involving approaches that quantify disparities in green space access among racialized minorities and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, and the other using qualitative methods that demonstrate that most green spaces remain inaccessible and unwelcoming to disabled visitors. Using generalized additive models (GAMs) that controlled for demographic factors and climatological characteristics, we find that residential areas with more green space generally have a higher proportion of disabled residents. The statistical results run counter to expectations from the literature, thus complicating the prevailing narrative and indicating a need for mixed-methods research to examine multiple dimensions of access and environmental justice. Using cluster analysis to assess spatial trends, we detect residential clusters of high disability and low green space and find that they are located in predominantly non-White, urban, and more socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods compared to clusters of high disability and high green space. Cluster analysis results suggest that there are inequities in green space access at the intersection of disability, race, and class, as well as across the urban -rural continuum. Key Words: cluster analysis, disability, environmental justice, GAMs, green space access.

T o date, widespread interest in green spaces and their health benefits has generated two distinctive areas of geographic research (Foley 2018). One line of research uses spatial analysis to quantify disparities in access to green spaces among different populations and estimate their associations with health outcomes (Foley 2018; Labib, Lindley, and Huck 2020). This scholarship reflects increasing attention to inequities in environmental benefits in the environmental justice literature since the late 1970s, a shift from environmental justice ' s historical focus on environmental harms amid marginalized populations (Byrne 2017). A substantive body of environmental justice research focuses on distributive justice of urban green spaces -the notion that there should be equal allocation of green space regardless of social axes of difference (Byrne 2017). In practice, distributive justice examines whether different populations have equivalent levels of access in terms of green space number or size, proximity, or green space per capita (Byrne 2017). Scholars have found unequal distributions of urban green space in the United States, with racialized minorities and socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods having significantly worse access to green space than predominantly White and wealthy neighborhoods (Heynen, Perkins, and Roy 2006; Dai 2011; Duncan et al. 2013). Other studies find that although disadvantaged groups live closer to parks compared to privileged groups, they tend to have access to smaller, lower quality parks (Rigolon 2016; Williams et al. 2020). From this evidence, it is clear that environmental injustice and racism figure into the inequitable allocation of green space and that marginalized communities are deprived of the restorative benefits of such spaces.

A second strand of geographic investigation uses qualitative and humanistic approaches to evaluate place-based, embodied experiences in green spaces (Foley 2018). Much of this research builds on Gesler ' s (1992) seminal work on therapeutic landscapes where the analytic focus is on how places are perceived to be therapeutic and how healing processes in these places promote people ' s well-being. Drawing from cultural geography, Gesler (1992) highlighted how landscapes can be interpreted through people ' s sense of place and the symbolic meanings that people instill on to landscapes. The predominant narrative about green spaces is that they are understood to be therapeutic sites that can promote people ' s physical and mental health (Finlay et al. 2015; Bell et al. 2018; Fong, Hart, and James 2018). A closer consideration of people ' s embodied experiences, however, reveals a more nuanced story. People ' s engagements with green spaces are mediated through their social identities and contexts (Dinnie, Brown, and Morris 2013; Plane and Klodawsky 2013; Bell et al. 2018) such that some groups are unable to access green spaces and have nontherapeutic experiences (Plane and Klodawsky 2013; Finlay et al. 2015). As an example, Latinx residents describe how they feel excluded and not welcomed in certain parks in Los Angeles due to a lack of signs in Spanish, perceptions that park users are predominantly White, and personal experiences of discrimination (Byrne 2012).

Disability is an important social axis of difference that influences people ' s experiences with green spaces, but less attention has been paid to disability compared to race, ethnicity, and class. The research on green space and disability is comparatively sparse, and largely uses qualitative methods. This literature reveals that like nondisabled populations, disabled people ascribe similarly positive meanings to green spaces and value such spaces for their therapeutic benefits (Mathers 2008; Macpherson 2017; Corazon et al. 2019). Green spaces are found to improve the mental health and social interaction of adults and children with disabilities (Jakubec et al. 2016; Lakhani et al. 2019; Li et al. 2019). Most green spaces, however, remain inaccessible and uninviting to disabled visitors. Many green spaces are physically inaccessible to people with mobility and vision disabilities, particularly more ' natural ' spaces that provide closer physical and sensory contact with vegetation and wildlife (Macpherson 2017; Corazon et al. 2019). For those with developmental and learning disabilities, negative sensory and social encounters in green spaces cause visitors to feel unwelcome and unwilling to return (Mathers 2008; Li et al. 2019). A focus on disability -with its range of body and mind differences -is critical for disrupting normative notions of green space accessibility that typically assume users to have nondisabled bodies.

Notably absent from the research on disability and green space is the use of quantitative, spatial analytic methods to measure and confirm the inaccessibility issues that are raised in qualitative studies. To complement and build on prior findings, our article investigates disparities in green space access among disabled populations on a large scale using statistical models and cluster analysis. The geographic extent of our analysis is the continental United States, extending the geographic scope of previous research, which focused on a specific locality or a set of urban sites (Heynen, Perkins, and Roy 2006; Dai 2011; Duncan et al. 2013). We also compare green space access across the urban -rural continuum, expanding on the conventional focus on urban sites to consider rural locations (Szaboova, Brown, and Fisher 2020). Because disabled individuals are more likely to experience social isolation and poor health compared to nondisabled populations (Strauser 2013), regular access to green space might be important for mitigating the negative effects of other factors on health. This project provides muchneeded estimates of green space access for examining inequalities among disabled residents and for identifying where improvements in access are most needed to enhance the neighborhood conditions and wellbeing of residents with disabilities.

## What Counts as Green Space (Access)?

Green spaces are natural environs that include trees, grass, parks, forests, natural reserves, greenways, and community gardens. A customary approach to quantifying green space is using a vegetation index or a land-use database (James et al. 2015). Vegetation indexes are developed using satellite imagery that captures light reflected by green plants and thus characterizes a terrain ' s greenness. Of these indexes, the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) is the most used in epidemiological studies (James et al. 2015). Land-use databases classify land into categories based on their primary use. Categories relevant to green spaces include tree canopy, forests, and open space. Yet another approach is to measure the distribution of parks or open spaces and people ' s proximity to them. In our study, we use and compare data from a vegetation index and a land-use database. We considered using park data, but in our review of national data sets, we found that they have limited coverage of local parks. As an example, the Protected Areas Database of the United States, which is the official national inventory of protected areas for recreational and other uses, estimates that although the database includes 95 percent of federal lands, it only captures 60 percent of parks and other lands at the state, regional, and local levels (U.S. Geological Survey n.d.). As the focus of this study is on residential and local green spaces, the use of such a data set is likely to result in highly inaccurate estimates of green space access.

Quantitatively, green space access is measured in different ways: as a quantity within an area (e.g., mean NDVI within a census tract), as proximity to a discrete green space, or as green space per capita (James et al. 2015; Rigolon 2016; Byrne 2017). These measures can be described as representing green space availability or distribution. These quantities, however, merely capture one dimension of access. Other components of access include the actual use of green spaces, which include indirect (e.g., viewing from a window), incidental (e.g., encounters while conducting another activity such as cycling to work), and intentional (e.g., hiking) interactions (Keniger et al. 2013). The quality of green spaces is also important as quality influences use (Jones, Hillsdon, and Coombes 2009; Bell et al. 2014). To understand the causes of inequities in green space access, a critical evaluation is needed of the historical and social reasons why certain groups have less access than others (Byrne 2017). Nonetheless, although geographic availability characterizes one facet of access, it is a necessary condition of access because green spaces need to be within reach for people to use them. Furthermore, a quantitative examination of green space access for disabled populations remains uninvestigated. A population-level analysis is a key first step toward understanding distributive justice pertaining to green space and disability.

There is widespread interdisciplinary interest in urban green spaces and their potential to contribute to healthy places and populations. The prevailing view is that green spaces can improve the physical and psychological health of individuals and communities through multiple pathways. Plants can filter polluted air, regulate heat and humidity, and mitigate noise. Parks, forests, and greenways can provide spaces for respite, physical activity, and social interaction (Byrne 2017; Markevych et al. 2017; Fong, Hart, and James 2018). Studies find that higher access or exposure to green space is linked to better mental health (de Vries et al. 2013) and self-reported health

(Huang et al. 2021), lower overweight and obesity (de Vries et al. 2013; Dadvand et al. 2014), decreased risk of stroke mortality (Hu, Liebens, and Rao 2008), lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality (Coutts, Horner, and Chapin 2010), lower morbidity (Maas et al. 2009), and decreased risk of low birth weight (Agay-Shay et al. 2014).

Interest in urban greenspace extends beyond academia to policy circles where greenspace development is driven by global movements to create ' healthy ' and ' sustainable ' cities that improve the quality of life for residents (Byrne 2017; Bell et al. 2018; Pearsall and Eller 2020). Neoliberal logic, however, often shapes green development and getting a return on investment is prioritized over equity considerations (Byrne 2017). In some U.S. cities, greening strategies are followed by speculative development and gentrification (Wolch, Byrne, and Newell 2014). A growth in privately owned public spaces has further limited the public accessibility of many city parks (Pearsall and Eller 2020). Although there is an urgent need to recenter equity and to generate greening strategies that benefit marginalized populations, meaningful discussions of access issues for disabled residents remain largely absent from academic and policy discourses.

The urban -as opposed to the rural -figures prominently in green space research and policy, leaving a notable gap in knowledge about rural green spaces. Although rural areas are greener, people ' s access to green spaces might deviate substantially from that of city dwellers. Rural areas are typically more disadvantaged than their urban counterparts. Rural residents tend to have poorer health, lower incomes, and less access to public services (Erickson et al. 2018), which could in turn affect how they access green spaces (Szaboova, Brown, and Fisher 2020). Indeed, a study in a rural county in the United Kingdom found that socioeconomic disadvantage is a key factor influencing residents ' access to green and other natural spaces (Szaboova, Brown, and Fisher 2020). Here, residents are discouraged from traveling to a natural space due to transportation options being unaffordable for those with low incomes (Szaboova, Brown, and Fisher 2020). Given that disability prevalence is highest in rural areas in the United States (McCoy, Davis, and Hudson 1994; Wong 2016), it is important to pay attention to rurality when investigating matters concerning disabled populations.

## Disability and Inaccessible Green Spaces

In 1990, the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) was signed into law, prohibiting discrimination against disabled individuals in the United States. Although this generated guidelines for making parks and playgrounds more accessible and inclusive (e.g., via the 2000 ADA Accessibility Guidelines for Play Areas and the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design), many recreational spaces continue to be inaccessible, thus restricting disabled people ' s access to these spaces and their participation in recreational activities (Younker 2010; Rimmer et al. 2017; Calder, Sole, and Mulligan 2018). Furthermore, these guidelines only include accommodations for a narrow range of disabilities (e.g., wheelchair users), overlooking access issues for individuals with other disabilities (e.g., hearing impairment, visual impairment, cognitive disability). To design green spaces that are truly inclusive, access issues must be considered for all people with diverse disabilities.

Green spaces remain inaccessible in part because many professionals who are responsible for compliance with accessibility standards have a limited understanding of the needs of people with disabilities and of universal design -the concept that environments should be designed to be accessible to all people regardless of their background (Imrie and Hall 2001; Evcil 2010; Sherman and Sherman 2012; Sterman et al. 2019). There continue to be park and recreation professionals who have limited cultural competency training particularly as it relates to disability (Anderson and Stone 2005), and designers who have little to no training on accessibility standards (Evcil 2010; Sherman and Sherman 2012). In the United States, many design professionals view the ADA as legalistic and punitive, and consequently adhere only to the minimum requirements of the law rather than seeking to achieve universal access (Sherman and Sherman 2012). Developers have conveyed skepticism about providing access needs for what they view as an insignificant minority of the population (Imrie and Hall 2001). These studies indicate a dire need for better education and training about disability and universal design, as there is a lack of awareness that disabled people comprise the world ' s largest minority group (United Nations n.d.).

Bringing more attention to disability is a key step toward understanding and addressing greenspace inaccessibility in research and policy. This article uses statistical methods to analyze the distribution of green space for disabled people in the United States and to generate generalizable results, building on qualitative findings of case studies that demonstrate that most green spaces are inaccessible to disabled individuals. To our knowledge, there are no quantitative findings on green space access for disabled populations. Therefore, the results will provide a better understanding of the geographic extent to which green spaces are accessible or inaccessible to people with disabilities. We focus on residential green space as disabled individuals are more likely to spend their time at home or in their neighborhood (Pag  an-Rodr  ı guez 2014; Myers and Ravesloot 2016). We also use cluster analysis to detect hot spots of high disability and low green space. In doing so, this research reveals where green space inaccessibility is most prominent and where accessible green space is most needed.

## Materials and Methods

## Study Area

The study area is the continental United States, which comprises forty-eight conterminous states and the District of Columbia. We omitted Alaska, Hawaii, and Puerto Rico because our cluster analysis methods rely on polygon contiguity and these areas are not contiguous to the continental United States.

The United States is a large region with diverse demographics and green space availability. Nearly 20 percent of the population has a disability (U.S. Census Bureau Public Information Office 2016), which is a higher prevalence than the global estimate of 15 percent (Kostanjsek et al. 2013). Green space provision is spatially heterogeneous, with more coverage in rural areas compared to urban ones (Wen et al. 2013). At the same time, green space availability has been trending downward nationally with rising urbanization (Nowak and Greenfield 2018). As green space coverage declines, it becomes increasingly urgent to quantify disparities in green space distribution and to identify localities with a critical lack of green space.

## Data

Our data set is at the scale of census tracts and includes data collected during the year 2010 or close to it. Census tracts are common proxies for neighborhoods and are widely used to represent residential neighborhood characteristics, including green space coverage (Wen et al. 2013). We used the TIGER/Line shapefile for the tract boundaries (U.S. Census Bureau 2012).

Demographic data were downloaded from the National Historical Geographic Information System, which came from the U.S. Census Bureau ' s 2010 decennial census and the 2008 -2012 American Community Survey (ACS; Manson et al. 2019). We obtained data on disability, age, sex, household income, educational attainment, race and ethnicity, and employment. The Census Bureau defines disability as the presence or absence of limitations related to vision, hearing, cognitive, ambulatory, independent living, and self-care capacity. One caveat is that disabled, low-income, and racialized individuals are ' hard-to-count ' (Lee et al. n.d.), rendering disability more visible among White, wealthier populations.

To distinguish census tracts across the urban -rural continuum, we used 2010 Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes from the U.S. Department of Agriculture (2020). We categorized census tracts as metropolitan, micropolitan, or rural. These categories are determined by levels of urbanization, population density, and daily commuting patterns.

To account for climatological characteristics, we used K € oppen-Geiger climate classifications (Beck et al. 2018). Each census tract was assigned to one of four climates: tropical, arid, temperate, or cold. Two census tracts were originally classified as polar, but due to the small number, we reclassified them as cold.

We used two popular metrics of green space: NDVI and tree canopy cover (James et al. 2015). For NDVI, we used the Temporally Smoothed Weekly AQUA Collection Six Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer from the U.S. Geological Survey (n.d.). We calculated the mean of the fifty-two weekly values in the year 2010. As a measure of green vegetation, NDVI ranges in value from   1 to 1, with higher values indicating higher densities of green vegetation. Percentage tree canopy cover was downloaded from the Multi-Resolution Land Characteristics Consortium ' s National Land Cover Dataset (U.S. Department of Agriculture Forest Service n.d.). Values range from 0 to 100 percent and most of the data were from the years 2009 through 2011. In the text that follows, green space refers to NDVI and tree canopy unless noted otherwise.

Our data set started with 72,518 census tracts. We excluded tracts with no people, no housing, no data for the variables, and low reliability in the ACS data (i.e., coefficient of variation &gt; 30 percent; Jurjevich et al. 2018). Our final data set includes 60,963 tracts.

## Methods

Data Processing: NDVI and Tree Canopy. There were two stages of data processing for NDVI and tree canopy. First, we used zonal statistics from the exactextractr package in R version 4.0.2 (Baston 2021; R Core Team 2021) to calculate mean NDVI (250 m) and mean percentage tree canopy (30 m) for each census tract. The exactextractr package allows cells to be partially covered by zones and weighs cell values by the covered area fraction, unlike most other implementations of zonal statistics that only consider each raster cell to be either completely within or outside of overlaid polygon zones. The result is more accurate summaries, particularly for small tracts where a large proportion of cells overlaid by the tract are ones that intersect tract boundaries. To our knowledge, this is also a higher performance implementation than other R methods and makes computations for the entire conterminous United States more tractable.

Then, with the outputs, we calculated the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) of tracts within each climate class for mean NDVI and mean percentage tree canopy. In doing so, we rescaled their values to range from zero to one relative to their climate. This allows green space values to be comparable between climates. For example, the Southeast where it is more temperate is much greener than the Southwest where it is more arid; thus, the Southeast ' s green space will be higher on average than that in the Southwest. By calculating the ECDF within each climate and rescaling green space, the tracts fall along the same scale, and the same value in the Southwest and in the Southeast (e.g., 0.5) are of equal rank relative to their corresponding climate.

Statistical Analysis: Generalized Additive Models. We used generalized additive models (GAMs) from the mgcv package in R to examine the association between percentage disability as the outcome and green space and other covariates as predictors. Disability is our outcome variable because our focus is on the residential environments of disabled residents. For green space, we used the ECDF for mean NDVI and mean percentage tree canopy in separate models. The other covariates include percentage female, percentage non-Hispanic White, percentage employed, median household income, median age, and RUCA. These types of covariates have been used in previous research to examine their associations with disability prevalence (White et al. 2010; Saikua et al. 2016). Except for RUCA (which is a categorical variable), all predictors are nonlinear because their estimated degrees of freedom were much larger than one.

During the concurvity checks, we dropped from the final models a thin plate regression spline for education and for the interaction between the longitude and latitude of the census tract centroid. The latter was intended to be included as a covariate to account for spatial dependence, but there was high concurvity (  0.80) between longitude and latitude and NDVI and tree canopy, indicating that the green space variables account for much of the spatial structure of the data. For the climate GAMs, we also dropped one covariate in three climate classes due to high concurvity.

Cluster Analysis: Colocation Bivariate Local Join Count Statistics. To detect clusters of disability and green space, we generated Anselin and Li ' s (2019) colocation bivariate local join count statistics using GeoDa. This statistic allows us to detect the colocation of observations containing two binary variables with values of one surrounded by other colocated observations (Anselin and Li 2019). Following Anselin and Li (2019), we used 999 permutations and pseudo p value  0.01 for cluster detection.

We sought to identify two types of rare event clusters: high disability and high green space, and high disability and low green space. We coded disability and the green space variables as binary variables. Observations were coded one if disability was  20 percent, as 20 percent approximates the national disability prevalence (U.S. Census Bureau Public Information Office 2016) and the value of the highest decile. Green space values were coded 1 if they were the highest and lowest decile for the ECDF for mean NDVI and mean percentage tree canopy by climate class. Otherwise, observations were coded as 0. We defined neighbors using the queen criterion. Compared to other measures of local spatial autocorrelation that assess values around the mean, a local join count that uses the upper or lower quantile of the distribution in a variable detects clusters with more extreme values (Anselin 2019; Samuels-Kalow et al. 2021).

For the remaining covariates, their associations with disability are extremely similar between both GAMs (Table 2, Figure 1). Census tracts with more disabled residents have a greater share of White and older residents, a smaller share of female and employed residents, and lower median household income. Except for percentage White and median age, the associations between all other covariates and percentage disability are largely negative. Compared to metropolitan areas, micropolitan and rural areas have a higher share of disabled residents.

People with disabilities are more likely to experience socioeconomic disadvantages and poorer health due to restricted access to the labor market and health care (Iezzoni 2011; Wong et al. 2020). These adverse circumstances stem from discrimination against disabled individuals (Kruse et al. 2018) and from the underdevelopment and economic decline of the rural areas where many live (Erickson et al. 2018). Consistent with these established trends, our findings demonstrate that areas with more disabled residents tend to have lower household income and employment.

At the same time, our results reveal the limits of an analysis focused on distributive justice. The areas with more disabled residents also tend to be located in more rural areas, which have more open countryside and are therefore greener. Although green spaces have the potential to improve people ' s health, however, the socioeconomic disadvantages in rural areas might minimize any benefits from green space abundance. Furthermore, it is expected that rural residents with disabilities are likely to find their surrounding green spaces to be inaccessible as prior qualitative studies reveal that socioeconomically disadvantaged rural residents are unlikely to benefit from nearby green spaces despite their proximity to them (Szaboova, Brown, and Fisher 2020) and indicate that people with disabilities experience physical and social barriers to accessing green space (Mathers 2008; Macpherson 2017; Corazon et al. 2019; Li et al. 2019).

## Clusters of Disability and Green Space

Descriptive Patterns. Across the United States, there is spatial heterogeneity in the residential locations of people with disabilities (Figure 4) and in the provision of greenspace (Figure 5). Notably high concentrations of disabled residents (  20 percent) are located in the Southeast and Southwest, as well as along the West Coast and in the states of Maine and Michigan. Most tracts with 30 percent or more of residents with disabilities are in Kentucky and West Virginia. As for green space, there are similar trends between NDVI (Figure 5) and tree canopy (Figure 6). The greenest tracts are concentrated in the East, parts of the West Coast and the South, and in the northern regions of Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Michigan. The least green areas are in the Midwest, Southwest, and in the Mountain states.

Clusters: Geographic Characteristics. There are significant (pseudo p value  0.01) and distinctive clusters of high disability (  20 percent) and high green space (top 10 percent), as well as high disability (  20 percent) and low green space (bottom 10 percent; Figures 7, 8). The green space values for NDVI and tree canopy are the rescaled values using the ECDF of the tract-level mean within each climate class. These clusters represent rare colocations of disability and green space, the census tracts where disabled residents have the highest and lowest coverage of residential greenspace. Clusters of high disability and high green space are largely located in the Southeast and in the states of Maine, Oregon, and Washington. Clusters of high disability and low green space are more geographically dispersed and are more likely to be located in cities, with large rural clusters in California and Nevada.

Compared to clusters of high disability and low green space, clusters of high disability and high greenspace are more rural and cover larger areas in more states (Table 6, Figures 7, 8). None of the clusters of high green space (both NDVI and tree canopy) are located in major cities with a population greater than 100,000 residents. Clusters of high disability and low green space tend to be in more urban areas, and their locations are spread out across the United States. Figure 7 includes the locations of cities that contain clusters of low NDVI and high disability, and Figure 8 maps the cities with clusters of low tree canopy and high disability. More clusters were detected in major cities (  100,000 residents) for low NDVI ( n ¼ 18) than for low tree canopy ( n ¼ 14). Clusters with low NDVI and low tree canopy were detected in eleven cities: Oakland, San Francisco, New Orleans, Detroit, New York, Cleveland, Cincinnati, Toledo, Allentown, Philadelphia, and Memphis.

If rural areas tend to be greener and have more disabled residents, then it is logical that tracts with higher disability are associated with more green space. A greater abundance of green space, however, does not necessarily mean that residents benefit from green spaces, as more ' natural ' spaces are often inaccessible to people with physical disabilities (Macpherson 2017; Corazon et al. 2019). Furthermore, Szaboova, Brown, and Fisher ' s (2020) study on rural green space demonstrates that there are socioeconomic constraints to access. There might be a green space paradox at work in rural communities that differs from that in cities, where urban greening intended to reduce environmental injustice has the pernicious effect of increasing property values, attracting gentrification, and displacing disadvantaged residents (Wolch, Byrne, and Newell 2014; Pearsall and Eller 2020). More investigation in rural areas is needed to understand disabled residents ' experiences with accessing green space.

There is variability by climate type. In temperate and cold climates, there is a positive association between disability and green space, particularly for the greenest tracts. Furthermore, many of these areas are located within the Disability Belt (a multistate region encompassing Appalachia, the Deep South, and extending out west into Missouri and Arkansas), with high proportions of residents who are disabled, poor, underemployed, and receive disability benefits (McCoy, Davis, and Hudson 1994; Wong 2016; Pearsall and Eller 2020). In contrast, the correlation between disability and green space is relatively flat, declining, or not statistically significant in arid and tropical climates.

When comparing NDVI to tree canopy, the associations with disability are largely similar, thus supporting the positive correlation between disability and green space. There are, however, deviations between the two green space metrics at both continental and climate scales. Each metric captures a different aspect of green space: NDVI is a measure of greenness of vegetation, whereas tree canopy focuses on a specific type of vegetation. A major limitation with NDVI is that it captures all vegetation regardless of their utility (James et al. 2015). Meanwhile, tree canopy pertains only to trees, therefore overlooking other types of vegetation that might be relevant to human health (e.g., gardens). Our results reveal more variability between NDVI and tree canopy by climate class than at the continental scale, suggesting that comparisons between different green space metrics are needed at finer scales, as one measure might be more pertinent than the other for some localities. Additionally, diverse health conditions have varied associations with different green space measures (Mears et al. 2020), thus supporting our decision to include and compare different green space metrics in our analyses.

Through our cluster analysis, we identify clusters of high disability (  20 percent) and low and high green space (bottom and top 10 percent, respectively) across the urban -rural continuum and find two levels of disparities. First, we find that all clusters are socioeconomically disadvantaged compared to national median values. Second, clusters of low green space are more socioeconomically disadvantaged than clusters of high green space. Clusters of low green space are located in more urban areas, have lower household income and employment, and are predominantly non-White. The latter results align with previous research in U.S. cities that reveal stark racial and class disparities in green space access (Heynen, Perkins, and Roy 2006; Dai 2011; Duncan et al. 2013; Rigolon 2016). Our findings build on these prior studies by finding inequities at the intersection of disability, race, and class.

For future work on access to urban green spaces, an intersectional approach that considers disability alongside race and class is much needed to examine and address multiple systems of oppression. In our study, we use disability data from the Census Bureau, which identifies disabled individuals, low-income people, and racialized and ethnic minorities as ' hard-to-count ' populations (Lee et al. n.d.). Consequently, disability is more visible and legible among White, higher income populations, as is the case in our results. An intersectional approach thus requires mixed-methods research to generate insights on undercounted and underrepresented communities.

The clusters that we identify might be useful case studies for future research, in particular clusters of low NDVI and low tree canopy, which are located in eleven major cities: Oakland, San Francisco, New Orleans, Detroit, New York, Cleveland, Cincinnati, Toledo, Allentown, Philadelphia, and Memphis. A closer investigation into these localities is likely to generate deeper insights on local green space access and how it affects the well-being of residents with disabilities. This might in turn help local stakeholders prioritize investments in green space infrastructure as a means to enhance residents ' access to green spaces and to improve their overall quality of life. Part of these investments might include cultural competency training among park and recreation professionals and the employment of disabled individuals into these positions to improve the accessibility of green spaces for disabled visitors. On another note, if green space provision is difficult due to perennial weather conditions (e.g., too hot, cold, dry), then other options might need to be considered that are viable for local climates. Alternatives could include the development of accessible greenhouses or other types of indoor spaces, or the renovation of other types of natural spaces for recreational uses that provide benefits similar to green spaces. At the same time, care needs to be taken to ensure that greening strategies benefit local residents and are not followed by speculative development and gentrification as has been the case in some U.S. cities (Wolch, Byrne, and Newell 2014).

Although our study focuses on geographic availability -a vital condition of accessibility -future research is needed to assess multiple, intersecting dimensions of access that go beyond geographic availability, such as acceptability, accommodation, and affordability (Penchansky and Thomas 1981). Research findings suggest that the quality of green spaces available to people living in more socioeconomically disadvantaged areas is low despite its ample provision (Jones, Hillsdon, and Coombes 2009; Rigolon 2016). This indicates that there are qualitative differences between different green spaces and that a greater availability of green spaces does not necessarily increase the benefits people receive from them. Even when green space availability is high, use of these spaces among local residents might be low. Therefore, although people with disabilities have greater green space availability, findings from qualitative studies demonstrate that they might not actually have access to these spaces (Mathers 2008; Macpherson 2017; Corazon et al. 2019; Li et al. 2019). Consequently, they are not gaining the social and health benefits that are supposed to come with green spaces. To evaluate people ' s perceptions and situate their circumstances within local sociopolitical contexts, deeper investigations into specific places are required. Qualitative methods involving field work and primary data collection are needed to examine the underlying processes driving our study ' s geographic patterns. This would allow us to assess the utility and attractiveness of different green spaces, and to identify specific mechanisms for improving their accessibility.

In the United States, there is also a critical need to both adhere to and go beyond ADA compliance. Although the ADA is supposed to enforce accessibility standards for the building and modification of parks and recreational facilities, many recreational green spaces remain inaccessible and in need of renovation (Rimmer et al. 2004; Bobo 2019; Blanks 2020). To many people with disabilities, natural environments are perceived as characteristically inaccessible as they often do not include curb cuts, access ramps, and accessible routes (Rimmer et al. 2004). At the same time, the ADA applies compliance standards for a narrow definition of disability -one that centers around accessibility for those with physical disabilities while overlooking access concerns for those with mental disabilities. It is essential to address the needs of people with physical and mental disabilities in considerations of accessibility to develop conditions for greater physical and social inclusion within green spaces.

Our examination of geographic availability -a requisite for access -quantifies the distribution of residential green space for people with disabilities, detects clusters where the spatial inaccessibility of green space for disabled residents is alarmingly high, and identifies future research directions to better understand disability and green space access. A key strength is that our study analyzes green space and disability distributions across a large geographic extent (over 7.6 million km 2 ) that spans across the urban -rural continuum. Other studies have examined much smaller geographic areas, with a focus on city populations and urban green spaces (Heynen, Perkins, and Roy 2006; Dai 2011; Duncan et al. 2013; Rigolon 2016). Our article provides generalizable findings that build on prior qualitative studies on disability and inaccessible green spaces (Mathers 2008; Macpherson 2017; Corazon et al. 2019; Li et al. 2019). Furthermore, more research is needed on rural experiences (Szaboova, Brown, and Fisher 2020). Urban and rural areas are likely to be different, both quantitatively and qualitatively. Previous work indicated that people with disabilities concentrate more in rural counties than in urban ones (McCoy, Davis, and Hudson 1994; Wong 2016), and our findings confirm similar distributions for census tracts. To expand on our work, there is a need to critically interrogate how green space varies across heterogeneous contexts by investigating the following questions: What resources count as green space? What health-promoting functions do these spaces provide? For whom are they accessible?

Our study has a few limitations related to methods and data. Our use of statistical techniques limits the scope of analysis to distributive justice. More qualitative and mixed-method work is needed to attend to multiple dimensions of environmental justice -such as recognition and procedural justice -and to assess the social processes that condition green space design and access. Another limitation is that we exclude parks from our analysis due to limited coverage of local parks in national data sets. Future research should investigate park data and access in specific localities, and conduct sensitivity analyses, before scaling up to the continental scale. Furthermore, we analyze green space availability within census tracts, thus focusing on geographic proximity and access to green space by residential location. Many individuals might spend some of their time in other places outside their homes, such as their place of employment. As a result, our study represents a portion of people ' s total green space availability. We also examine people with disabilities of all ages. Further investigation is needed on green space access by different age groups, including children and older adults, as needs differ with age. Finally, our data are cross-sectional and capture patterns for one time period (i.e., the year 2010). Future research should assess people ' s green space availability based on their activity spaces to investigate access outside the home, and should undertake longitudinal studies to examine changes over time. Despite these limitations, our findings offer baseline nationwide results for future studies to build on.

As the United States experiences declines in green space availability, it becomes increasingly important to understand how these reductions might affect people with disabilities as well as other marginalized populations. It is particularly vital to center disability in conversations of environmental justice to critically evaluate definitions and implementations of access. Doing so will help improve the designs of recreational green spaces and move us toward the construction of more inclusive spaces.
