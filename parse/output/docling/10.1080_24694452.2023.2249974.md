## Signaling Hinterlands and the Spatial Networks of Digital Capitalism

Jovanna Rosen  and Luis F. Alvarez Le  on †

 Department of Public Policy and Administration, Rutgers University -Camden, USA † Department of Geography, Dartmouth College, USA

This article develops the concept of signaling hinterlands to explain how digital social networks extend and shift the interconnections between a metropolis and its hinterlands. The relationship between metropolis and hinterlands -the multiple, overlapping, networked geographies on which cities rely -is a long-standing area of geographical inquiry. This dynamic reflects networks of densely and more sparsely populated places connected by asymmetric relations, thus far primarily envisioned as physical goods-related production activities. How do these relationships change in a geographical political economy centered around digital technologies? We argue that digital technologies expand the productive capacity of the hinterlands, reshaping the dynamics between places within and beyond the metropolis. With digital social networks and digital platforms, places can gain value through their potential to signal and amass social, cultural, and economic capital. Signaling hinterlands reflect the simultaneously cultural, symbolic, technological, political, and economic relations involved in networking places under digital capitalism. We leverage Google Maps visitor posts to document the interconnections between the San Francisco Bay Area metropolis and Calla Lily Valley, a rural locale that we argue has become a digitally induced hinterland. Mapping postings from users who recorded visits to Calla Lily Valley, we examine how digital traces re-create and alter the asymmetric relations between rural and urban, metropolis and hinterlands in the digital economy. Key Words: digital capitalism, digital growth machine, digital technology, hinterlands, networks.

T his article develops the concept of signaling hinterlands to explain how digital social networks extend and shift the interconnections between a metropolis and its hinterlands. The relationship between metropolis and hinterlands -the multiple, overlapping, networked geographies on which cities rely -is a long-standing area of geographical inquiry. Hinterlands are areas outside the metropolis connected by asymmetric relations to the metropolis, reflecting networks between densely and more sparsely populated places. Thus far, the literature characterizes the activities underlying the metropolis -hinterlands dynamic primarily around physical goods, in terms of production activities (e.g., agriculture, mining) and waste acceptance. How do these relationships change in a geographical political economy centered around digital technologies, where material goods represent only part of the productive capacity of hinterlands areas?

We argue that digital technologies expand the productive mechanisms underlying the hinterlands relationship beyond physical goods, reshaping the networks between places within and beyond the metropolis both in terms of which geographies have value as hinterlands, as well as what resources they offer. With digital social networks and platforms such as Instagram, Yelp, and Google Maps, places gain value through their potential to signal and amass social, cultural, and economic capital under digital capitalism. This dynamic enables a set of activities -and a corresponding set of hinterlands -where production extends beyond physical goods. Signaling hinterlands help elucidate the simultaneously cultural, symbolic, technological, political, and economic relations involved in networking places through digital platforms. We leverage an original social media data set to document the interconnections between the San Francisco Bay Area metropolis and Calla Lily Valley, located on the California central coast. We argue that Calla Lily Valley has become a hinterland enabled by digital technologies. Mapping the relationship between Calla Lily Valley and the Bay Area, we show how digital traces re-create and alter the asymmetric relations between rural and urban, metropolis and hinterlands in the digital economy.

First, we review the digital growth machine concept to trace broader shifts in urban growth and sociospatial impacts under digital capitalism. Next, we present hinterlands literature to show how metropolitan growth has always depended on dispersed geographies to support basic functions, creating spatial networks defined by asymmetric flows of goods, resources, and people. We show how the digital growth machine necessitates new sets of hinterlands to support its distinct activities. We introduce signaling hinterlands to illustrate how digital technologies reshape the productive capacity and visibility of hinterlands areas, introducing new sites with extraction potential to support tech sector activities. We trace the relationship between the Bay Area and Calla Lily Valley, a site that has gained prominence on digital social networks, to describe how digital capitalism reinscribes the hinterlands dynamic while altering hinterlands processes and geographies. Throughout, we show how the sociospatial transformations precipitated by digital capitalism extend beyond metropolitan boundaries. Such transformations necessitate new operational landscapes to sustain the novel consumption activities enabled by digital technologies, linking new geographies into urban-oriented sociospatial networks.

## Platforms and the Digital Growth Machine

Digital capitalism has produced important sociospatial shifts that have transformed cities and solidified the digital technology industry ' s economic influence (Sadowski 2020). Acting in coordinated ways to form what we have termed the ' digital growth machine ' (Rosen and Alvarez Le  on 2022), urban and digital technology sector actors leverage urban and digital capital accumulation possibilities to create new profit opportunities through four interrelated pathways. First, building on long-standing urban growth dynamics, the digital growth machine supports land development and technology sector industrial attraction to pursue urban growth and exchange values. Second, it creates mechanisms for increasing and extracting exchange values beyond traditional land development and intensification. Third, it enables intermediation and new markets. Fourth, the digital growth machine develops new urban digital renderings, such as the interactive maps on Yelp, NextDoor, and Google Maps, which shape land and asset-related value and perceptions of place. These strategies center the digital technology sector in urban governance, rearranging urban physical and digital landscapes (Alvarez Le  on and Rosen 2020; Rosen and Alvarez Le  on 2022).

The digital growth machine perspective illustrates how the tech industry operates not just in cities, but on cities, reshaping urbanism and urban economies as firms and platforms integrate into the everyday materiality of cities (Barns 2020). Thus, the capital accumulation strategies underlying even digitally induced urban growth rely on places and spatial expressions to function. Digital capitalism necessitates and produces new operational landscapes, much like the global urbanizing processes in which it is embedded (Katsikis 2018; Brenner and Katsikis 2020). Digital networks and platforms mediate this process: Even as actors operate on platforms such as Yelp and Google Local Guides, their reviews reflect physical world activities (visits, experiences, recommendations) translated into digital renderings, mapped onto concrete locations, which mediate how people experience places. As the tech industry has become a dominant cornerstone of economies under contemporary capitalism (Sadowski 2020), the quest for new places and spaces from which to extract capital now operates through technology firms and across both physical and digitized landscapes (Greene and Joseph 2015; Rosen and Alvarez Le  on 2022). Although the digital growth machine emphasizes urban processes and sociospatial outcomes, the industry ' s success and the activities undertaken on digital platforms require spatial expressions and uses beyond urban cores. Because cities exist and grow in relation to the hinterlands -geographies affected by these spatial network ties and urban activities -we must understand how the digital both expresses and remakes the metropolis -hinterlands relationship.

Digital social media platforms offer a lens to explore the changing nature of metropolis -hinterlands relations. These platforms function through user-generated content oriented around the activity of signaling, whereby users share activities, information, and visual imagery via digital networks, building a particular image of themselves and their lives. Signaling concerns ' things one does that are visible and that are in part designed to communicate '

(Spence 2002, 434). As sociologist Gambetta (2011) explained, ' signals are the stuff of purposive communication. Signals are any observable feature of an agent that are intentionally displayed for the purpose of altering the probability the receiver assigns to a certain state of affairs or ' event '' (xv, italics added). Digital platforms like Yelp, Instagram, Snapchat, and Google Maps channel signals: Users share content to communicate myriad messages to their networks, from information about their lives to knowledge about a particular place or topic. As digital social platforms have become increasingly prominent, social-media-based entrepreneurial activities, like influencing, have become viable careers for prominent users, who monetize their brand, lifestyle, or expertise through advertising revenue.

The logic of signaling via digital platforms fundamentally reflects the varying affordances of social, cultural, and economic capital. Bourdieu (1986, 15) famously identified multiple forms of capital (defined as ' accumulated labor ' ) beyond economic capital (money). Social capital emerges from the resources that do or could exist through social connections. Cultural capital reflects culturally valued, symbolic attributes, including goods, personal characteristics, and qualifications (e.g., educational degrees or nobility). Importantly, the different, nonmonetary capital types can be converted into economic capital. Therefore, social connections and cultural status have value that can generate money; social structure and networks shape economic outcomes (Bourdieu 1986; Granovetter 2005). Digital social media platforms represent a powerful means for users to amass and demonstrate social and cultural capital: Users leverage their social capital on the platform (connections and views) to signal cultural capital -from attending an event to sharing photos from an exclusive destination. Successful users can translate these capital forms into economic capital, either directly, through advertising revenue, or indirectly, through accumulated status derived from signaling activities, which can also translate into social status offline. These activities can attract additional followers -thus enhancing a user ' s social capital -reinforcing existing status in a positive feedback loop.

Although platforms differ in the types of influencing they inspire or enable, and in their relationship to place, they similarly enact spatial transformations and require concrete places in which to operate.

Users seek new places and activities from which to generate content that, in turn, can help them gain prominence on social media platforms and in their networks. Altogether, places can create, reinforce, or amplify the signals undertaken on social media platforms. Therefore, at least some activities on social media platforms are not only spatial, but hinge on specific geographies, enrolling places in the social networks inhabited by platform users. These activities can transform the places themselves. Studies have examined how social media content is coproduced with concrete places, focused on urban settings (Boy and Uitermark 2017). As Degen and Rose (2022) argued, urban experiences of space and time are reconfigured through the intensive use of social media platforms like Instagram, driving the emergence of what they called ' the new urban aesthetic. ' Yet, the digital growth machine sweeps vast geographies into its capital accumulation processes, which suggests that the metropolis and hinterland relationship transforms through digitization. We turn to this question next.

## Hinterlands as Operational Landscapes of the Digital Growth Machine

Prior research conceptualizes the relationship between the metropolis and the nonmetropolitan places on which it depends as its ' hinterlands, ' which Brenner and Katsikis (2020) defined as ' the variegated non-city spaces that are swept into the maelstrom of urbanisation, whether as supply zones, impact zones, sacrifice zones, logistics corridors or otherwise ' (24). Framed as hinterlands, nonmetropolitan areas can serve as landscapes for extraction to support urban functions. In this way, the metropolis and its hinterlands are linked through spatial networks across which capital, goods, resources, and people flow. As metropolitan regions have grown in size and complexity, so, too, have their hinterlands (Cronon 1991; Aguilar, Ward, and Smith 2003; Billen, Garnier, and Barles 2012; Brenner and Katsikis 2020).

The hinterlands perspective highlights how urban growth necessitates ' operational landscapes '-disperse geographies that support the metropolis. Hinterlands have historically served two essential functions: first, to provide resources and produce goods for metropolitan consumption (agriculture, resource manufacturing, and resource extraction), and second, to accept urban outputs (waste disposal). These functions reshape the social, spatial, and political characteristics of the hinterlands, creating broad transformations defined by asymmetrical relationships and extraction. Under globalization and enabled by technological advancement, the hinterlands are less restricted to a regional extent, but can extend to a planetary reach given global supply chains and capital flows. Altogether, the hinterlands and their productive capacities underlie urban growth and metropolitan activities -including, we argue, the digital growth machine (Cronon 1991; Billen, Garnier, and Barles 2012; Katsikis 2018; Brenner and Katsikis 2020).

The hinterlands -metropolis relationship follows a broader recognition in geographical debates that urban dynamics rely on dispersed and shifting geographies to support growth. Among these, Harvey ' s (2001) ' spatial fix ' concept suggests that, in response to potential overaccumulation and to support new profit-seeking, capital must continually enroll new geographies. The spatial fix is necessary to advance the overall capitalist project of the constant pursuit of capital accumulation. These dynamics, which frequently reflect activities originating from urban cores, redefine places into operational landscapes based on their potential to serve as new capital accumulation sites. Therefore, as the character and spatial organization of a political economic system changes, areas outside of urban cores can come to play different roles in a network of human settlement and economic activities. For instance, as Gilmore (1999) has shown, in moments of crises, (racial) capitalism turns to new forms of spatial fix, which in the United States over recent decades drove state-led development of a prison industrial complex that has explicitly targeted nonurban geographies.

The rise of the technology industry as a prominent economic and urban force suggests new operational landscapes for capital accumulation tailored to this industry ' s needs. Greene and Joseph (2015) theorized the ' digital spatial fix ' to demonstrate how digital technologies ' change the geography of capitalism ' by creating virtual spaces that provide ' new sites of accumulation [and] … new spaces of exploitation ' (223 -24). Digital technologies expand geographies of extraction into virtual spaces, even as virtual interactions shape connections and behavior in the physical world, from dating applications to social media. As the authors noted, ' the old rules still apply even though the methods have advanced ' (Greene and Joseph 2015, 224). Even as economic geography shifts under digital and platform capitalism, capitalism continues to depend on extraction geographies and associated spatial patterns.

## Signaling Hinterlands

The rise of digital technologies and platforms, including digital social media, necessitate operational landscapes that extend beyond activities related to physical commodities. Rather, these platforms are fundamentally designed for users to leverage, cultivate, and signal both cultural and social capital, through user connections. Users produce cultural capital through aesthetics, visual imagery, and the perceptions fostered of the users -whether as experts of a particular place (e.g., on Google Local Guides and Yelp, where users review places) or as users evoking influence through imagery and activities (e.g., on TikTok and Instagram; Tarr and Alvarez Le  on 2019; Payne 2021). Social media platforms and the cultural activities and avatars they create, like ' digital nomads, ' sit at the intersection of aesthetics and materiality (Bozzi 2020). In turn, places become redefined by the intersection of their position within spatial networks (e.g., metropolis -hinterland), social networks (e.g., popular destinations on Instagram), and cultural significance.

Places and geographies thus provide the substrate for the myriad signaling activities that occur through digital social media platforms. Physical sites integrally shape the functions and economic logics of platforms like Yelp and Google Maps, as users signal about places in different ways (from restaurant reviews to stories about tourist destinations). Simultaneously, as users build multimedia discursive stock about places, they also leverage their own participation in digital platforms to gain credibility, display altruism, or demonstrate expertise. On platforms such as TikTok, Snapchat, and Instagram, geotagging illustrates how places are often instrumental to any status that users aim to cultivate. Combined with hashtags, geotagging enables users to converse with each other, establish brands, and gain attention, while inherently tying their activities and visual imagery to a particular place -using location to signal cultural capital. Discussing digital nomads and status signaling activities on Instagram, Bozzi (2020) explained, ' the necessary reliance on location-based apps to gain information while abroad makes [geotagging] materially enmeshed in the collective cultural production that shapes the digital nomad as a cultural avatar … [the app] thus also plays a role in materializing a type of geography of tourism ' (6 -7). In this way, activities aimed to build cultural capital on platforms frequently specifically leverage the status of places to afford users status on the platform. Yet, as Crampton et al. (2013) suggested, focusing on activities like geotagging can reveal some geographic patterns while obscuring other social and spatial processes. Therefore, spatialized expressions of social media must be studied as part of broader geographic dynamics -a reality that we highlight through our focus on the metropolis -hinterland relationship.

These digital activities that relate to place, like geotagging on social media, can generate network effects that transform places, however, leading to outcomes like overuse of specific sites. Knepper (2017) traced the overuse of Horseshoe Bend, a now-popular tourist spot near the Grand Canyon, to geotagging. As a result of its popular depiction through geotags, the location experienced a sharp increase in tourism within a five-year span, from 1,000 visitors a year to more than 4,000 daily visitors by 2017. The related environmental degradation from such intensive tourism has sparked critics to decry the practice of geotagging specific locations (Knepper 2017).

The cumulative effect of digital social media on specific places, then, re-creates certain sites as hinterlands of the tech industry -physically separate from urban cores but intrinsically tied to their functions through signaling activities: signaling hinterlands . These activities shift tourism toward a site-specific extractive practice where particular places enable users to gain prominence and influence in digital networks, connecting to other users engaged or interested in similar activities. Altogether, this shift has implications for the sites themselves -demonstrated by Horseshoe Bend -as well as for the metropolis -hinterland extractive dynamic that has long connected densely and more sparsely populated places through networked activities of exchange.

Digitization processes shift not just the geographic extent of the hinterlands, but also the resources and capital forms underlying this relationship. Signaling hinterlands reveal how many nonurban places become swept into the centripetal dynamics of the digital growth machine, simultaneously functioning as amenities for users relying on technology platforms to build cultural cache, tourism destinations for the tech and urban elite, and capital accumulation sites for technology firms and actors intertwined in this industry. These effects are exacerbated by the tendencies and norms of digital platforms, where users skew toward positive and ' sanitized ' depictions, and favor unique, local places that enhance an individual ' s online image (Boy and Uitermark 2017). Altogether, therefore, digital landscapes do not reflect the full characteristics of a place, but a version that is simultaneously shaped by the affordances of digital platforms and the signaling intents of users -whether as a recreation space, a cultural or social capital signal, or other activity.

At a macro level, the networked, online presence of places on digital platforms operates to elevate some places over others, furthering uneven development dynamics (Castells 2010; Boy and Uitermark 2017). Through this process, digital technologies can help place some sites ' on the map ' while rendering others increasingly invisible -exemplifying the signaling operations and effects of these platforms. These dynamics differentiate places based on their varying value and utility to the tech sector, including technology-valued cultural, physical, and other amenity assets. This process drives economic functions that relate to, but differ from, traditional tourism: It is focused on one industry, centered in places that have prestige within and are accessible to that industry, its workers, and users, and occurs both in physical space and across digital domains. Hinterland spaces now play a key role in producing ' content ' for the tech industry as well as recreation possibilities -re-creating places outside the metropolis into playgrounds, reshaping their economies, driving entirely new markets, and straining local infrastructure from housing to transportation. The digital traces of these activities drive further consumption, altering how people experience places (Boy and Uitermark 2017).

Next, we explore this dynamic by examining the emerging signaling hinterlands relationship between the San Francisco Bay Area and Calla Lily Valley.

## Calla Lily Valley as a Signaling Hinterland

The greater Monterey Peninsula encompasses an exurban and rural area on the central California coast roughly 120 miles south of San Francisco, making the region an easy weekend trip from the heart of Silicon Valley. The region has long been a tourist destination famous for the golf courses of Pebble Beach, the Monterey Bay Aquarium, the picturesque beaches and towns including Carmelby-the-Sea, and the iconic Highway 1 road trip along the Big Sur coastline, one of the most famous highway stretches in the world. Given its famous and legible imagery, and its proximity to the Bay Area, the region has experienced significant changes alongside tech industry growth, inspiring new attention. These dynamics have not just fed activity into long-standing, prominent attractions like the aquarium. Rather, the area ' s visibility on digital social media platforms and the search for new places to ' discover ' appears to have changed both how people recreate and where they visit, creating new linkages between the metropolis and regional locales.

In this article, we examine this dynamic by focusing on Calla Lily Valley, located along the Big Sur coast, a remote, ninety-mile coastline beginning just south of Carmel-by-the-Sea. Calla Lily Valley refers to a small canyon along Garrapata Beach where calla lilies bloom in the spring, fed by a creek that runs to the Pacific Ocean (see Figure 1). Notably, Calla Lily Valley is not an obvious stop along Highway 1 through Big Sur; unlike famous landmarks such as the Bixby Bridge, it is not easily visible from the road and it is accessed via a short walk from an unmarked highway shoulder. The site, however, has recently become a prominent, crowded detour, now geolocated on Google Maps, Apple Maps, and the official Tesla map, featured in Instagram reels, and popularized through social media images (see Figure 2). TikTok videos of Calla Lily Valley reached 31.5 million views as of November 2022 -even as the site is not well featured in the popular press and the Big Sur coastline largely lacks cell service. 1

Calla Lily Valley illustrates how social media platforms can transform a place into a new hinterland, in this case closely linked to the Bay Area. On 10 November 2022, the Instagram hashtag #callalilyvalley yielded 2,085 posts, the most prominent of which were closeups of flowers, scenic landscapes, couples photography, pet photography, and ' inspirational ' content, such as quotes accompanying the photos.

Whereas Instagram is a photography-centered platform, Google Maps centers its mapping functions, fed by user activities such as reviews, photos, and other volunteered content linked to places on the map. As of mid-November 2022, Calla Lily Valley had 176 reviews on Google Maps and a 4.7-star rating. Google Maps highlighted key terms associated with this place according to the number of reviews: flowers (twentyfour), beach (nineteen), hike (eight), lilies (seven), stream (six), photos (five), drive (five), waves (three), march (three), and gem (three).

The number of photos linked to Calla Lily Valley was not reported, but many photos in the reviews show a similar type of content to Instagram, whereas other photos depict points of reference and directions. Unlike Instagram, which is more aesthetically oriented, photo quality on Google Maps reviews varies widely. Google Maps, however, provides a unique opportunity to explore the spatial networks that are shaped and remade through social media. To conduct this analysis, we collected all the reviews of Calla Lily Valley on Google Maps as of 11 November 2022. This Web scrape yielded 176 reviews. We highlight three key patterns from these reviews, which can be read as hinterland parallels to the social-media-enabled spatiotemporal transformations identified by Degen and Rose (2022) in their examination of a ' new urban aesthetic. '

First, the temporality of the images reveals growing interest in Calla Lily Valley: Before November 2020 it had only six reviews, but this number quickly grew to eighty-four reviews by November 2021, and then to 174 reviews by November 2022. This increased attention supports the interpretation that Calla Lily Valley gained sudden, widespread prominence through social media, although not necessarily in traditional news media or even local coverage. Second, as Figure 3 shows, postings exhibit a distinct seasonality, a pattern that reflects the natural characteristics of the site: The ephemeral nature of a valley of lilies blooming from roughly January to April offers a prime opportunity for generating social media content.

Finally, the spatial patterns of Calla Lily Valley reviewers reflect both the traditional metropolis -hinterland dynamic and the distant geographies visible through digital social networks. Because the locations of users who posted reviews of Calla Lily Valley were not available on Google Maps, we collected the locations of the top ten photos with most views posted by each reviewer. This empirical approach offers a window into the network of places from which these users are signaling as well as the places and postings that receive the most attention in each user ' s respective networks. As Figures 4 and 5 show, the largest concentration of these photos is linked to areas surrounding the San Francisco Bay Area, followed by Los Angeles County and Southern California. These patterns suggest not only that users seek new places from which to generate social media content, but that the signaling performed from those places is simultaneously tied into deeply entrenched metropolis -hinterlands dynamics and connected to broader (increasingly digitized) networks in which goods, people, capital, and information circulate.

## Signaling Uneven Development

Calla Lily Valley exemplifies how social media platforms inspire users to undertake digitally enabled signaling practices in particular places, drawing these geographies into networks of production between metropolis and hinterlands. Such signaling reveals information about places, but also about platforms, including how platforms are intertwined with spatial and economic patterns and processes such as metropolis -hinterlands dynamics. Under digital capitalism, hinterlands spaces have come to support new ways to produce economic and noneconomic capital, including ' content ' for the tech industry and the urban-oriented digital growth machine that the industry powers. Thus, digital platforms necessitate new sets of operational landscapes that manifest as hinterlands, which differ from previous forms in both location -which places have signaling value -and in the valued resource -in this case, the signaling effect. Throughout, these activities produce social and spatial transformations in the places enmeshed in these digitally mediated sociospatial networks, driving uneven development.

Further, the signaling hinterlands concept suggests important insights into the functioning of the digital growth machine, the ways in which it generates economic and noneconomic capital, and the sociospatial outcomes produced. The emergence and entrenchment of signaling hinterlands illustrate how the four pathways of the digital growth machine can operate in intersecting and reinforcing ways to secure growth, capturing new places, actors, and activities in the search for capital (Rosen and Alvarez Le  on 2022). Signaling hinterlands reflect the emergence of digital social media platforms that now intermediate social interactions, feeding value back into the platforms and digital renderings of place (promoting the third and fourth pathways). Further, the digital traces of Calla Lily Valley invite tourism by technology-oriented actors and enhance the region ' s cultural prestige. These changes drive economic development through economic spillovers and local growth, increasing the exchange values of extra-urban land in the process (supporting the first and second pathways). Indeed, the prevalence of short-term rentals on platforms like Airbnb -which cater directly to the types of visitors drawn by signaling hinterlands and who use digital social media platforms -has inspired local regulations to address related housing market impacts in nearby cities like Carmel-by-the-Sea.

Taken together, these pathways operate to enable and entrench the digital growth machine within and beyond urban areas. The economic spillovers and multiple capital forms produced by social intermediation and enabled by digital renderings directly feed local growth and land-related exchange values, contribute to local tax bases, support commerce, and create new opportunities for extracting profit from land and places. Simultaneously, signaling hinterlands fuel the digital growth machine by enabling the operations and value of tech giants, re-creating places and social dynamics in the image of tech workers and platform users, and driving uneven development. Understanding metropolis -hinterland relationships through processes of signaling across digital social networks thus helps elucidate how both city and country are being re-created via digital platforms to generate economic and other capital forms, extending digital growth machine dynamics into new operational landscapes.
