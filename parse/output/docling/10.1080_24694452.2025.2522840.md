## Resituating Urban Geopolitics: Connections and Frontiers

James D. Sidaway

Department of Geography, National University of Singapore, Singapore

There has been a surge in references to urban geopolitics over the last twenty-plus years. Reviewing claims that warfare has urbanized, however, yields questions about the delineation and frontiers of the urban. The multiple meanings and definitions of ' geopolitics ' and ' urban ' beckon a broadening range of sites and an analytical deepening of urban geopolitics. To these ends, reviewing and seeking to develop the field in conceptual terms, the article revisits rural -urban interactions and reflections from selected African and Asian sites. The overall aim of the article is not to delimit urban geopolitics, but to deepen and expand agendas, broadening the range of cases that inform these. This also invites deeper geopolitical engagement with literature on extended and planetary urbanization. Key Words: cities, critical geopolitics, planetary urbanization, revolutions.

C lassically, geopolitics, as it emerged over a century ago, has focused on the global, the imperial, and the national stages. Frequently, too, classical geopolitics invokes natural physical entities of land and sea, terrain, and relief, such as the ways that Hungarian geopolitical discourse took the Carpathian basin as a reference point (Balogh 2021). Organic understandings, enfolded from the outset as in Rudolf Kjell  en ' s ' The State as a Living Organism ' (Holdar 1992; Tunander 2001), evolved in the hands of right-wing nationalists and fascist regimes from the late 1930s. Similar subscriptions developed enduring focal points in Latin American military geopolitics, reflected in notions of the Left as a cancer to be eliminated from the body-politic, as in the widely promoted geopolitical narratives of the Chilean dictator Augusto Pinochet (Hepple 1992).

The development of critical geopolitics since the 1980s deconstructed and denaturalized such national and imperial discourses (  O Tuathail 1996) while positing alternative foci. Among other scales and sites, references to urban geopolitics have proliferated. As Bialasiewicz (2016) summed up:

Over the past decade, the term ' urban geopolitics ' has entered the policy as well as academic lexicon. In an oped piece published in the summer of 2012, Saskia Sassen prognosticated the emergence of a new sort of ' urban geopolitics, ' noting that from tackling environmental

Although a valuable review of geopolitics and urban armed conflict in Latin America (Demarest 1995) had earlier surveyed urban counterinsurgency texts and revisited the literature on urban guerrilla warfare, it was Graham (2004b) who specified urban geopolitics as ' the telescoping connections between transnational geopolitical transformations and highly local acts of violence against urban sites ' (191). The wider expansion of references to urban geopolitics is relatively recent. A Research Companion to Critical Geopolitics (Dodds, Kuus, and Sharp 2013) published just over a decade ago contains neither chapter heading nor index references to ' urban, ' or ' cities, ' and a Google Ngram search reveals just how steep the growth of work citing ' urban geopolitics ' has been (Figure 1).

The surge in references to urban geopolitics has been associated with claims that warfare has increasingly urbanized. This has been reflected in evolving U.S. military doctrines (Danielsson 2025), notwithstanding some countervailing tendencies (Cheikhali 2025). In related terms, especially with the destruction of Gaza since October 2023 bearing dreadful witness, there have been renewed discussions about urbicide -as detailed in the next section. Yet as the article goes on to consider, there are extensive histories of warfare in cities and of cities becoming targets. Moreover, much violent conflict remains beyond towns and cities, as considered in this article, with vignettes that draw on literatures about conflicts in the Central African Republic (CAR) and Myanmar. The article proposes that rather than referring to a straightforward urbanization of conflict, there is considerable analytical value in foregrounding urban -rural connections. Claims about the urbanization of conflict therefore connect with thorny questions about the delineation and frontiers of the urban. This article considers how multiple meanings and definitions of geopolitics, and the urban, might beckon a broadening range of sites and conceptual deepening of urban geopolitics.

In terms of the overall structure of the arguments, the next section describes how urban geopolitics has evolved and what are the main foci. Seeking to develop the field in conceptual terms, the article considers Maoism ' s urban geopolitics prior to the reflections from other selected African and Asian sites, while addressing urban -rural geopolitical interconnections. Such a relational perspective offers insights that go beyond reference to the urbanization of warfare, to foreground connections beyond cities. In turn, this invites deeper engagements about the definitions of the urban and hence with literatures on extended -or planetary -urbanization.

Clearly there are other pathways that might be taken beyond those charted here, such as to develop feminist approaches to urban geopolitics -some templates, from research in Palestine, Turkish Kurdistan, and South Asia, can be found in Clark (2013), Mustafa et al. (2019), Natanel (2016), and Truelove and Ruszczyk (2022). The article is not intended as that last or definitive word, however, and inevitably it can only offer a partial review and program. The overall aim of this article is therefore not to delimit urban geopolitics, but to review and reframe agendas.

## Parameters of Urban Geopolitics

According to Spanu (2023) the turn to urban geopolitics means ' examining the connection between transnational geopolitical transformations, the production of space, and acts of violence at the urban scale ' (2). Describing urban geopolitics as ' a heterogeneous interdisciplinary engagement, ' Rokem and Boano (2023, 1671), noted, however, that ' generally it is not a theory-based approach -there is no ' urban geopolitical grand theory or theories '' (1672). Although chasing a grand theory might not be helpful, the stage is certainly ripe for careful examination and development of empirical and theoretical horizons.

Taking stock a few years earlier, Rokem et al. (2017) discerned two strands of urban geopolitics: work on ' militarization of urban space , surveillance and security ' and ' research … focused more specifically on urban conflicts within ethno-nationally contested cities, especially in relation to the role of planning and architecture ' (253, italics in original). To this, we might add military history at the urban scale (e.g., Keogh 2019) and other historical work on cities as stages or protagonists in wider imperial, national, and geopolitical dramas, but where the city becomes the narrative focus. Examples include Koch ' s (2018) study of the ' spectacular urbanism ' positioning Kazakhstan ' s capital Astana (previously Nur-Sultan), and other political projects of Baku, Ashgabat, Abu Dhabi, Doha, Naypyidaw, and Bandar Seri Begawan. Single-city studies such as Mazower (2003) on Thessaloniki (Salonica), McKay (2022) on Berlin, Cochrane (2023) on Belfast, Reill (2020) on Rijeka (Fiume), Cronon (1991) on Chicago, and AlSayyad (2013) on Cairo, are among many others that frame their narrative through the city ' s histories rather than national or imperial foci; with the city as a compelling historiographical focus. In these terms, too, writing of Ukrainian cities, Schl € ogel (2018) argued that:

One can ' read cities, ' decrypt them as textures and palimpsests, uncover their strata in a kind of urban archeology, in order to make their past speak. Cities are documents of the first order, and they can be parsed and decoded. In the perspective I propose -as an alternative to the macrocosmic-global and microcosmic views -cities then reveal themselves to be the points in which spaces of history and historical experience attain their maximum density. (14)

Indeed, another strand of urban geopolitics focuses on cities and city-regions as geopolitical actors (Jonas and Moisio 2018; Moisio 2018), building on earlier work on the theme by Sassen (2001) and others. In turn, the political agency and power of cities in complicating ' international ' politics (Jerrems 2020; Acuto et al. 2023; Martinez and Bunnell 2024; Pejic et al. 2024) -what Cobbett and Mason (2021), drawing on the case of Djibouti, called their ' worlding strategy '-offer further evolving points of departure or analysis. Hence, a growing literature on city diplomacy. Moreover, Robin and Acuto (2018) pointed out, cities and their global networks emerge ' as a process of reframing and rescaling existing understandings of the global. In that sense, the question of urban knowledge production -especially that of urban data creation -is an essentially geopolitical one. However, insights from critical geopolitics have been rarely used in current debates on global urban policy and urban data politics ' (76). At the same time, cites as sites for interstate diplomacy -such as the urban geographies of embassies and international organizations -merit critical scrutiny. This goes beyond the location of embassies, to consider ' What architectural forms do they take? How are international organizations accommodated? How does all of this relate to wider geographies of clustering, gentrification, and securitization? ' (Mamadouh et al. 2015, 565). In related terms, ' architectural gifts ' from one country to another, be they symbolic buildings (e.g., stadiums) or more mundane (e.g., housing or hospitals), are, as Stanek and Richard (2024), noted, also ' potent mechanisms of geopolitical reshuffling, premised on an uneven power relation between giver and receiver. '

Each of these vectors of urban geopolitics links to diverse literature across architecture and planning studies, political geography, peace and conflict studies, and histories of sectarian and national conflicts. These literatures are too expansive to review in full here, but examples include the collection by Bollens (1998); the material surveyed by B  adescu (2023), Elfversson et al. (2023), and Rokem and Boano (2023); and further Atun and Doratli (2009), Bou Akar (2018), Calame and Charlesworth (2009), Gentile (2019), Mills (2017), Pullan and Baillie (2013), Shtern and Bollens (2023), and Shtern and Yacobi (2019). A key early antecedent was Boal ' s (1969) work on urban territory and segregation in Belfast at the outset of the violent conflict in Northern Ireland that became known as The Troubles (c. 1968 -1998). The events of 11 September 2001 (hereinafter 9/11) and the ' war on terror, ' however, subsequently focused much Anglophone 1 urban geopolitics -as in the titling of a landmark compilation on Cities, War, and Terrorism: Towards an Urban Geopolitics (Graham 2004a), although chapters in the book also examined Berlin, U.S. cities during the Cold War, and Sarajevo.

Of course, there are deep colonial antecedents, as explored in Saberi ' s (2019) interpretation of security strategies directed at ' preventing radicalism ' in European cities as an urban geopolitical question: ' one that is the relational outcome of the assumed ungovernability of the Other at the urban and international scales and which blurs spaces of liberal peace and war ' (1). Insofar as Israeli security policy has informed the U.S. (and its allies ' ) strategies in the war on terror, Palestine often features in urban geopolitics. Weizman ' s (2007) study of the architecture of (Israeli) occupation has become a benchmark. The Russian attacks on Ukrainian cities since February 2022 had already reanimated references to urbicide (Mezentsev and Mezentsev 2022), characterized by Campbell, Graham, and Monk (2007) as ' the killing of cities. ' The Israeli bombardments that have devastated Gaza since October 2023 have led to further references to urbicide, although some had earlier referred to ' asymmetric ' and ' slow ' urbicides there and elsewhere in Palestine (Graham 2002; Gola  nska 2022; Tayeh 2022) and reflecting on Gaza, Chiodelli (2022) described urban ruination ' not as collateral damage, but as an objective in and of itself ' (2). Ramadan (2009) similarly interpreted urbicide as a conflation of scales, in which an urban target as a whole becomes a ' space of exception ' that stands in for a political target (combatants or the enemy). Abujidi (2014) referred to urbicide as ' a holistic discourse that encompasses the destruction of the entire urban setting (fabric, tissue, infrastructure, landscape and so on) and its entire built and spatial network ' (39).

The destruction in Gaza has also been called domicide (Wintour 2023), although that term had earlier been used to refer to the destruction of homes by a range of agencies (e.g., the construction of dams) in addition to armed conflict and colonial conquest (Porteous and Smith 2001). The terms have also been applied to the incendiary bombing of Japanese cities in World War II (Fedman and Karacas 2012) and in reference to how postconflict urban reconstruction might involve further destruction of homes and attendant community erasures (Coward 2009; Haugbolle 2011; Fregonese 2020; Azzouz 2023), as well as to other twentieth-century aerial bombing campaigns (Hewitt 2015), building on earlier accounts of ' place annihilation ' (Hewitt 1983), and to the forcible emptying of Cambodian cities (which, however, saw their physical infrastructure largely preserved) in 1975 (Tyner et al. 2014). Elsewhere, urbicide has been approached through the lens of Black geographies and histories of antiBlackness. McKittrick (2011) remarked:

Many discussions are primarily concerned with the militarization of, and damages to, urban space in Europe, Africa, and the Middle East. Yet blackness and black geographies in the Americas are not wholly absent from analyses of place annihilation. Indeed, references within urbicide literatures to the ' global south, ' ' planet of slums, ' gentrification, white flight, the inner city, uprisings of the 1960s, and the ' wretched of the earth ' provide a glimpse of how black cultures in the Americas are both embedded within analyses of urbicide and shaped by urbicidal acts. (951)

An expanded perspective was developed, too, by Herscher (2007), writing about Pristina, Kosovo, describing how urbicide took place ' not only during war, but also before war (under the name of ' modernization ' ) and after war (under the name of ' reconstruction ' ) ' (62). In related terms, Berman also used urbicide in describing how the massive ' slum clearance ' and highway construction from the 1950s through the 1970s that dissected the South Bronx yielded displacement and ruination that he explicitly compared to the rubble of war in post-1945 Europe (Berman 1996) and the aftermath of 9/11 (Berman 2014). Decades earlier, however, the New York Times architecture critic Huxtable (1972) had used the term in a similar way. Notwithstanding the 9/11 context in contending configurations as an annex to foreign policy and security studies (Coaffee 2021), urban geopolitics therefore represents a ' heterogenous corpus of literature, ' although, as Fregonese (2018) went on to note, tending to cite ' a handful of case studies (most often in Israel/Palestine) where militarism and warfare constitute the prism through which the urban is made sense of ' (xiii).

Augmenting the theoretical basis for urban geopolitics and approaching the urban as complex and multidimensional phenomena, the focus in what follows is on interactions and relations of urban sites with wider networks. This raises a series of analytical questions about relationships between the urban and wider territories, whereby, ' Urbanization encompasses a much broader set of territories, not only dense agglomerations, their proximate hinterlands, and their long distance networks of connectivity, but also a great diversity of territories ' beyond the city '' (Schmid 2023, 27). In so doing, this furthers the scope for a deeper engagement between urban geopolitics and literatures on extended (sometimes, scripted as planetary) urbanization, augmenting both fields. In what follows, the article first reexamines urban geopolitics through the lens of twentieth-century revolutions, starting with the key Chinese template. The article then draws on selected African and Asian case studies to reconsider claims about the urbanization of warfare that have accompanied the development of urban geopolitics. The focus here is the nexus of urban -rural connections, which in turn articulate with wider debates about the scales and frontiers of urbanization. Such empirical and analytical moves yield resituated urban geopolitics, attendant to diverse historical trajectories and wider urban and social theory.

## Surrounding the Cities from the Countryside

Although there are other examples of nationalist and conflict discourses that invoke the rural as a reservoir of national purity and attendant sacrifice, or conversely as contamination for a cosmopolitan city, such as those described regarding the wars accompanying the collapse of Yugoslavia by Jansen (2005) and Simmons (2001), one of most significant forms of geostrategic thought on city -countryside relations was born in twentieth-century China. The notion of the countryside surrounding the cities in a conflict was developed in the context of China ' s civil war and struggle against Japanese imperialism culminating in the communist victory on the Chinese mainland and Mao ' s proclamation of the People ' s Republic in Tiananmen Square on 1 October 1949. As the British historian of China Bickers (2017) characterized it:

What was most striking about the new state was its rural foundations: a peasant revolution, led by a [former] peasant had complete control over the world ' s most populous country. No other such state existed or had existed in world history … the ' Shanghai-ization ' of China -the spread of hybrid urban culture from the coastal cities -should be countered, for it was complicit in China ' s fall and was demeaning. The ' humiliation ' visited on China by imperialism could be cleansed by the authentic, wholesome culture of rural China. (293 -94)

The subsequent shift in China ' s trajectory in the later part of the twentieth century became especially legible in terms of urban -rural relations. Visser (2010) stated that ' there is no question that cities increasingly envelop the countryside, not only geographically and demographically (40% urbanization in 2005) but in terms of cultural influence ' (3). Subsequently, China ' s cities and extended urban networks are being envisioned on a planetary scale via the Belt and Road Initiative (Williams, Robinson, and Bouzarovski 2020; Bunnell 2021; Mayer and Zhang 2021; Zheng et al. 2023; Curtis and Klaus 2024). Visser (2010) prefaced his book with the text of the announcement issued in 1976 on the death of Chairman Mao, enshrining his historic mandate ' that the seizure of political power by armed force in China could be achieved only by following the road of building rural base areas, using the countryside to encircle the cities and finally seizing the cities, and not by any other road ' (1). A key enunciation of the countryside surrounding the cities materialized in the mid-1960s, however, when visions of guerrilla struggle led by China ' s example were projected onto a world stage. As Adie (1972) narrated:

It is significant that the first succinct Chinese enunciation of the concept of the world countryside surrounding the world cities was made by the former mayor of Peking, P ' eng Chen, at Djakarta in May 1965. He quoted a passage from D. N. Aidit ' s ' Set afire the banteng spirit! Ever forward, no retreat ' : ' On a world scale, Asia, Africa and Latin America are the village of the world, while Europe and North America are the town of the world. If the world revolution is to be victorious, there is no other way than for the world proletariat to give prominence to the revolutions in Asia, Africa and Latin America, that is to say, revolutions in the village of the world. ' (21 -22)

D. N. Aidit was then leader of the Partai Komunis Indonesia (PKI) -which would be violently repressed later that year -and his ' piece on the Banteng (wild buffalo) spirit [was] a play on the usual clich  e Bandung Spirit ' (Adie 1972, 22) invoking the Third Worldist aspiration of liberation articulated at the 1953 Bandung or Afro-Asian Conference. Chaliand (1977) charted this systemic moment in a book originally published in French in 1976:

A sort of Third World euphoria began to be felt at the close of the 1950s, during the Algerian war, and it was soon given a boost by the radical turn of events in the Cuban revolution in 1960 -61. … Third World euphoria was an important characteristic of the 1960s, both in the Third World itself and in the West, especially among students and youth … . It developed in the context set by ' decolonization, ' whether by decree or by force; by the emergence of armed revolutionary movements in Asia, Latin America, and Africa; by a controversy between Russia and China, hinging, in the official texts, on problems of revolutionary strategy; and finally, by the dreary state of politics in most of the advanced industrial countries of the West. (xiv -xv)

Most of these revolutions had a strong rural base, from Cuba to Indochina and Portuguese Africa.

Taking stock of the wider arc of revolutions that formed the background to the conjuncture mapped by Chaliand, Halliday and Molyneux (1981) -writing about the Ethiopian case -observed that: ' unlike all post-1945 social revolutions in the Third World, apart from Iran, it was not made in the countryside, let alone by peasants, although rural contradictions and a variety of rebel forces that recruited peasants contributed to the weakening of the ancien r  egime ' (14). Similarly, the social upheaval across Afghanistan in the late 1970s began with an urban coup. Sen Gupta (1985) noted how: ' Of all the Marxist revolutions in the Third World, the Afghan revolution has come most conspicuously from above. The Chinese revolution actually set the pattern for subsequent revolutions:

protracted peasant revolutions in countryside, the establishment of revolutionary government in the liberated areas, and then advancing from the rural areas ' (158). In sharp contrast, ' from above ' , signifies an urban locus, reflecting the Afghan revolution ' s origins at the hands of the (faction-ridden, city- and armybased) Marxist party, first established in the mid1960s. Sen Gupta then details how, in 1978, the: ' revolution broke out in Kabul. Its struggle for power began and ended in Kabul ' . But: ' There was no insurrection in Kabul; only a takeover, in which people had no part to play, acting as mere observers. For the first six months, the distant villages nestling between the rugged fingers of the Hindu Kush did not even feel the impact of the seminal political change in Kabul ' (158). Complex urban -rural dynamics quickly unfolded in Afghanistan, however. The revolutionary government soon became dependent on Soviet aid and troops but faced widespread rural rebellion (with foreign support) and collapsed entirely in 1992. After a fraught interregnum, the Taliban advanced on and captured Kabul in 1994. They withdrew in the face of the U.S.-led invasion in November 2001. The Taliban recapture of the Afghan capital in August 2021 recalled the fall of Saigon in April 1975, when U.S. forces abandoned the capital of the collapsing regime of the hitherto U.S.-backed South Vietnam (officially the Republic of Vietnam) to the Vietnam People ' s Army, who had waged a long guerilla war, outmaneuvering U.S. counterinsurgency. The countryside again surrounds the cities.

In several other ongoing conflicts, a complex picture prevails in terms of the locus of conflict and urban -rural dynamics. The war in Libya since the uprisings in 2011 had a combined character in terms of urban -rural dynamics, reflecting a network of cities, tribal and confessional affiliations, and state and firm infrastructures of hydrocarbon development, although the upheaval began as an urban uprising in its second city of Benghazi. What Mundy (2019) termed ' the violent and internationalized parcelization of Libya ' s sovereignty since the 2011 intervention ' has been mirrored in Sudan since April 2023. When the Sudanese conflict began, dispatches could note how, ' Sudan ' s capital city, Khartoum, is the main battleground of the conflict that has engulfed the country ' (Armed Conflict Location and Event Data Project 2023). As Craze (2025) noted, ' The conflict initially pitted the Sudanese army against the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) -a paramilitary organization formed during the reign of dictator Omar al Bashir (1989 -2019). In the war ' s first weeks, the RSF overran much of Khartoum … including the Presidential Palace ' (1). By March 2025, the Sudanese army had recaptured the palace and Khartoum, but the RSF controls many other towns and swatches of the countryside, especially in the southwestern Darfur province. Port Sudan, across the Red Sea from Jeddah, has been serving as the army ' s de facto capital city. The palace remains empty. Each side (and local militias) connect with a range of foreign backers and extractive economies focused on gold and livestock. Craze (2025) noted how:

The Sudanese civil war is at once too local and too international to be addressed by a diplomatic process that focuses on the two belligerents, which have a shaky hold over the militias they have enlisted, and whose businesses profit from the war. The forces breaking apart Sudan are structural and have parallels elsewhere in the region: the collapse of state capacity, military forces backed by mercenary state and nonstate actors, and the fragmentation of the body politic, are also characteristics of conflict in Yemen, the Central African Republic and Somalia. (10)

Similarly, the 2020 to 2022 conflict in Ethiopia, like the one that led to the collapse of its military regime in 1991, saw rural and peripheral rebellions advancing toward urban seats of government -and conflict within them reflecting wider geopolitical fractures (Weldemichel 2025). To analyze these dynamics, Thompson, Mohamoud, and Mahamed (2023) called for ' a trans-scalar analytic that theorizes geopolitical borders and urban borderlands as intertwined milieus ' (302). The conflict in Yemen that began in 2014 soon engulfed the cities, commencing with the capture of the capital, Sanaa, by Ansar Allah, referred to as the ' Houthi ' movement (Hamidaddin 2022). Cole (2024) referred to the conflict as ' a complex web of ground troops, support for surrogates and mercenaries, economic investments, and strategic concentration on securing the entire Arabian Sea and Gulf of Aden littoral ' (62). Yet attention to the interface of cities and power in the Arabian Peninsula has focused on the Persian Gulf, neglecting the cities of the Hijaz, notably Mecca 2 (Makkah) or Yemen. Urban geopolitics therefore needs to consider a wider range of case studies than hitherto, and in so doing, also move questions about the character and frontiers of the urban to center stage. The rest of this article explores such prospects, mobilizing a range of conceptual and empirical literature as underpinnings.

## The Relational Urbanization of Warfare

Accompanying references to urban geopolitics are claims that ' Warfare has migrated into cities ' (King 2021, back cover), or as Graham (2010) elaborated:

Warfare, like everything else is being urbanized. The great geopolitical contests -of cultural change, ethnic conflict and diasporic social mixing; of economic reregulation and resource exploitation; of ecological change -are, to a growing extent, boiling down to violent conflicts in the key strategic sites of our age: contemporary cities. The world ' s geopolitical struggles increasingly articulate around violent conflicts over urban strategic sites, and in many societies the urban violence surrounding such civil and civic warfare strongly shapes quotidian urban life. (16)

Analytical complexities emerge, however, when looking at conflicts such as those in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) where as many as 6 million people have been killed since the mid-1990s. The scale of suffering and the involvement of diverse protagonists (including armed forces from Angola, Rwanda, Sudan, Uganda, and Zimbabwe) prompted the tagging of DRC ' s deadly conflict as ' Africa ' s World War ' (Prunier 2008). Much of the killing and combat in DRC has taken place in rural areas. Yet, B € uscher (2018) noted the rural focus of the literature on DRC and related violence in the Great Lakes region of central-eastern Africa, but stressed ' complex interactions between conflict and urbanization, such as the emergence of new urban centers in conflict areas, or the complex functions of cities in regional agendas of peace-building interventions ' (196). B € uscher ' s focus on these interactions -in which cities are key loci -profoundly connects with conflicts beyond and, in the case of the DRC, notably transcends its eastern border with Rwanda and connects with power structures in the Rwandan capital of Kigali (Mathys 2017; B € uscher 2020). More widely, Pech, B € uscher, and Lakes (2018) argued that, ' While perceptions of warfare in Africa have mainly been shaped by a strong focus on rural areas, the role of urban areas in myriad forms of conflict remain little understood; this calls for closer interaction between conflict studies and urban studies ' (98). Although DRC ' s capital city of Kinshasa has not seen large-scale armed conflicts, Kinshasa ' s relationship to the wider conjuncture in the DRC is complex. Trapido (2016, 57) charted these lineaments, whereby the capital became:

Just one pole among many for the evacuation of commodities to the global economy. The eastern Congolese regions became the fiefdoms of unruly barons, often theoretically commanders in the national army, who played a complex game, juggling between the various power centers. Yet however feeble its grip on the hinterland might be, Kinshasa has always retained its position as first among equals. Whoever manages to rule in the capital will be recognized as sovereign. And sovereignty grants its holder significant resources; aid and loans; rents from industrial enclaves (however feeble); a veto over who multinational capital can treat with; the power to issue passports and visas; votes to hawk at the UN -all of which tip the scales for Kinshasa over its rivals. The corollary of this is that ' national politics ' has typically meant, first and foremost, controlling the capital city.

Coup d '  etats (and failed attempts) have been frequent occurrences in many postcolonial African states. These are invariably staged in capital cities. Capital cities falling to armed insurrection or rebel forces, however (rather than in a coup d '  etat), are less frequent. Kampala, Uganda, was briefly occupied by Tanzanian troops in 1979, following Tanzania ' s invasion that deposed President Idi Amin. Rebels also advanced on and seized Kampala in 1986. Addis Ababa fell in 1991 to rebels from Tigray. Kigali, Rwanda, was captured by rebels in 1994 in the wake of the Rwandan genocide. Mogadishu has experienced a series of conflicts since 1991, including occupation by Ethiopian troops in 2006. The war in Sudan since 2023 has seen the center of Khartoum (as well as other cities) change hands (amidst over 15 million Sudanese being displaced).

Bangui, the capital city of the CAR, therefore, stands out in having changed hands twice in recent decades through force of armed rebels arriving in the city from the countryside. It has repeatedly become a battleground in the last quarter-century, with civilian casualties sustained in 2001, 2003, and 2013, and massive population displacement in 2013. As Pogue (2024) noted, the associated fratricidal killing became known as La Crise and, ' At its worst, in December 2013, 1,000 people were murdered in a single weekend in Bangui ' (17). Since then, Bangui periodically came under siege by the Coalition des patriotes pour le changement -comprising an array of rebel groups. The government forces holding Bangui have been backed by 12,000 United Nations (UN) troops, Russian military and security contractors, and the Rwandan army. That government, in turn, came to power through force of arms in 2013, overthrowing prior president and army chief of staff Franc ¸ois Boziz  e. In the earlier violent changes of government that convulsed Bangui, an attempted coup in 2001 led to 50,000 people being displaced from their homes as the army split and Boziz  e retreated to Chad. Boziz  e himself had come to power in 2003 backed by troops from Chad, who entered Bangui. The violence in CAR seldom features in the world ' s media, however, as Maja-Pearce (2021) explained:

Nowadays, CAR is of little strategic interest to the former colonial powers, which is why the ongoing tragedy is barely reported. This is in contrast to, say, neighbouring Chad, where the recent death of the long-serving Idriss D  eby [president of Chad from 1990 until his death in battle against rebels in the north of Chad in April 2021] captured the world ' s attention because of the country ' s position in an ' unusually dangerous neighbourhood '-Boko Haram in the east, Isis in the north -against which it acts as a bulwark.

Conflict in the CAR therefore receives sporadic contemporary media coverage and Bangui itself is not referenced in any of the existing literature on urban geopolitics. Until Kilembe ' s (2015) study of the local dynamics of a Bangui neighborhood, the few scholarly accounts of postcolonial Bangui date to three decades ago (Adrien-Rongier 1981; Villien 1985; Baticle 1994). 3 Yet Bangui has become folded into international connections and flows of commodities and money. Backed by Russian military contractors, the current government has issued mining contracts for areas under its control to politically well-connected Russian firms. In 2013, more than 300,000 people, more than half of Bangui ' s population, fled their homes for camps elsewhere in the city and its outskirts or in DRC and other neighboring countries (DTM 2014). Syrian cities have experienced similar levels of civilian displacement, and a similar proportion of Mosul ' s overall population (but numbering more than 1 million people) were displaced by the fighting in 2016 and 2017. Beyond parts of Iraq, Syria, and Ukraine, however, to date this century (until the Sudanese conflict since April 2023 and Gaza since October 2023) only Bangui 4 had seen such a large fraction of its population forced to flee their homes.

Full detailing of the conflict in CAR -which acquired a sectarian character -is beyond my scope here. The first coup in 1966 led, with French indulgence, to the proclamation of a monarchy ( Empire centrafricain ) in 1976. The monarch, a former soldier in the French imperial armed forces who had served in French Indochina, Jean Claude Bokassa, 5 was then overthrown with decisive French military intervention in 1979. Multiple coups, rebellions, foreign interventions (including seven French military deployments), and violent struggles have followed since. The sectarian turn has accelerated in the last decade with competing rebel coalitions known as Selaka and anti-Balaka, the latter increasingly scripting all Muslims as outsiders or nonnationals. Questions of belonging and identity have been expressed in violent attacks targeting the minority (less than 15 percent) Muslim communities (Lombard and Picco 2021), which in Bangui and elsewhere in CAR amount to a concerted anti-Muslim displacement. Although the scale of this violence, arguably one of the most significant anti-Muslim mobilizations since the Bosnian war, is poorly documented, 6 the interactions of this violence with ongoing and residual power struggles from earlier rounds of politics and conflict are complex (Vlavonou 2023). As de Vries and Mehler (2019) observed, ' In the CAR the numerous coup attempts, rebellions, and shifts in power have never resulted in a political settlement, successful power-sharing or the outright victory of an elite group ' (313). Seemingly, no stabilizing or negotiated order emerges. The critical literature on the conflicts of everpresent power struggles, however, point up interconnections between Bangui, rebellion in the provinces, extractive economies (of minerals and timber), and neighboring states. The more compelling accounts focus on the dynamics of these connections -a strategy advocated in other critical work on conflicted postcolonial sovereignty-scapes (Mampilly 2003; Sidaway 2003; Paasche and Sidaway 2021; Bakonyi 2022). Hence, Lombard and Carayannis (2015) ' are sceptical of all those analyses that describe CAR ' s problems, including the ongoing war, as a function of its ' weakness ' or even ' failure. ' These descriptors tell us nothing about the nature of the relationships -within the CAR ' s borders and beyond -that are the core dynamics of governance in the CAR ' (11, italics in original).

These dynamics have historical roots. The forced cotton, coffee, and tobacco cultivation of imperial concessions have been supplanted by more lucrative extractions of diamonds, gold, uranium, timber, and the spoils of poaching, including ivory. In 2016, Wikileaks (https://wikileaks.org/car-mining/) published a cache of documents on Chinese and Western companies ' corrupt deals in CAR. Mapping an aspect of the grip of this predatory political economy, the Brussels-based International Crisis Group (2014) visualizes it as a series of corridors and itineraries (see Figure 2). Their plotting extends over only CAR and the neighboring states, however. A wider account would need to chart international ivory markets, Russian security and mining interests, French and South African security contractors, and a French sugar and beverage conglomerate (The Sentry 2021), Lebanese and French timber merchants (Global Witness 2015), as well as the globespanning markets for gold and diamonds. A finer scale geographical plotting would also include the hundreds of roadblocks and checkpoints that manage the webbed flows of extraction and accumulation, mindful, too, in Murrey and Mollet ' s (2023) critical analytic, of ' the material and embodied effects and consequences of extractivisms ' (761). One such mapping of the ' politics of pillage ' (Schouten and Kalessopo 2017) in CAR identified nearly 300 checkpoints ' administered ' by a wide range of forces and yielding over $10 million per year in revenue.

As a site to think through the meanings of urban geopolitics, Bangui therefore invites study of the relational interaction of the urban and the geopolitical -the nexus of connections with rural spaces, other cities, and with planetary flows of minerals, weapons, and money. Describing ' the elite ' s road to riches, ' Smith (2015) reported: ' Concessionary politics are by no means exclusive to CAR. … However in most countries, including states in sub-Saharan Africa, concessions are more of an exception … . Yet in CAR, the prospection, delimitation, negotiation, and of renegotiation, of concessions have become the default mode of governance ' (112 -13).

Those commanding power (at different scales, from Bangui to each of the hundreds of checkpoints) are able to (informally or formally) grant concessions. Bangui is far from the bigger circuits of oil and geopolitics that envelop western Africa, from Lagos to Luanda around the Gulf of Guinea. Landlocked CAR is on the edge of a map (see Figure 3) of these that prefaces the text of Soares de Oliveira (2007), who showed how state capitals ' and to a minor degree other urban centres ' (112) become domains of state violence, frequently involving private and foreign forces and contractors. Bangui is therefore a key, but not the sole, locus of such power and ensuing formal concessions, and the city -and the central apparatus of statehood -becomes a key target for rebellions that begin in the provinces or are fostered by actors (governments and in some cases insurgents) in the six neighboring states.

Considering the complex meanings and connections of the urban considered earlier, a focus on relational connections with wider nonurban hinterlands yields a different arc of urban geopolitics to the hitherto prominent threads. Rather than a straightforward urbanization of conflict, the analytical focus is on urban -rural connections. Such trajectories are mirrored in other cases. In this context, Ramadan and Fregonese (2017) have traced the connections between Palestinian mobilizations elsewhere in Lebanon, beyond the capital city, in fostering what became an urban, camp-based, Palestinian revolution in Beirut from the late 1960s onward. Examining urban -rural interactions in the case of conflict and rebel governance in Colombia led O ' Connor (2023) to call for ' more detailed assessment of the ties between urban and rural units and support networks, and a more explicit comparison between patterns of social tie creation in urban and rural environments ' (8).

Postcolonial Burma (known as Myanmar since 1989) also offers an instructive example, given continual and complex arrays of provincial insurgencies since independence in 1948. For much of this period (1962 -2011 and since 2021) the country has been under military rule. Even through the civilian interregnum in the decade after 2011, the Myanmar Armed Forces, continued to exercise decisive influence in governance, a role entrenched through the constitution. The major port city Yangon (the capital until 2005, when the then-military regime moved the country ' s administrative capital to the purposebuilt city of Naypyidaw more than 300 kilometers to the north) and the second city Mandalay have periodically become the domains of large-scale civilian protests, most notably in 1988, when mass protest in Yangon was violently quelled by the military. Notwithstanding the events of 1988 and other demonstrations and state violence over the decades, large-scale armed conflict has not occurred in Yangon since World War II, when the city (then British colonial Rangoon) was occupied by Japanese forces. Throughout decades of conflict in Myanmar, in which provincial areas have changed hands and numerous ceasefires have been called, the military have established and embedded extractive political economies with parallels to those found in CAR. Considering these linkages and the recycling of attendant profits into the built environment of Yangon, Sarma and Sidaway (2020) detailed how:

[The military] allocate resource concessions (for precious gems, timber, wildlife products and latterly natural gas among other commodities) and some of the attendant flows of capital (as well as illicit ones linked to narcotics and weapons) are being recycled into the redevelopment of Yangon ' s urban fabric, where they intersect with foreign capital through laws that require the latter to enter into joint ventures with local partners and brokers. As a code-word for these key intermediaries, the term ' crony ' has entered everyday Burmese. (453)

Urban geopolitics of Yangon must necessarily attend to these transformative flows and trace the crucial connections between frontiers of extractive accumulation in Myanmar ' s peripheries and the urban frontiers of redevelopment and exclusion in downtown Yangon. Examining these interactions and building on Sarma and Sidaway ' s account, Rhoads (2023) noted in relation to Yangon that:

Myanmar ' s systematic dispossession of religious and ethnic minorities is well-documented as a tool for counterinsurgency through territorialisation. However, the specific contours of the relationship between minorities, territorialisation, and urban dispossession remain underexplored. The article argues that legislative changes linking identity, property, and belonging led to widescale invisible dispossession of minorities, through the mechanisms of law, citizenship and bureaucracy. Such dispossession gave birth to multiple urban frontiers -temporal spaces that break down existing property relations and create new ones through territorialization. (122)

Cases such as Bangui and Yangon, and the others more briefly considered here, signal the contradictory processes at work around the intersections of cities and geopolitics. Their dimensions, constituents, and vectors are more variegated and ' entangled '-to adopt a term used in another recent consideration of urban geopolitics from northeast India (Das, Mittal and Sarma 2025) -than the typical starting points for urban geopolitics, that refer to the urbanization of warfare.

## Resituating Urban Geopolitics

This article has advocated broadening urban geopolitics and attending more systematically to connections, sites, and processes beyond city limits. In turn, however, this speaks to the wider debate about the character of the urban. Antonsich and Hoyler (2019) commented that, ' As insightful as this literature has been, the links between urbanism and geopolitics continue however to remain largely unaddressed. This is because urban geopolitics does not seem to be particularly interested in the notion of urbanism, but principally hones in on the urban as a scalar dimension which complements the traditional focus on the state ' (145). Questions about the boundaries of the urban, however, are foregrounded in a literature on ' planetary urbanization, ' and related notions of ' extended urbanization, ' whereby the analytical categories of urban, nonurban, rural, and city are problematized (Brenner 2014; Acuto 2015; Martinez, Bunnell, and Acuto 2021; Schmid

2023). Of course, there is a prior literature that approached cities and countryside as deeply intertwined, drawing on examples in Latin America and Southeast Asia (Roberts 1978; McGee 1997). Recent responses to the idea of planetary urbanization have questioned the ways that the concept implies ' that power resides in the city, with urban processes radiating out and transforming the nonurban, ' recognizing instead ' that agency is dispersed and that rural -urban relations may be initiated from the rural as well as from the urban ' (Wang et al. 2023, 3). Yet as summarized in a case study that critically applies the evolving planetary urbanization literature to understanding issues of urban growth, dispossession, and forced displacement in and around the city of Tete in northwestern Mozambique, Lesutis (2021) wrote:

In order to redefine the meaning of the ' urban, ' these theorists venture into areas previously understood as ' rural ' locations in urban studies -they conceptualize zones of resource extraction, agro-industrial enclosures, logistic and communication infrastructures, or waste disposal sites as ' operational landscape of the urban. ' Even if these territories may not (and often do not) resemble the city in terms of population density, settlement properties, or infrastructural and social fabric, supplying raw materials, energy, water, food, and labor, they play a strategic role in supporting the growth of the world ' s largest urban agglomerations. (1198)

Although Lesutis did not refer to or situate his case study as urban geopolitics, his (and other) applications of the planetary urbanization warrant being placed in closer dialogue with urban geopolitics. Reflecting briefly on this elsewhere, I asked: ' Is it a corollary of planetary urbanization that urban [if the latter is worldwide] and geopolitics [in the literal sense of earth-politics] are homologous or equivalents? ' (Sidaway 2018, 235). A definitive answer is probably unachievable. By negotiating such problematics, at the intersections of multiple frontier processes, and the kinds of lateral links presented in the case studies in this article, however, urban geopolitics navigates wider theoretical and historical fields. The limit case is perhaps the self-destruction of humanity during World War II. The Holocaust required an extended urban apparatus, seamlessly blending Nazi wickedness with networks of railroads, camps, armies, and bureaucrats whose logics anticipate subsequent, ever-present potentials of nuclear holocaust: banalized, urbanized, and meticulously planned. As the historian Landau (2016) reflected: ' In the Nazi period, the sheer ordinariness of the decisionmakers and perpetrators stands in terrifying contrast with the quite extraordinary consequences of their decisions and actions. The destructive potential of the Cold War and post-Cold War eras is a child of the same cultural framework -rampant technology, technocracy and bureaucracy -which made possible the Holocaust ' (289).

Over a decade ago, the relationship between the concept of targeting (in its many forms) and notions of understanding, imagining, and shaping the urban led Bishop, Clancey, and Phillips (2012) to reflect on The City as Target . And Gregory (2011) had written of the geopolitical abstractions underpinning urban targeting ' whereby the kill-chain converted cities into numbers, coordinates, shapes, and images ' (274). When geopolitics urbanizes, the logical corollary is that humanity itself becomes the target. Resituated urban geopolitics must be critically attendant to such connections or it -and humanity -cannot claim any futures, only barbarisms stacked on atrocity.
