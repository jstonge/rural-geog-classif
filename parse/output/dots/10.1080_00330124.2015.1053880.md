Research on small cities has begun to attract the attention of scholars who argue that contemporary urban scholarship, in its preoccupation with the largest and most advanced world-class cities, have largely ignored small to medium-sized cities. In China, although much attention has been paid to economically advanced urban centers, there actually has been a steady stream of work on small cities. This article profiles how a comparatively smaller city in western China attempts to market itself by selectively placing itself within various social-spatial and political-economic realities. Through Jinghong, we illustrate how local officials and planners attempt to center the city as a gateway to Southeast Asia. By activating, often discursively, multiscalar transborder strategies, local officials in Jinghong not only mobilize ethnic imaginaries, but they also adopt forms of entrepreneurial tactics to promote growth. Developmental strategies of Jinghong not only vacillate between (and draw on) both rural and urban resources; they are furthermore expected to alleviate rural poverty. Through highlighting the agency of small cities like Jinghong in China, this article speaks to the broader developmentalist critique of third- and fourth-world cities as an unfortunate footnote in global urban restructuring, often depicted as places of uniform marginalization and structural irrelevance. Indeed, by focusing on the geography of small cities and giving due attention to their size and proximity to rural spaces, case studies like Jinghong might yet point empathetically to different ways and imperatives of "being urban" where the weight that they carry can also be duly recognized. **Key Words: China, development, rural resources, rural-urban nexus, small cities.**

The general assessment of this strategy is that although it has brought unprecedented growth to the region, the disparity between western and coastal regions has persisted (Grewal and Ahmed 2011). Two main reasons are suggested to explain this. First, massive capital injections by the central government into the region cannot continue indefinitely. Second, the region faces environmental limitations such as insufficient water and arable land resources (Tian 2004).

Nonetheless, contextualizing such a clarion call for growth into a more local scale analysis shows that although there are both discursive and material spaces for places in western China to embark on their own economic campaigns, the nature of such a market economy can be significantly different from one Chinese city to another. This is notwithstanding the constant intercity discursive referencing and purported learning by less developed cities in relation to their more successful counterparts. In this regard, this article aims to profile how a comparatively smaller city in western China attempts to market itself by selectively placing itself within various social-spatial and political–economic realities. Through Jinghong, we illustrate how local government officials and planners attempt to center the city as a gateway to the Southeast Asia region. By activating, often discursively, multiscalar transborder strategies, local officials in Jinghong not only mobilize ethnic imaginaries, but they also adopt forms of entrepreneurial tactics and urban imaginaries to promote growth. We highlight two challenges, situated in two different spatial scales, in the “catch-up” processes of Jinghong. First, although the developmental aspiration is modeled after the more successful coastal cities, Jinghong’s opening up is necessarily rooted in regionalist impulses (in contrast to the global reach of top-tier Chinese cities). We argue that this poses particular challenges to Jinghong’s urban imaginaries and aspirations and its relationship to the broader market. Second, and relatedly, we argue that the developmental strategies of Jinghong vacillate between (and draw on) both rural and urban resources; they are furthermore expected to address rural developmental problems.

It should be stated at the outset that we are not suggesting Jinghong's case is exceptional. Rather, we aim to contribute to the much neglected scholarship on small cities like Jinghong and demonstrate their relevance to the production of urban knowledge and theories. In other words, we ask what small cities can tell us about the broader urban developmental processes. In recent years, research on small cities has begun to attract the attention of scholars who argue that contemporary urban scholarship in its preoccupation with the largest and most advanced world-class cities have largely ignored small to medium-sized cities; the latter of which actually constitute the greater part of the urban world. Bell and Jayne (2009, 683), in particular,

have called on scholars to “think big about small cities” by engaging with studies on small cities that “enable important interventions into a diverse range of debates within urban theory, including how small cities are integrated into global flows of capital, culture and people” (Jayne et al. 2010, 1411). Importantly, research must also pursue an understanding of the ways in which “smallness is bound up with particular ways of acting, self-images, structures of feeling, senses of place, aspirations” (Bell and Jayne 2009, 690; see McManus and Connor [2013], for a cognate discussion on the agency of rural communities). There is hence a need to give due emphasis to understanding the urban experiences of small cities in their own terms and eschew the metrocentric tendency of judging them with established theories and a priori standards that are derived from large global cities and metropolis (Bunnell and Maringanti 2010). Our research follows up on these exhortations.

In China, although much attention has been paid to economically advanced mega urban centers such as Beijing, Shanghai, and Shenzhen, there actually has been a steady stream of work on small cities and towns (see, e.g., Deng 2000; Shi 2000; Feng 2001; S. J. Xu and Zhang 2004). Indeed, as early as 1986, Fei emphasized the importance of studying small cities and township development in China so as to solve the country's “big problem” on population management and rural developmental issues.

By 2010, 75.1 percent of urban residents lived in midsized cities (Report on the development of small and medium-sized cities in China 2012). Although it is beyond the scope of this article to provide a systematic study on small cities in China, S. J. Xu and Zhang (2004) highlighted four phases in their development. These are 1949 to 1954 (the initial resurgence of small cities and towns following the establishment of the People's Republic of China), 1953 to 1956 (decline of small cities due to policy shifts from central government as well as negative impacts from the Great Leap Forward movement, which decimated the economic foundation of small cities), 1967 to 1978 (further stagnation of small cities and towns due to “ten-year unrest” during the Cultural Revolution), and post-1978 (resurgence of small cities and towns due to economic reforms).

It can be argued that the multitiered governance structure in China privileges the central and, to some extent, provincial government decision over regional, county, and local townships, with important policies being determined by higher authorities, but the decentralization of the Chinese urban system over the past decades has meant that greater autonomy has now been granted to local government (including small cities and towns). As Li and An (2010) illustrated in their study of four small towns in Shanxi Province, small towns can acquire resources and have used migration from their hinterlands to bolster their local economic growth. This article draws from fieldwork conducted in the summers of 2007–2008 and 2011–2012. Individual interviews were conducted with officials from the Economic Planning Department and the Agricultural Development Department in Jinghong. Informal discussions were also conducted with local business owners and tourism workers. The nature of our interaction was broad and focused on their personal views, as well as the views of the agencies they represent, on how to develop Jinghong and the challenges ahead. This article is largely interested in the development of Jinghong since the early 2000s. Following this introduction, we highlight how the concept of urban entrepreneurialism is relevant to China and Jinghong. Next, we discuss aspects of Jinghong’s rural–urban nexus and developmental tensions as well as its strategic positioning within the larger Southeast Asian region. We conclude with a sympathetic assessment of the plights of small cities like Jinghong.

Marginal Cities as Target for Entrepreneurial
Policies and Development Intervention

The fixation on coastal and successful cities not only ignores the socioeconomic complexity of other Chinese cities, but it also unhelpfully promotes urban imaginaries of some cities as always lagging behind and playing catch-up or imitating other more successful coastal cities (Gaubatz 2008). Such “loser cities” (Roseau 2009) are often portrayed negatively as stragglers in the dominant global urban hierarchy and in dire need of external investment and stimulus because their endogenous growth is limited. Consequently, they are often portrayed as objects for developmentalist intervention (Escobar 1998). This view, however, obfuscates the agency and responsibility of some of these places as the driver for rural poverty alleviation themselves.

Nonetheless, in an attempt to catch up, many mar-
ginal cities do embark on urban restructuring schemes
that parallel the dictates of Western urban entrepre-
neurialism (Logan and Molotch 1987; Jonas and Wil-
son 1999; Short and Kim 1999; Jessop and Sum 2000).
It has been widely argued that globalization and urban
restructuring of cities have resulted in the formation
of a new entrepreneurialism where city officials now
focus on the speculative deployment of local resources
to attract private investments and stimulate local eco-
nomic regeneration in cities (Leitner and Garner
1993). In the context of China, Wu (2002) character-
ized urban entrepreneurialism as being fundamentally
a state project, although we argue that it would be
more useful to unravel the various scales of state
involvement and their specific responsibilities.

In China, even as local governments are increasingly
granted more autonomy, they also have to diversify

their revenue sources and provide their own funds to
build urban infrastructure projects. This is not to say
that the local state is relegated to a mere “business
partner” alongside nonstate actors, as it still wields
enormous power and continues to play an important
role in providing strategic guidance (see Oi 1995;
G. C. S. Lin 2002). Yet, Yeh and Wu (1999) critically
pointed out that some local officials advocate pro-
growth strategies for both economic growth and pro-
moting their own career advancement. As a result,
rampant imitation, redundant construction, and spec-
ulation in Chinese cities are not uncommon. Hence,
although there certainly has been heightened intercity
competition in China, one must not be too hasty to
homogenize the developmental experiences of
Chinese cities, big or small (J. Xu and Yeh 2005). It is
fair to say that there exists significant space for Chi-
nese cities to chart their particular developmental
paths, outside of the predominant “western models”
(or, indeed, any perceived directives from the central
government), even as such paths engender the broad
logics of progrowth, public-private partnerships and
foreign direct investments (Zhang and Peck 2014).

In the case of Jinghong, local entrepreneurial strate-
gies entail the mobilizing of a new urban imaginary
that associates itself with successful urban counterparts
such as Shenzhen. Such interurban referencing not
only works to legitimize the entrepreneurial discourses
of local planners, but it also helps to reimage Jing-
hong's purportedly backward status by latching onto
an already known symbol of success. As Ong (2011,
17) pointed out, "the practice of citing a 'more suc-
cessful city'—itself an unstable category—seems to stir
urban aspirations and sentiments of inter-city rivalry
as well as standing as a legitimation for particular
enterprises at home." We are not suggesting that Jing-
hong will become a replica of a "model" Chinese city,
nor that it strives to be so. Rather, we show how a
small city such as Jinghong is discursively caught up in
urban aspirational models by embarking on various
entrepreneurial strategies, even as it attempts to valo-
rize the particular resources afforded by its sociospatial
circumstance.

There are, indeed, interesting divergences between
Jinghong and larger cities like Chongqing in the west
and other coastal cities in the east. For example, there
is a more obvious focus on rural resources to help it
develop and a concomitant concern toward rural pov-
erty alleviation. The latter point is often cited in our
conversations with our respondents, even as there is
no evidence that the central or provincial governments
quantify their performance in this regard.

Overall, this article uses rural development and
urban economic frames to show how Jinghong’s
entrepreneurial pulses are not geared solely toward
intercity competition but are also meant for alleviating
rural poverty. Jinghong’s strategy to boost the city’s
competitiveness mobilizes the rural economy, drawing
it materially into urban space (Woods 2011). Dyadic
conceptions of urban–rural relationships are

Conceptually, Jinghong demonstrates how the seemingly dissimilar goals of extraction from rural resources and poverty alleviation are drawn into a dis- course of urban entrepreneurialism, interurban referencing (both within China and in the Indo-China region), and rural–urban interdependency. In so doing, it points to the need for framing the develop- ment of small cities beyond urban economic terms but to include rural developmental ones, too.

Jinghong, Xishuangbanna in Context

Yunnan is an ethnically diverse province bordered by Laos, Vietnam, and Myanmar. One third of its 42 million people are ethnic minorities and up to 41 percent of the population live below or near the poverty line (Lu 2012). Jinghong is the capital of the Xishuangbanna Dai Autonomous Prefecture in Southwest Yunnan (Banna for short; see Figure 1) and has a population of over half a million (Jinghong City Statistics Office 2011).¹ As the prefectural capital, Jinghong is the urban center of Banna, where all gov- ernment agencies are located, and it is the main, if not only, dispenser of developmental strategies and proj- ects to the rural areas.

Developing the region has always been a political
focus at the highest level of governance in China due
to the persistent impoverishment of minority groups
(Zhang 2012). Relatedly, the geopolitical stability of
the western and border regions is a particular concern
as well, as a result of the transborder ethnic ties that
minorities in western China have with those in Viet-
nam, Laos, and Myanmar. Banna has, for example,
been a buffer zone between China and mainland
Southeast Asia since the fifteenth century. In that
sense, the central government has always been wary of
minority groups establishing deep ethnic ties across
borders. Yet, in an age of market economy, city offi-
cials in Jinghong have tried to valorize ethnic unique-
ness and appeal to a transborder ethnic solidarity in
their efforts to build an entrepreneurial city, as elabo-
rated later. In the next two sections, we illustrate the
developmental tensions that arose from the specific
cultural politics, history, and location of Jinghong.

Tourism, Regionalization, and the Spatial
Representations of Jinghong

Banna was established as a Dai Autonomous Prefec-
ture in 1953. Notwithstanding the particularities of
Jinghong's sociopolitical, historical, and environmental
contexts, the drivers behind its catch-up processes are
resolutely developmentalist, market-oriented, and
modernist. The launch of a regional development pol-
icy in the 1990s encapsulates such a market discourse
perfectly. In 1999, the Central Government officially
embarked on an ambitious plan to bring the marginal-
ized western provinces to par with other relatively
more prosperous places in eastern and coastal China.

The Great Western Development (*xibudakaifa*) pol-
icy is meant to be a “a long term project . . . focusing
on improving infrastructure, providing incentives for
investment, protecting the natural environment, forg-
ing economic adjustment, nurturing human capital
and sectors with a comparative advantage and aiding
the poor” (Lai 2002, 458). Under this broad directive,
a range of social–economic policies (both macro and
micro) have been implemented in numerous regions
in the west. For Banna, since the 1990s, a large-scale
regional development project was initiated, centering
on the Lancang River and Mekong River and Jing-
hong in Banna. Such policies aim to enroll marginal-
ized communities into the market economy and
capitalize on their unique sets of skills and identities.

Implicit in the discourse of the Great Western
Development is the nod toward the coastal cities. For
example, the coastal city of Shenzhen was mentioned
several times by respondents as a city that Jinghong
can look up to. Interestingly, among the many cities in
the western region, Jinghong is not the only one that
aspires to be the Shenzhen of the west, but Kashgar in
Xinjiang Province is, thus far, the only western city
that has been officially anointed as “Shenzhen of the
west” by the Central Government (Ministry of
Commerce, China 2011). As mentioned earlier, there
are clear and self-acknowledged differences between
the place identities of Shenzhen and Jinghong, not
least being Shenzhen’s positionality as the hinterland
or proxy for Hong Kong that directly links it to
the global market (Luo and Shen 2012), whereas Jing-
hong’s opening up is confined to the broader mainland
Southeast Asia region. Analytically, then, the juxtapos-
ing of Jinghong and Shenzhen is at best rhetorical and
the experience of Shenzhen’s economic growth bears
few direct lessons to the development of Jinghong.
Indeed, even at a broad discursive level, the distinct
challenges in some of the developmental initiatives
Jinghong officials have undertaken, particularly in the
tourism sector, are tangential to Shenzhen’s context.

Following years of consistent growth, tourism
receipts in 2013 hit a record high of US$2.74 billion
in Xishuangbanna, of which 80 percent was generated
by Jinghong (“Major tourism conglomerates” 2014).

Equally important is the fact that the city of Jinghong itself has a limited draw for tourism. Hence, there is a self-acknowledged need to discover, develop, and maintain ways of enthralling tourists; yet at the same time these initiatives must be experientially unique. In this respect, in the past ten years, to increase the tourism potential of Jinghong, the biggest Theravada Buddhism temple in mainland Southeast Asia was constructed. The Mengli Temple was built in 2008 with an investment of 350 million yuan and the total built-up area of the temple complex is 24,000 m².

An official explained that the temple will help concretize the image of Southeast Asia in Jinghong as Theravada Buddhism is the most popular religion in Southeast Asia, especially in countries like Thailand, Laos, Myanmar, and Cambodia. majority of tourists to Jinghong are still domestic, with international tourists constituting less than 5 percent of tourist arrivals in 2010 (Jinghong City Statistics Office 2011).

## The Rural-Urban Nexus in Entrepreneurial Jinghong

Beyond the regional imagings of Jinghong and Banna that are geared toward tourism, other efforts to make the city entrepreneurial have also been met with challenges. The main problem is that, notwithstanding the recent waves of market-oriented development plans, Banna for the most part is essentially still an agrarian society. According to the latest population census in 2011, 59.43 percent of Jinghong's population is considered rural. Hence, as mentioned earlier, Jinghong city's strategy for catching up cannot help but involve in important and intimate ways rural places and rural economies. Yet, how does a city attempt to adopt a coherent rural-urban interface to profit from its rural base?

Jinghong's overall development master plan is arguably couched in the grand narratives of progress, development, and modernity. The tourism sector described earlier is one important way in which Jinghong capitalizes on the rural economy, with the agricultural sector being the other route. Either way, this incorporation of the rural economy is folded into an entrepreneurial discourse. Furthermore, as with tourism development, it needs to function as a rural poverty alleviation strategy concomitantly. For example, city officials we spoke to took pains to explain that Jinghong needs to revamp its “backward” image by adopting modern urban practices and capitalizing on its indigenous resources. Such narratives are typically deemed to require decisive changes in the purposes, organization, and delivery of economic strategies that are focused on the local, urban, or regional levels and infused with some kind of entrepreneurial spirit.

The Banna agrarian economy is historically based on cash-cropping with tea, rubber, and sugarcane being the most popular crops. Poverty alleviation measures in the region have hence been focused on encouraging the minorities to cultivate particular crops. Nonetheless, the limits of such crops in alleviating poverty have long been recognized, not least because market prices for them can fluctuate dramatically. Chapman (1991), in his study of Yunnan rubber plantations, very early on questioned the long-term benefits of rubber to small family farms that have to compete increasingly with bigger and often state-run enterprises. Concomitantly, limited labor availability and minimal technological investments have seen smallholding crop production stagnating in Yunnan (Li 2006), throwing doubt on the effectiveness of this strategy's ability to alleviate rural poverty.

What is different is their fairly recent interest in niche agriculture and their attempts to entrepreneurially package these products to maximum advantage. As one agricultural official explained:

> Our strengths are our fresh air and unpolluted land. We cannot go the way of our neighbors. It won't work if we start to plant rice in a large commercial scale. We cannot compete with Thailand. We are targeting specialized food and agriculture and tourism. The development strategy is actually very simple, differentiate and showcase our strengths! (S. In late 2006, the Jinghong Agricultural Department introduced an indigenous pig-rearing program to the Jinuo people. Recognizing the potential to turn an erstwhile subsistence activity into an entrepreneurial one, the Agricultural Department encouraged villagers to rear the small ear pig for sale. The Agricultural Department was confident of the success of this latest market-oriented plan for several reasons. First, it believed that rearing small ear pigs will not demand too many household resources from the villagers, whose main economic activity hitherto has been tea and rubber plantation. Second, it believed that there is an untapped market in this indigenous breed of pig. Last but not least, because of the proximity of the Jinuo mountains to the city itself, it envisaged that transportation of the pigs would not be too much of a burden (see Neo and Chen [2009] for a more detailed discussion of this initiative).

Clearly, the market ethos is palpable in this endeavor, even as its original goal was poverty alleviation. Hence, in the rural outskirts of the city, formerly small-scale subsistence farmers have also adopted an entrepreneurial outlook. In an interview, a pig farmer expressed how his desire to expand his pig-rearing operations might not materialize: “Here in the mountains, I cannot hope to have a big farm, it is impossible. We need to be on the plains to do that” (C. Kai, personal interview, 27 May 2008). Along the same lines, an agricultural official elaborated:

> The market is there and it is big. We must push more out to urban consumers. ... That is why we have also set up a specialized butchery in Jinghong city itself. You must have a shop in the city that only sells this meat, to let consumers know that they are buying a very unique product. (W. Hong, personal interview, 28 May 2008)

Indeed, the first such specialized butchery is located, interestingly, on one of the main shopping streets in

the city. The presence of such a shop is a visual testament of the links the urban center has with the rural. Inside the shop are posters that describe in great detail the small ear pig, its physiology, and the environment in which it is bred. A recurring motif is the stress on the pigs' indigeneity to the Banna region. Since this scheme was introduced in 2007, it has seen encouraging success. To date, more than 2,000 rural families are enrolled in the scheme, earning about 4,000 to 5,000 yuan a year. The annual production of the pig meat is 1,680 tonnes and there are now four specialized butcheries throughout Yunnan Province (Pan 2009).

Another more organically developed urban attraction is the cluster of tea shops (specializing in the indigenous Pu-er tea) located in close proximity to the hotel district in Jinghong. The “tea district” materially connects the urban to the rural economy and functions as an urban tourism site as well. Although the shops in the district are ostensibly tea traders, they welcome tourists. Each of the shops contains are a wealth of information on the Pu-er tradition in the region and introductions to the various tea plantations and factories in the rural areas of Banna. When queried about this cluster of shops, an official explained:

> That was not really planned. The tea merchants wanted to be near the Jinglan Hotel [a high-end hotel] as many businessmen stay there. So it is convenient for them to talk business. It quite quickly became an area where tourists can visit, too, not just businessmen. (X. Chen, personal interview, 3 July 2012)

The specialized butchery and the tea district are visible testaments of Jinghong extending beyond its own spatial limits to draw on (rural) products and images that will enable it to rise above the competition. Hence, urban-based entrepreneurial strategy to sell the city cannot be divorced from its economic realities (in this case, its dominant rural economy), its broader social-cultural circumstance, and its political responsibility to help its hinterland. The preceding anecdotal examples suggest that the development path of Jinghong is guided, in the broadest sense, on the principle of capitalizing on its rural resources (e.g., tourism, organic livestock, and tea) and a desire to alleviate rural poverty (more explicitly seen in the tourism and organic livestock sectors). Nonetheless, although poverty alleviation is a perennial developmental goal of the Chinese government, across various administrative scales, there is little to suggest that the provincial or central government has explicitly prescribed how Jinghong should grow.

## Conclusion

We depart from the typical urban scholarship that tends to focus on large successful coastal cities in China to consider the nature and role of small

We show how Jinghong's development is bound up with various urban entrepreneurial developmental objectives, including urban reimaging and interreferencing, but also serves as important node that mediates urban and rural economy, as well as promotes rural growth.

We argue that even as city officials employ the rhetoric of urban entrepreneurialism, what they are actually doing is more contextual. Although it can be criticized that the broad strategies adopted by the local urban officials to jumpstart Jinghong and Banna's economy have been done in a somewhat piecemeal fashion, a closer analysis has shown that they have tried to leverage the unique strengths of the locale, rather than copy wholesale from other successful Chinese cities. In the case of Jinghong, urban entrepreneurialism is driven with a strong spatial logic that combines urban boosterism with rural poverty alleviation objectives, sensibilities, and responsibilities.

Through highlighting the agency of small cities like Jinghong in remote China, this article also speaks to the broader developmentalist critique of third- and fourth-world cities as an unfortunate footnote in global urban restructuring and often depicted as places of uniform marginalization and structural irrelevance (Shatkin 1998). Indeed, by focusing on the geography of small cities and giving due attention to their size and proximity to rural spaces, case studies like that of Jinghong might yet point empathetically to different ways and imperatives of “being urban” where the weight that they carry can also be duly recognized. ■
