# Examining Spatial Disparities of Obesity: Residential Segregation and the Urban-Rural Divide

Heewon Chea and Hyun Kim

University of Tennessee


This study investigates spatial disparities in obesity among different racial and ethnic groups from the urban-rural perspective through a case study of the southeastern United States, a region marked by its prevalence of chronic diseases and degree of residential segregation. Two proposed measures—local/global entropy indexes and a set of the obesity-segregation combined index (OBESEG)—are prescribed to exploit the degree of spatial disparity between counties' obesity rate and residential segregation in the urban-rural perspective. A strong interrelationship is confirmed between obesity rates and residential segregation, particularly for high levels of obesity and residential segregation, as well as for low levels of obesity and residential segregation at the county level. The findings identify several clusters of obesity-segregation correlation across the region. This urban-rural perspective helps reveal that clusters of rural areas have a high degree of segregation, whereas urban areas tend to create clusters of counties having less segregation and less obesity.

**Key Words:** entropy index, obesity-residential segregation, obesity-segregation combined index, spatial disparity.

En este estudio se investigan las disparidades espaciales que ocurren en obesidad entre diferentes grupos raciales y étnicos desde la perspectiva urbano-rural, adoptando como estudio de caso el sudeste de los Estados Unidos, una región macada por la prevalencia de enfermedades crónicas y por el grado de segregación residencial. Se prescriben dos medidas propuestas—los índices de entropía local/global y un conjunto del índice combinado de obesidad-segregación (OBESEG)—para explotar el grado de disparidad espacial entre las tasas de obesidad de los condados y la segregación residencial en la perspectiva urbano-rural. Se confirmó una fuerte interrelación entre la tasa de obesidad y la segregación residencial, en particular para los altos niveles de obesidad y de segregación residencial, lo mismo que los niveles bajos de obesidad y segregación residencial a nivel de condado. Los descubrimientos identifican varias aglomeraciones de la correlación obesidad-segregación a través de la región. Esta perspectiva urbano-rural ayuda a revelar que las aglomeraciones de las áreas rurales tienen un alto grado de segregación, mientras que las áreas urbanas tienden a crear aglomeraciones de condados que tienen menor segregación y menos obesidad. **Palabras clave:** disparidad espacial, índice combinado de obesidad-segregación, índice de entropía, obesidad-segregación residencial.

More than a third of U.S. adults suffer from obesity, which is becoming one of the most pervasive critical health issues in the United States (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC] 2018). Obesity is cited as a leading cause of various negative chronic health outcomes, such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, stroke, liver and kidney disorders, cancers, and mental issues; as a result, it is responsible for diminishing quality of life (Ogden et al. 2014). The main risk factors for obesity include lifestyle, eating habits, and genetics (Ogden et al. 2015). Research has found that different levels of obesity are observed among diverse racial and ethnic groups, particularly whites, blacks, and other minority groups (Cozier et al. 2014; CDC 2017a, 2017b). Recent research has attempted to unravel the relationship between geographical characteristics and the prevalence of obesity to determine whether physical environment and cultural representation

affect obesity by creating residential segregation, or vice versa, over time (Caprio et al. 2008; Kershaw and Pender 2016). Residential groups that are heavily exposed to unfavorable socioeconomic conditions and environments often engage in negative health behaviors, which usually manifest as a unique spatial pattern, such as residential segregation or geographic clusters (Finch et al. 2010). Although previous studies have scrutinized the relationship between residential segregation and obesity, few studies have developed appropriate methods for doing so, giving rise to different interpretations depending on geographic scale and representations of obesity data within the context of residential segregation. The reasons for these inconsistent results and analyses are twofold: First, the methods used should reflect the characteristics of obesity issues without careful deliberation on the variability of spatial units and extents. Traditional segregation indexmeasures, a popular method of geographic pattern analysis, do not capture the local characteristics of the region and thus produce misleading impressions, however (Cozier et al. 2014; Kershaw and Pender 2016). On a global scale, most research has focused on the national or state levels as the standard extent of spatial analysis. Although the prevalence of obesity is well studied at the national and state levels, patterns of residential segregation are identified differently depending on geographic scale. National- or state-level analysis is often too broad to depict detailed but significant spatial patterns of obesity and differences among diverse groups, suggesting the need for downscaling analysis to the county or census tract level when data are available. Second, current research has focused more on the urban setting than on rural areas, because the former is recognized as a more critical and more complicated means of explaining trends in obesity rate and residential segregation (Whitaker and Orzol 2006; Day et al. 2013; Cook et al. 2017). Considering the dominance of obesity, however, rural areas should be considered a comparative countersetting to urban areas.

This study investigates spatial disparities in obesity among different racial and ethnic groups in the urban–rural perspective through a case study of the southeastern United States. This region is selected for two reasons: (1) its greater prevalence of chronic diseases, such as high blood pressure and diabetes, which are linked to rate of obesity, and (2) its lack of diversity amid a notable degree of residential segregation (Hale 2010; Sharma 2014). Moreover, the region has a wide range of obesity rates and experiences residential separation across counties as a result of its clear rural–urban distinction. The study also seeks to develop appropriate measures with which to exploit the degree of spatial disparity between counties in the urban–rural perspective and specify which areas are of critical concern or require immediate attention. The proposed measures are designed to identify areas of interest both locally and globally. The results will improve health outcomes by highlighting spatial disparities, helping health and regional policymakers mitigate potentially negative health disparities between regions, and providing reasonable evidence for the benefits of living among diverse populations. The research consists of three phases. The first phase explores adult obesity rates at the county level in the United States with a view to examining the statistical significance of the southeastern United States. This region includes twelve states, delineated based on the functional homogeneity of their economies, as determined by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA 2018): Arkansas (AR), Mississippi (MS), Louisiana (LA), Alabama (AL), Tennessee (TN), Kentucky (KY), West Virginia (WV), Virginia (VA), North Carolina (NC), South Carolina (SC), Georgia (GA), and Florida (FL). In the

second phase, the degree of residential segregation among the racial and ethnic groups of the study area is measured and characterized as clusters. For both phases, we propose a set of integrated measures designed to determine the positionality of each state from a two-dimensional perspective: adult obesity rates and residential segregation within an urban–rural perspective. The main merits of the proposed measures— (1) the local/global entropy indexes (LEI and GEI) and (2) the obesity–segregation combined index (OBESEG)—are twofold. First, the measures enable the different ranges of multiple indexed measures, which are driven by socioeconomic or geospatial variables, to transit multidimensional positionality planes through a process of standardization. In the second phase, the measures classify the criticality of an instance (in this article, county) into four categories by degrees of obesity and segregation. Furthermore, the composition of racial and ethnic groups is used to investigate the underlying relationship between obesity rates and residential segregation. In the third phase, several key impressions for health and spatial policymakers are drawn by juxtaposing these processes into an urban–rural context.

## Background

### Factors of Obesity

Prior research on obesity issues primarily sought to identify crucial factors contributing to, or eventual causes of, obesity. Such research has generally focused on individual factors such as genetics, environment, physical activity, diet, and stress level. Essentially, genetic and environmental factors have long been thought the most important key risk factors for obesity. Recent research by Hill and Melanson (1999), however, identified causes of overweight by examining longitudinal cohort data sets from multiple countries. Counter to common belief, they concluded that genes do not significantly affect obesity but rather that environments conducive to decreased physical activity and limited sleep hours result in reduced energy expenditure, which was identified during the late twentieth century as the main factor in increased obesity. Gangwisch et al. (2005) also confirmed that adults who sleep fewer than seven hours tend to have a higher than average body mass index (BMI). Very recently, Kowaleski-Jones et al. (2017) indicated that walkable neighborhoods affect female obesity rates. Unhealthful eating habits are another factor in obesity: Fast food and alcohol are known to cause and exacerbate obesity. Jeffery et al. (2006) revealed that the frequency with which people eat fast food has a significant effect on energy intake, because frequent eating of fast food tends to create more fat in the body, causing a higher BMI than in those who are physically active and who eat a healthy diet. analyzed the role of racial and ethnic disparity in
obesity among adolescents transitioning into adult-
hood, and Whitaker and Orzol (2006) concentrated
on preschool children; both studies focused on the
effects of transitions during the life cycle.

Residential Segregation and Health Disparities

A different perspective involves focusing on place,
which provides space for living and restrains the
environment on behalf of residents. Various studies
have examined the relationship between health out-
come and place, with special focus on health
inequality and disparities. Macintyre, Ellaway, and
Cummins (2002) explored the relationship between
health and place, dubbed *place effect*. Bernard et al.
(2007), insisting on the need for proper conceptual-
ization of place, which can contribute to the measur-
ing of local health outcomes and disparities,
proposed a theoretical concept of neighborhood
based on Giddens's structuration theory. Finch et al.
(2010) explored the neighborhood effect on health
outcomes by examining concentrations of advan-
tages and disadvantages between highly educated
and less educated neighborhoods. The concept of
neighborhood can be substituted by the concept
of residential segregation—an archetypal example of
limited environments' effect on everyday life that
is thus linked to residents' health outcomes.
Conversely, the segregation of residential areas is a
representation of different characteristics among
neighborhoods. Recently, Caldwell et al. (2016,
2017) examined racial and ethnic residential segrega-
tion in terms of the health care opportunities

available in urban versus rural areas. The findings
highlighted race and ethnicity and geography as fac-
tors in the health disparity seen in rural areas, indi-
cating that residential segregation can aggravate the
accessibility to health care amenities. In particular,
certain racial and ethnic groups (e.g., African
Americans and Hispanics living in rural areas) are
marginalized from health care. According to Massey
and Denton (1988), residential segregation is the
degree to which two or more groups live separately
from one another in different parts of the urban
environment. Residential segregation emerges from
either voluntary or involuntary motivations (Charles
2003). Voluntary residential segregation involves a
group of people who purposefully reside together
based on their common preferences and culture—
for example, similarities in SES, educational level,
and race or ethnicity. The goal of voluntary residen-
tial segregation focuses on enhancing residents’ sat-
isfaction. In contrast, involuntary residential
segregation occurs when a lack of finances or infor-
mation drives people to live in an unfavorable region
(Bernard et al. 2007), reducing opportunities for
economic activity and limiting access to resources
and services (Williams and Collins 2001).
Ultimately, such adverse conditions negatively affect
the health outcomes of residents in involuntarily
segregated communities. Cozier et al. (2014) identi-
fied racial and ethnic status as an important indica-
tor of SES. Suspicions of a link among racial and
ethnic differences, genetic characteristics, and com-
munity environment (CDC 2018) have raised ques-
tions about the potential relationship between
obesity rate and residential segregation by racial and
ethnic group, because residential segregation is asso-
ciated with SES and environment as an underlying
cause of obesity in the United States. A traditional
approach to segregation measures usually focuses on
examining similarities and dissimilarities between
two groups, such as white versus black or white ver-
sus non-white. Such a dichotomy allows exploration
of whether segregation is present in the composition
of dominant racial and ethnic groups relative to
minorities, but this approach minimizes the signifi-
cance of other groups that might play an important
role in residential segregation. Although obesity has
been shown to have a strong relationship with resi-
dential segregation, virtually no studies have consid-
ered it in a rural–urban contrast. Although most
obesity research has focused on metropolitan areas
(Whitaker and Orzol 2006; Day et al. 2013; Cook
et al. 2017), it has ignored metropolitan areas’ dif-
fering levels of urbanization among subregions, as
well as rural areas’ differences in living conditions
and environment, such as racial and ethnic compo-
sition. For these reasons, the southeastern United
States is a suitable setting for exploring the relation-
ship of obesity and racial and ethnic segregation
from an urban–rural perspective.## Data and Methods

Adult obesity rate is defined as the percentage of the adult population aged twenty or older that reports a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m². The adult obesity data set for counties in the United States is derived from the County Health Rankings & Roadmaps database (CHRR) shared by the Population Health Institute of the University of Wisconsin (County Health Rankings 2018). Note that the CHRR data are compiled from various sources related to health, medical, and demographic characteristics and that the baseline spatial unit for the variables is aggregated at the county level. The original source of the adult obesity rate is the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) of the CDC.

Residential segregation data at the county level were collected from the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey (ACS) 5-Year Estimates of 2012 to 2016 (U.S. Census Bureau 2018). The data include a cross-sectional proportion of racial and ethnic groups at the county level for the entire United States. For this research, we reclassified the original racial and ethnic composition into four groups: (1) non-Hispanic white, (2) non-Hispanic black, (3) Hispanic, and (4) non-Hispanic others, including the subgroups Asian, Native Hawaiian, and other Pacific Islander, as well as some other races from Table B03002, “Hispanic of Latino origin by race.” This classification was made for exclusivity among racial and ethnic characteristics to correctly measure degree of segregation. Note that populations of “two or more races” are excluded from this research, because their total population is too small to be applied to the analysis. To ensure consistency of time period for both sets of data, 2015 adult obesity rates were used for this study, because this was the year that best matched the census ACS data set. All data sets were structured to the county level using geographic information systems (GIS). Finally, the classification scheme of urban and rural is made from the definition by the U.S. Census Bureau (Ratcliffe et al. 2016). The southeastern United States includes 704 rural (66.2 percent) and 359 urban (33.8 percent) counties (n = 1,063). To identify any interrelationship between obesity ratio and residential segregation, two methods are used: (1) two entropy indexes (LEI and GEI) to measure racial segregation and (2) a set of integrated interrelationship indexes called the OBESEG with which to examine the degree of geographic interrelationship between segregation and obesity. Both methods were used to identify areas of particular interest (e.g., prevalence areas) based on the magnitude of both criteria. The LEI and GEI essentially measured the degree of segregation of one group over other groups using individual observations or total number of observations (White 1986). A local index, LEI ($h_i$), is the index of segregation in the

county *i* of interest. As shown in Equation 1, the index is the log-sum of the proportion of a specific racial or ethnic group at spatial unit *i*. The spatial unit *i* is set as county *i* in this study. Note that the value of *h* ranges from 0 to ln(*k*), where *k* is the number of groups. If the value of county *i* is close to 0, then the racial and ethnic groups in county *i* are completely segregated compared to other counties. Conversely, a value approaching ln(*k*) indicates the perfect dis-segregation of the racial and ethnic groups in county *i* compared to other counties.

$$
h_i = - \sum_{j=1}^{k} p_{ij} \ln(p_{ij}), \quad (1)
$$

where *k* is the number of racial and ethnic groups, and $p_{ij}$ is the proportion of population of *j* racial or ethnic group in county *i*.

On a global scale, the GEI *H* informs the overall level of segregation in the southeastern United States including *j* counties. Equation 2 measures the variance between the entire entropy index for the region of interest and the population-weighted average of $h_i$ for all counties that are members of that region. The maximum value of *H* is 1, representing a region that is significantly segregated at the global level, whereas the minimum value, 0, indicates that all racial and ethnic groups are evenly distributed over the region of interest.

$$
H = \frac{(\hat{H} - \bar{H})}{\hat{H}}, \quad (2)
$$

where $\hat{H}$ is the entropy index for the whole unit (global scale), and $\bar{H}$ is the population-weighted average of $h_i$ for all counties *i* (local scale).

The second method, OBESEG, was designed to capture the interrelationship between obesity rate and residential segregation through a standardization process for the obesity rate and the residential segregation rate, respectively, by positioning each observation on a two-dimensional plane. The idea for OBESEG was driven by an integrated measure approach proposed by Kim and Song (2018) and Ye and Kim (2014), who combined multiple indexes into a single index to comprehensively assess an observation or event for input variables through a unique standardization procedure. The OBESEG was completed via a few steps. The first step involves standardizing the degree of obesity rate ($OBE_i$) and residential segregation rate ($SEG_i$) of county *i* into a fixed range [-1, 1]. The computations are shown in Equations 3 and 4.

$$
OBE_i = \left[ \frac{2(O_i^{\text{county}} - O_{\text{min}})}{O_{\text{max}} - O_{\text{min}}} \right] - 1 \quad (3)
$$

$$
SEG_i = \left[ \frac{2(S_i^{\text{county}} - S_{\text{min}})}{S_{\text{max}} - S_{\text{min}}} \right] - 1, \quad (4)
$$

where $O_i^{\text{county}}$ is the obesity rate at county *i*, $O_{\text{max}}$ is the highest obesity rate among all counties, $O_{\text{min}}$is the lowest obesity rate among all counties, $S_i^{\text{county}}$ is the residential segregation rate at county $i$, $S_{\text{max}}$ is the highest residential segregation value $b_i$ among all counties, and $S_{\text{min}}$ is the lowest residential segregation value $b_i$ among all counties.

As indicated by both equations, the original metric is used to standardize an observation $i$ from the value into the positionality range [-1, 1], with + indicating higher positionality among all observations, 0 the middle (even), and - lower positionality. For example, the county having the highest obesity rate is positioned at +1, whereas that having the lowest is placed at -1 in $OBE_i$. Others will be located along the scale in positions corresponding to their degree of variance from the lowest obesity rate ($O_{\text{min}}$) in relation to the difference between $O_{\text{max}}$ and $O_{\text{min}}$ in the original range. Next the standardized $OBE_i$ and $SEG_i$ of each county $i$ are coordinated on a two-dimensional plane $OBESEG_i$ where four quadrants (Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4) are created by two axes. The positionality of each county $i$ is coordinated in one of the quadrants. Q1 represents the plane associated with counties having a high prevalence of obesity and residential segregation rate (i.e., $OBESEG_i$ = [H, H]). As the counter quadrant to Q1, Q3 represents counties having low rates of obesity and residential segregation ($OBESEG_i$ = [L,

L]). As a mixed plane, Q2 displays counties having a low (-) obesity rate and high (+) residential segregation rate ($OBESEG_i$ = [L, H]), whereas Q4 corresponds to a high (+) obesity rate and low (-) segregation ($OBESEG_i$ = [H, L]). The advantage of the $OBESEG_i$ is that it enables joint assessment of a county's two metrics by removing the scale effects that result from use of different units or ranges. As a final measure, the $OBESEG$ determinant ($OBESEG^{\text{Det}}$) calculates the weighted centroid (i.e., the mean center) of $OBE_i$ and $SEG_i$ by Equation 5. The measure conveys the positionality of state $j$.

$$
OBESEG_j^{\text{Det}} = [\bar{x}, \bar{y}] = \left( \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} OBE_i}{N}, \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} SEG_i}{N} \right), \qquad (5)
$$

where $i$ is the index of counties of state $j$, $j$ is the index of the state, and $N$ is the number of counties at state $j$.

## Conclusions

Although possible interrelationships of obesity and residential segregation have been suggested, virtually no previous studies have clearly revealed such a relationship between them or identified areas of critical concern. This study demonstrates that a set of proposed measures in conjunction with the urban–rural perspective and different geographic scales (state and clusters) reveals an underlying relationship between obesity rates and residential segregation. As presented in the results, significant interrelationships, in varying degrees, can be uncovered depending on the method selected. In this study, the southeastern United States was selected to examine the interrelationships, because the region is known for a greater prevalence of chronic diseases and degree of residential segregation. In sum, high–high and low–low levels of obesity and residential segregation relationship are well identified at different geographic scales—the state and cluster levels—from an urban–rural perspective using the proposed measures. Identification of such clusters using these measures has important policy implications, because the identified clusters typically have a single prevalent racial or ethnic composition and bring concerns about health disparities between urban and rural areas. Finally, according to this study, the proposed measures could be useful when researchers explore how unique combinations of socioeconomic factors are explicitly interrelated at various times and geographic scales. ■
