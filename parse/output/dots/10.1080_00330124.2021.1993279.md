# Policing the Global Countryside: Toward a Research Agenda

Richard Yarwood

University of Plymouth, UK

This article provides an overview of work that has examined the geographies of crime and policing in rural areas. Using policing in the United Kingdom as a jumping-off point, it outlines the significance of organized crime in rural places and argues that global, rather than community-based, perspectives are needed in its study and policing. It concludes by drawing attention to the value of Southern criminology in developing a research agenda to understand the geographies of rural policing. **Key Words:** crime, globalization, policing, rural, Southern criminology.

The rise in rural crime has come about due to two main factors. Social and economic change has seen the number of farms fall and close-knit communities collapse. Modern transport links now enable thieves to steal farm machinery and move it into mainland Europe in a matter of hours.

—NFU Mutual (2017), quoted in National Police Chief's Council (2018a, 3)

This quote is taken from the introduction of the Rural Affairs Strategy of the United Kingdom's National Police Chief's Council. It appears to blame crime on the loss of “traditional” rural structures and a subsequent “disintegration” of society¹ (Carrington, Hogg, and Sozzo 2016), something that Pahl (1968) might have considered an “uncritical glorifying of old-fashioned rural life” (265). Although geographical research on rural crime has challenged the social ecological basis of this rhetoric (Barclay 2007; Bottoms and Wiles 2007; Donnermeyer and DeKeseredy 2013; Ceccato 2015a; Donnermeyer 2016; Hollis and Hankhouse 2019), these ideas continue to influence the policing of the countryside with important consequences (Young 1993; Yarwood and Gardner 2000).

Although the quote acknowledges the connected, globalized nature of the countryside (Woods 2007), the study and policing of rural crime has remained rather insular by continuing to focus largely on “the distinctive character of crime in rural and regional locales” (Carrington, Hogg, and Sozzo 2016, 4) and the role of communities in policing them (Yarwood 2010; Ceccato 2015b). In turn, this can neglect some crimes, contribute to social exclusion, and support a view that communities are the cause and solution of rural problems (Desforges, Jones, and Woods 2005; Cheshire 2016).

To illustrate this point, this article uses the policing of rural crime in the United Kingdom as a jumping-off point to argue that geographers and those tasked with policing the countryside should take a global view of the countryside (Woods 2007) to better understand the nature and geographies of rural crime.

## Rural Policing in the United Kingdom

Increasingly, rural policing in the United Kingdom is being driven by concern about thefts from agricultural premises (National Police Chief's Council 2018a). Surveys by The National Rural Crime Network (2018) have revealed that rural businesses have suffered high rates of victimization (Table 1) and, in 2019, the National Farmers' Union (NFU) reported increases in thefts of agricultural vehicles (£9.3 million in insurance claims), quad bikes (£3.1 million), livestock (£3 million), and, specifically, Land Rover Defenders² (£2.1 million; National Farmers' Union Mutual 2020; see Figure 1).

Although further empirical research is needed to establish the significance and geographies of these networks (McElwee and Smith 2015), the United Kingdom's Rural Affairs Strategy (National Police Chief's Council 2018a) is focused almost entirely on crimes against farms (Table 2). Other crimes that occur in rural areas but are not specifically rural, such as thefts from homes (Yarwood 2005) or domestic violence (Little 2017), are the subject of separate strategies (National Police Chief's Council 2018a, 2018b). Elsewhere, the National Farmers' Union and National Farmers' Union Cymru (2015) also suggested that “rural communities also perceive ‘no go areas’ for police and a lack of action against certain individuals and groups who are well known to the police as repeat offenders” (3). Although particular groups are not explicitly mentioned, existing work suggests that some minority groups, such as travelers, young people, or migrants, are associated with criminality on the basis of difference or simply being “out of place” in the country (Yarwood and Gardner 2000; Holloway 2003; Vanderbeck 2003; Askins 2009). At the same time, increases in crime have been

widely attributed to organized crime (National Police Chief's Council 2018a) and local gangs (National Rural Crime Network 2018) that “target and exploit rural communities across a range of crime types, for example organized plant theft, livestock theft, burglaries targeting firearms, poaching and hare coursing” (National Police Chief's Council 2018a, 6).

Although organized crime against property is seen as the predominant threat, crimes against people outside rural communities seem to be given less attention. Yet, neglected “others” (Philo 1992) are more likely to be victims of organized crime, such as human trafficking, slavery, and drug dealing. These groups have little voice (certainly compared to the powerful NFU lobby) and little recourse to justice.There is therefore a need to take a much wider, more critical view of rural policing that moves beyond property crimes and considers a much wider range of people in the countryside. It also challenges us to move from policing rural localities to the policing of the global countryside.

## The Global Countryside: From Places to Flows of Crime

Woods (2007) considered the globalized countryside to be “knitted-together intersections of networks and flows that are never wholly fixed or contained at the local scale, and whose constant shape-shifting eludes a singular representation of place” (499). Rural places are not easily identifiable, bounded communities; rather, they are constantly being “thrown together” by “constellations of processes” (Massey 2005, 141). Although geographers have taken up this call to understand rurality and globalization, their attention has mainly focused on legal activities (Carrington et al. 2018). Hall (2013) argued that “discussions of contemporary processes such as globalization, regulation and economic liberalization which admit only licit actors offer both empirically and conceptually limited readings of those processes” (380). He argued that a focus on organized crime groups not only reveals much about the spatiality and regulation of global crime but also sheds light on the processes, flows, and impacts of globalization. Three examples are outlined in the following sections (for full reviews, see Carrington, Hogg, and Sozzo 2016; Donnermeyer 2017).

### Drug Trafficking

Rural places are important in the production, distribution, and consumption of illicit drugs to the extent that this accounts for “8% of world trade, similar in size to the textile industry” (Hall 2013, 370). Despite international interventions as part of a “war on drugs” (Rengert 1996), the cultivation of drugs continues to make a significant contribution to the rural economies and livelihoods of both the Global North and Global South (Weisheit and Brownstein 2016). Thus, 3.3 million people in Afghanistan derive income from the production of opium poppies for “their comparative advantages and multifunctional role in relation to other crops and ... because it provides access to land and credit (Goodhand 2005, 207). This produce is connected by significant but illicit networks of technology, culture, and finance to other places (Barton, Storey, and Palmer 2010), rendering the countryside a “space of flows” and revealing “shifting forces of global capital” (Clark, Fraser, and Hamilton-Smith 2021, 259). This is reflected in the growth of “county lines” drug dealing in which urban gangs

recruit vulnerable young people in rural and coastal settlements to distribute drugs. This has been driven by crowded urban markets and networks that have prompted dealers to move out of cities to new markets in rural localities (Harding 2020). These shifts are made possible by a global supply of drugs, new technologies, and social media, allowing dealers to exert control over networks rather than territories.

### Slavery

A second example is illustrated in the global networks of forced labor and slavery. The International Labour Organization (2017) estimates that nearly 25 million people are in forced labor and, of these, 11 percent work in agriculture or fishing. This includes forms of bonded labor (when people become indentured due to debt), child slavery, forced marriage, descent slavery (being born into slavery), and human trafficking (Byrne and Smith 2016). Transnational trafficking makes illicit links between the Global North and Global South, reflecting that rural areas are both the suppliers and receivers of immigrant labor (Woods 2007; D. P. Smith 2018). Although migrant labor is regulated by the state (Rye and Slettebak 2020), illicit human trafficking exploits vulnerable and powerless groups by paying no or low wages, stealing passports, and deducting money for substandard accommodation (Allamby et al. 2011). This is compounded because workers might be unaware of their rights (Rye and Slettebak 2020), afraid of authority, or isolated by language, rendering them powerless to complain (Allamby et al. 2011).

Strauss (2012) contended that trafficking for forced labor has been driven by “increasing interconnections between the demand for flexible, low cost labor in the industrialized economies and a supply of workers unable to commodify their labor at home” (137–38). Findlay and McCollum (2013) also stated that the increasingly complex subcontracting of labor means farmers might be unable, or unwilling, to delve into the conditions of workers. At the same time, exploitation of farmers by supermarkets and competition from global supply chains that might themselves enroll exploitative practices pressures farmers to use cheap or perhaps illicit labor to maintain marginal businesses (Strauss 2012). Globalization is therefore a cause of modern slavery, but it has also been shaped by it. Slavery is not only hidden but its victims are often powerless. It is imperative that rural policing strategies look to counter these forms of organized crime and empower its victims to find justice.

### More-Than-Human Crimes

Woods (2007) stressed that globalization creates “hybrid assemblages of human and non-human entities” (499), binding people, animals, nature, andtechnology into international networks. If the study and policing of criminality is limited to crimes against people, this will lead to a partial understanding of rural crime as well as the changing nature of rural space (Enticott 2011). A reframing of green or environmental criminology (South 2017) in a global context has the potential to bring radical, less anthropocentric perspectives to the study of criminology (Carrington, Hogg, and Sozzo 2016). Poaching, by way of example, links regions of the Global South to urban markets of the Global North and has promoted military interventions by local and global actors (Lunstrum 2014).

Crimes against wildlife have indeed received increased attention (Fyfe and Reeves 2011; Potter, Nurse, and Hall 2016; National Police Chief's Council 2018b) to the extent that the National Police Chief's Council (2018b) referred to the *persecution* of wildlife, a term often used to describe hate crimes. Its use in this context recognizes the significance of crimes against nature as well the unequal power relations between society and nature that can lead to them.

Enticott (2011) suggested that wildlife crime reflects the relationship between contested ideas of rurality and criminality. Thus, poaching, although illegal, has formed part of rural imagination and identity likewise; hunting is highly contested and regulated yet central to some “folk” lifestyles (White 2016). Farmers therefore might engage in illegal culls as they are justified as necessary and tacitly accepted by some parts of the rural community (Enticott 2011), making it difficult to police. This emphasizes that rural places are not only affected by crime networks but may play a significant role within them. Other work has suggested that some farms can be used as fronts for drug production or illegal commodity chains (Donnermeyer 2017). R. Smith and McElwee (2013) also pointed out that some farmers also enroll themselves in organized crime groups to generate income through, for example, engaging in the illegal meat trade. Such crimes, they contended, go unnoticed or underreported due to social norms and community pressures to “conform, ‘keep the peace’ and avoid making accusations” (R. Smith and McElwee 2013, 116). It is therefore important to recognize how social constructions and performances of rurality are crucial to understanding how rural places are enrolled into networks of global crime.

## Conclusions

This article has argued that there is a tension between the global, multiscalar nature of rural crime and the often parochial ways in which rural crime is considered and policed. Based on this brief review, it is possible to identify three key areas for research.

### New Theoretical Perspectives

Recent anthologies reveal that research on rural crime has focused almost exclusively on the Global North (Mawby and Yarwood 2010; Donnermeyer and DeKeseredy 2013; Donnermeyer 2016; Harkness 2020), reflecting a myopia in both criminology (Friedrichs 2007) and rural geography (Milbourne 2017. It is not without irony that this article started with a discussion of British policing.) Despite the greater significance of rural populations and land use to the Global South (Weisheit 2016; Donnermeyer 2017), Carrington, Hogg, and Sozzo (2016) argued that the study of crime has been dominated by a “metropolitan criminology” that, among other things, has overlooked “major historical and contemporary forms and trends in criminal justice practice outside the metropolitan centers of the northern hemisphere” (3). This has not only sidelined rural areas but, more important, has taken for granted “a high level of internal peace within ... a stable nation-state system” (Carrington, Hogg, and Sozzo 2016, 3). This has not only privileged Northern perspectives, voices, and knowledge but has perhaps focused the study of crime within the boundaries of (Northern) states and ignored global connections between and across states.

“Southern criminology” has started to challenge these orthodoxies. It attempts to decolonize the production of knowledge to destabilize established binaries and empower those who are seldom given voice (Connell 2014; Moosavi 2019; Dimou 2021). In recognizing geographical differences, it seeks not only to refocus attention on the Global South but also to recognize how it has been shaped by colonial and neocolonial processes and, in doing so, highlights the connections between places (Ciocchini and Greener 2021). This is not only leading to a reconsideration of the ways in which farm crime is studied (Donnermeyer 2017) but, more important, is refocusing attention on those who have been neglected in the study of rural crime. These include women (Walklate 2018), indigenous people (Cunneen 2018), racial minorities (Currie 2018), and those, such as migrant workers, hitherto hidden or ignored in rural places (Carrington, Hogg, and Sozzo 2016). Crucially, Southern criminology challenges the hegemony of the Global North by calling for research to be by, rather than of or about, those from the Global South and minority groups (Connell 2014).

Research into the geographies of rural crime should embrace these perspectives to better understand differentiated experiences of crime and rurality. Key to this should be a consideration of rural space as networked and porous, rather than bounded and isolated.

### Global Perspectives in Policing

Policing continues to reflect class or economic interest and particular visions of rurality, and itperpetuates the idea that crime comes from “outside” rural communities. Despite the global nature of crime, rural policing continues to emphasize the idea of community (Kappeler and Gaines 2012; Ceccato 2015a), in part to compensate for scarce resources and the rollback of state policing (Donnermeyer 2016). Given the globalization of the countryside and the threat of organized crime, there is a need for joined-up forms of rural policing that, in common with other developments in rural policy, recognize a wider range of issues that cause farm crime and, most significant, look outside bounded communities. Policing should be driven by a form of global rather than local citizenship that recognizes how rural places are connected to and affected by other places (Desforges, Jones, and Woods 2005). This should encourage people to consider distant others by, for example, being alert to forms of slavery in their own localities. Neighborhood Watch might, for example, encompass a wider definition of neighbors in this context (Painter 2012).

## *Places Still Matter*

In February 2018 a group of about 100 migrant workers protested outside a police station in Helston, Cornwall (BBC 2018). Earlier the police had arrested three men as part of an operation against modern slavery. The Romanian and Latvian protestors had been working as flower-pickers in the local fields and living in accommodations on a Farm. The protesters adamantly denied that they were slaves or were being exploited but, instead, demanded the release of the three men. The arrests were well-intended and reflected a growing awareness of slave labor in rural areas. It appeared to misinterpret the situation, however, and, crucially, neglect the voices of those deemed to be victims of slavery. As (Donnermeyer 2017) noted, “farm crime is global ... but the context of farm crime is not” (123). Woods (2007) took pains to point out that globalization is best thought of as an incomplete series of events rather than a finished product: “local actors engage with global networks and global forces to produce hybrid outcomes” (497). As geographers, we should continue to be sensitive to place, recognizing the capacity of different people to act in different ways. Indeed, it is this appreciation of a global sense of place (Massey 1991) that will allow geographers to make important contributions to Southern criminology and, in turn, give voice to those most affected by rural crime and those most in need of its policing. ■
