# In the Shadow of the Green Revolution: Constrained Spatial Imaginaries and Smallholder Farming in Guatemala's Pacific Lowlands

**Birgit Schmook**

*El Colegio de la Frontera Sur (ECOSUR), Mexico*

**Claudia Radel**

*Utah State University, USA*

**Lindsey Carte**


*Universidad de la Frontera, Chile*

**Richard L. Johnson**

*University of Arizona, USA*

Much smallholder farming currently exists side by side with large-scale, industrial agriculture, as an outcome of the Green Revolution unfolding in uneven and dualistic terms. In Guatemala's Pacific lowlands, state incentives fuel expansion of industrial export agriculture. Large-scale sugarcane production has come to surround rural farming communities, where small-scale farmers grow maize and sesame with little state support. Drawing on a survey of nearly 200 households, seven-teen in-depth interviews, and four focus groups in five communities during 2014 and 2015 in the Department of Retalhuleu, our research explores the realities and imaginaries of smallholders as they negotiate their incorporation into the Green Revolution. In the face of minuscule agricultural plots resulting in limited production, cycles of agrarian debt, vulnerability to drought, and emigration to the United States, smallholders paradoxically work hard to replicate “modern” Green Revolution approaches in their own parcels. We argue that the proximal, uneven expansion of modern technocentric agriculture, fueled by the model of the Green Revolution, has helped cultivate “constrained spatial imaginaries” among smallholders, in which ideas and desires around improved agricultural practices remain bound to accessing hybrid seeds, chemical inputs, and technical extension, despite the way the Green Revolution has been implicated in their ongoing marginalization. **Key Words:** agrarian neoliberalism, constrained imaginaries, industrial agriculture, Pacific lowlands Guatemala, smallholder.

The transformation of peasant agriculture into capitalist agricultural production to address hunger and poverty, and keep pace with growing world population, was at the core of the Green Revolution (GR) that began in the early 1960s. Characterized by development and use of new crop varieties, increased use of synthetic inputs such as fertilizers and pesticides, and expanded irrigation, the GR was pushed by international and national institutions and implemented through extension services, subsidies, and agricultural inputs marketing. These multilevel pressures and promises sparked some initial hope and enthusiasm among farmers for improved yields and livelihoods (Wright 2010; Chhetri and Chaudhary 2011; Isakson 2014; Alonso-Fradejas 2015; Baranski and Ollenburger 2020). The GR has indeed enabled increased global food production and, through greater intensification, spared some land from being brought into cultivation

(Pingali 2012; Stevenson et al. 2013). Following decades of implementation, however, scholars have come to understand how the GR has unfolded unevenly both spatially and socially, increasing social inequality in many rural contexts and provoking significant, well-documented negative impacts on human and ecological health (Pimentel and Pimentel 1990; Kerr 2012; Niazi 2004).

In this article, we examine smallholder agricultural practices and experiences in one Guatemalan case study location and how they continue to be shaped, and even constrained, by input-intense modern agricultural approaches promoted by the GR and the subsequent expansion of industrial agriculture in a neoliberal economic policy environment. Examining how smallholders position themselves relative to expanding GR logics and practices remains crucial to understanding how the GR continues to reshape agrarian livelihoods and dynamicsat different scales. Based on this case study, we argue that, in some contexts, current landscapes of industrial agriculture, as one GR outcome, can result in smallholders experiencing “constrained spatial imaginaries” with respect to their own production.

The GR and its technologies are tied to an assortment of narratives around progress and modernity, hunger and poverty. As such, farming families in poorer countries have, since the beginning of the GR, been subjected to incorporation into its vision and specific presumptions of human-environment relations in agricultural systems. Scholars continue to debate how smallholders incorporate the GR's highly standardized cultivation prescriptions for market surplus production into their dynamic agrobiodiverse systems (Bellon and Hellin 2011; Kansanga et al. 2019; Vercillo, Weis, and Luginaah 2020). Yet, from the beginning, many scholars have expressed concern regarding the outcomes of these farming practices for smallholders. Geographer Carl Sauer observed and appreciated over a long period the practices of small-scale farmers in Mexico. Based on his observations, he flagged the dangers of GR agriculture for Mexico's farmers and local economies, particularly regarding the elimination of native plant varieties and sociocultural practices: “A good aggressive bunch of American agronomists and plant-breeders could ruin the native resources for good and all by pushing their American commercial stocks. ... Mexican agriculture cannot be pointed toward standardization on a few commercial types without upsetting native economy and culture hopelessly”¹ (cited in Harwood 2009, 390). Indeed, farmer movements have emerged around the world, and especially in Latin America, opposing industrial, GR-based agriculture and positing alternative agroecological approaches based on food sovereignty (Holt-Gimenez and Altieri 2013; Copeland 2019b). These noteworthy efforts are not universal, however. In some rural spaces, GR implementation over decades has captured smallholders' agricultural productive practices as well as their imaginaries of desirable agriculture. With limited room to maneuver or space for alternatives under a GR-driven, agro-industrial model, what some smallholders now express as “desirable” appears to be conditioned by what is immediately “possible.”

In Guatemala's Pacific lowlands, industrial sugarcane production has notably expanded over the past decade with numerous social and environmental problems for smallholders. Nonetheless, many smallholders still hope to benefit themselves from the promises of modern agricultural technologies. Our research in five smallholder farming communities in and around the Pacific lowland Aldea of Caballo Blanco in the Department of Retalhuleu explores the realities of small-scale farming families surrounded by expanding sugarcane cultivation on

large consolidating tracts of land, with little to no state support for their own production. Analysis of interview and survey data collected in 2014 and 2015 (with revisit in 2018) captures the lived experiences of these families as they persist and negotiate their condition.

Research in geography has identified the role of space and spatial relations in the formation of imaginaries. Wolford (2004) advanced the concept of “spatial imaginaries” in agrarian contexts to describe “cognitive frameworks, both collective and individual, constituted through the lived experiences, perceptions, and conceptions of space itself” (410) among rural workers and producers. Watkins (2015) further described spatial imaginaries as both discursive and performative—“ways of talking about places and spaces” that are ultimately “embodied performances by people in the material world” (509). Our own research expands on this concept to highlight the constrained or contested nature of spatial imaginaries, in which what is considered desirable is also conditioned by what is perceived as materially possible. In the case of smallholders on Guatemala's Pacific lowlands, desires for improved agrarian practices are expressed in terms of greater access to high-input (chemical) agriculture, despite general lament for lost farming practices of the past. Our research illustrates how smallholder spatial imaginaries can shift and become constrained under GR logics and material conditions, which ultimately help reproduce associated GR practices.

## The Guatemalan Context

The U.S. Agency for International Development started to promote GR technologies in Guatemala in the 1960s (Carey 2009), where they fell on fertile ground. In the first half of the twentieth century, Guatemala's Liberal governments strove to achieve recognition as a modern state (Carey 2009). GR technologies and practices represented progress and modernization (Wright 2010) and arrived to a country torn by political-ideological divisions. Anticommunist sentiment among agrarian and other political elites solidified in response to President Arbenz's land redistribution efforts in the early 1950s. After the 1954 U.S.-led coup disposed of Arbenz and reversed agrarian reforms, leaders of the military government and the conservative Catholic Church promoted adoption of GR technologies (Carey 2009).

Organizations like the Ford Foundation and the Rockefeller Foundation, which were instrumental internationally in financing the development of the new seed varieties, believed that GR technology would increase agricultural productivity and production, reducing hunger and increasing rural prosperity to make left-wing political ideologies less attractive to farmers. Many believed that modernizing agriculturein third world countries would also spread Western political ideas. As historian Keith Griffin observed, “Technical progress was regarded as an alternative to land reforms” (cited in Carey 2009, 293).

Despite promises of universal prosperity through creation of agricultural surplus, new varieties and technologies benefited big landowners more than smallholders in Guatemala, similar to elsewhere (Patel 2013). Input-intensive cultivation techniques provided landed elites with the tools to expand their production, increase the country's economic growth, and ignore smallholder demands for land reform or economic justice. Where smallholders did receive support, it was geared largely toward encouraging transitions to input-intensive, nontraditional export crops (specifically vegetables) at the expense of traditional milpa production² (Carey 2009; see also Arias 1990; Falla 2001). Austerity measures from neoliberal reforms in the 1980s and 1990s further reduced state attention to smallholder production (Carey 2009). Current neoliberal state policies and development programs hasten cultivar genetic diversity loss, environmental degradation, and smallholder dependence on inputs, grain imports, and wage labor, with heightened debt burdens (Carey 2009; Isakson 2014). In addition, greater exposure to global commodity markets and the absence of state support have forced smallholders to suffer under market volatility and poor returns (Isakson 2014).

Guatemala's historic land access inequalities remain intact. The last census to collect data on land access, from 2003, shows that the top 2 percent (in terms of size) controlled 57 percent of the country's arable land, whereas the bottom two thirds controlled 7 percent (Instituto Nacional de Estadística 2004). The state's strategy to alleviate historic land access inequalities through market-led land reform has had minimal impact in transferring lands to smallholders (Gauster and Isakson 2007). Although some smallholders in the Pacific lowlands own or have access to more land than average households in Guatemala's highlands, many struggle under challenging market conditions (Tomei 2015). Increased experience of climate change impacts such as drought further aggravates smallholder livelihoods (Hellin et al. 2018; Vargas et al. 2018; Donatti et al. 2019). After the 1996 peace accords, Guatemalan social movements were weak and divided and struggled to shape an inequitable and increasingly neoliberal agrarian landscape. This and other factors mean no socioeconomic reforms have challenged rural inequalities (Klepek 2012). The increasingly hostile situation for smallholders has precipitated substantial increase in rural migration to the United States.

In the Pacific lowlands, with around 14 percent of Guatemala's population, half live in poverty and 10 percent in extreme poverty (Tomei 2015). The expansion of industrial agriculture, especially

sugarcane production, is one of numerous materializations of the enduring GR project. The last two decades have witnessed even more aggressive advancement, with national and transnational corporations expanding here and elsewhere in Guatemala into lands previously cultivated by smallholders for other crops like maize (Alonso-Fradejas 2012, 2015). New investments in sugar emerged from national policies incentivizing biofuel production. According to a 2018 U.S. Department of Agriculture report, Guatemala is the fourth largest sugar exporter globally (Tay 2018). State support for biofuels and other industrially produced crops contrasts sharply with the absence of substantive investment in smallholder production and rural communities (Díaz 2012; Aguilar-Støen and Hirsch 2015; Copeland 2019a). Tomei (2015) noted that sugar mills manage around 80 percent of the sugarcane estates on the Pacific lowlands; they own some of the land and lease from other landowners. As many smallholders have traditionally rented surplus parcels from larger landowners for maize cultivation, the expansion of sugarcane production has reduced smallholder land access. This loss of access and concomitant inability to grow enough food for subsistence or markets has resulted in increasing vulnerability to climate change and price swings, as well as dependence on migration remittances (Carte et al. 2019) and wage labor on the sugarcane estates (among other enterprises) that deprived them of land access in the first place (Tomei 2015).

The decline of subsistence agriculture and land access, along with prolonged repression, corruption, and exploitation by the Guatemalan state, U.S. government, and transnational capital, have given rural communities little autonomy to enact alternatives (Copeland 2014). According to Copeland (2014), the resulting manifestation of a neoliberal democracy has created an “affective imaginary of defeat” in Guatemalan society (see also McAllister 2003). Likewise, agricultural development has not fostered widespread improvements in agriculture and livelihoods for smallholders but rather has exacerbated existing inequities and produced precarity. How, then, do smallholders position themselves in relation to “modern” agriculture? How do they envision their own agricultural aspirations in the shadow of the GR's material realities and logics? Do their spatial imaginations reflect this “affective imaginary of defeat”?

## Results

### *Smallholders' Livelihoods: Household Survey Results*

Survey data indicate that the majority of livelihoods in the studied Caballo Blanco communities depend on farming for both direct household consumption of maize and the generation of cash income through sale of maize surplus and of sesame. Sixty-eight percent of surveyed households engage regularly in farming. Remittances sent home by migrating family members support farming (see later), along with household consumption, as do cash earnings by other household members. Many men earn meager wages through day labor, as *jornaleros*, often in the fields of neighbors or nearby plantations and ranches. As we have published elsewhere (Carte et al. 2019), many of these farming households have low access to food throughout the year, with March and April (the apex of the dry season) being the most food-scarce months. For example, 83 percent of nonmigrant households reported food availability

in the home to be poor or very scarce during the month of April. By April, earnings from farming have been largely expended and local agricultural wage labor opportunities also end. Labor migration alleviates hunger for some households but not for all (Carte et al. 2019).

Farming occurs on small parcels often surrounded by the larger fields of sugarcane. The total area a typical family⁵ cultivates is just over a hectare on average (Table 1), due to land access constraints. Almost all surveyed farming households reported input-intensive practices on a single parcel of land, without fallow. Maize is planted in a first production cycle, with harvest in August and September. Most families then follow maize with sesame in the same field (Table 1), with harvest in November and December. Only a handful of surveyed households reported growing other crops, including beans, sweet potato, cucumber, rice, sorghum, and fruit. Many families have gardens and fruit trees, like mango and coconut, around their homes to supplement consumption. Some families (5 percent) with larger than average landholdings do also maintain some pasture for cattle, in addition to cultivating crops (Table 1).

Survey data demonstrate that farming families in Caballo Blanco attempt to replicate the strategies promoted by the GR. Hybrid seeds are purchased annually for both maize and sesame cultivation. Almost all families plant maize as a monocrop. Only 11 percent of households reported planting a companion crop with their maize, such as squash, beans, or cucumbers (Table 1), as would be common with traditional *milpa* cultivation of maize. Virtually every survey respondent detailed the use of purchased commercial fertilizers and pesticides (herbicides and insecticides). Resultant yields are perceived byfarmers as low, although the average maize yield (3.7 mt/ha) is higher than the approximately 2 mt/ha reported for small-scale maize farming in general in Guatemala (Tay 2019). The average reported ses-ame yield (0.56 mt/ha) is near the global average (0.51 mt/ha) but on the lower end of sesame-produc-ing countries (Myint et al. 2020). In many years, crop losses can be substantial, with drought, flood-ing, or increased pest occurrence. As reported in the survey, farmers attribute loss to growing conditions (rains and pests) but also equally to market and input conditions (the price they receive, their access to credit and inputs) that shape their lack of suffi-cient access to GR technologies.

Trapped in the GR: Focus Group Revelations

Until the 1980s, smallholders in the Caballo Blanco communities practiced traditional polycultural *milpa* agriculture and many had a few head of cattle. Participants explained that with the old *milpa*, crops were easier to produce and required less interven-tion. During a focus group, one woman commented, “It wasn’t hard to grow tomatoes [like today], you would only have to go and cut them.” Other women agreed, noting that the plants, “grew without even having to plant the seed.” No chemical or organic fertilizers were applied, “not even fertilizer; land only needed to be cleared and things grew.” Harvests from the milpa were complemented by abundant resources of fish, freshwater shrimp and iguanas, and backyard chickens.

According to community members, the abun-dance associated with that period ended in the 1980s with the introduction of hybrid maize seeds that replaced heirloom varieties or *semillas criollas*. Participants recalled that the seeds were introduced after visits from “people like” the authors (i.e., scien-tists), from companies like “Bayer or Monsanto” who “came to convince the people.” One woman, a community elder, explained, “If we would have con-tinued growing our crops the way we did before, we would still have the old maize variety. People let themselves be convinced by these companies too easily and then started to buy their seeds that sup-posedly would give a higher output of maize.” This created considerable shifts in agriculture for com-munity members. For one, seeds could no longer be saved. Farmers also needed to start using herbicides and fertilizers. This introduction marked the demise of *milpa* polyculture in the region: Companion crops could not survive the application of chemical inputs, especially herbicides, necessary to secure the higher maize yields promised by new seeds (Vigouroux et al. 2011; Schmook et al. 2013; Mascorro-de Loera et al. 2019; see Nyanga, Johnsen, and Kalinda [2012] for the same phenomena in maize cultivation in Zambia). Around the same time hybrid seeds arrived, farmers stopped cultivating maize during

two seasonal cycles and instead replaced the second cycle with sesame, which serves exclusively as a cash crop, in addition to a first cycle of maize primarily for consumption and, yield permitting, sale of sur-plus. Little by little, smallholder crop diversity dwin-dled, and smallholders now cultivate only a few crops in monoculture, as reflected in our quantita-tive data. Participants define the *milpa* today as just maize and sesame and in general lamented the loss of diverse maize landraces and polycultural and low-external-input farming techniques. They also view their participation in this system as necessary, how-ever: The hybrid maize is desired by the market; the traditional varieties “are no longer ‘good maize,’ the people say.”

Although most recognize that there was an increase in yields with these new seeds, the shift to buying seeds and chemical inputs also meant greater up-front cash investment, along with the consider-able impact on family food security and sovereignty. As one woman put it, “From when this started until today, there are already several types of seeds that have higher yields, but what doesn’t yield is the money to buy them.” A middle-aged man com-plained, “Here, instead of making profits, we have losses. Here we work because we want to work. What’s left is only enough to be able to live, because to say ‘I made a profit of 50,000 quetzales [around $6,500], I have enough to buy something,’ that doesn’t happen anymore.”

The shift from prior “traditional” to today’s “modern” agricultural strategies, and the simulta-neous abandonment of smallholder farmers from the calculus of agricultural development (as discussed earlier), means that farming families now depend on private loans to finance purchase of inputs, like seeds and pesticides, as well as for land rental. One farmer explained that they survive by

borrowing in order to produce because we don’t have our own money. The first time we planted, we already had the land plowed, and because we rent land, I said to my mom, “We already rented the land, now what are we going to do?” We took out a loan to plant, around 2,000 quetzales [around $260].

High interest rates, reported by interviewees as around 5 to 15 percent per month depending on the source of the loan, coupled with losses and lower yields, which have become more frequent due to rainfall irregularities, often make it difficult to repay these debts. One woman repeated a common refrain in the communities: “Sometimes people plant, they don’t have money and either they harvest or they fall into debt and decide to leave [to the United States] to get their family ahead and pay the debts that are accrued for seeds or lack of rains.” The accrual of agricultural debt is one of several factorsthat, woven together, drive migration to the United States (see also Johnson 2021). With few options for wage labor, besides selling land, of which the majority own very little, migration seems to be the main option to liberate families from cycles of debt. Agricultural debt is compounded by debt acquired to pay for health care and migration itself—in most cases, migrants must finance expensive journeys to the United States through high-interest informal mortgages (Johnson and Woodhouse 2018).

## Discussion and Conclusions: Smallholders’ Constrained Imaginaries

The adoption of newly bred crops accompanied by increased use of synthetic fertilizers, water, and pesticides was a hallmark of GR in Guatemala and other Latin American and Asian countries. Accompanying policies, such as extension and marketing services, and input subsidies facilitated farmer adoption (Chhetri and Chaudhary 2011). Traditional peasant cropping patterns, such as mixed cropping of cereals and pulses, were frequently replaced by monocropping. During this process, much peasant cropping knowledge and landraces in many locations were lost, resulting in drastic reduction of agrobiodiversity (Jacques and Jacques 2012). GR’s focus on a few cereal crops in monoculture was unsuccessful in fostering food security and poverty reduction in many contexts. On the contrary, it has promoted farmers’ dependence on expensive synthetic inputs, increased water consumption, and undermined farmers’ abilities to respond adequately to the challenges of climate change (Carey 2009; Kahane et al. 2013; Ebel et al. 2017).

Despite these general trends, scholars have noted unevenness in GR processes and smallholder responses. For example, there are cases of smallholders who grow improved varieties for the market while continuing to grow landraces for domestic use (see Bellon and Hellin [2011] for research in Mexico). Agrobiodiversity contributes significantly to ecological stability and resilience of cropping systems and their productivity (Kahane et al. 2013), and many farmers’ movements around the world now encourage agroecological practices, including ones in Guatemala (Copeland 2019b). In Caballo Blanco, the Comité de Desarrollo Campesino (Campesino Development Committee) is the most visible organizing entity, promoting an integrated vision of development that includes some extent of agroecological approaches. In general, however, these types of social movements—many of which promote a more creative or hybridized approach to benefiting from “modern” agricultural technologies—are less present here. The nongovernmental organization Semilla Nueva also works with farmers in this area of Guatemala, promoting (among other

technologies) improved maize seed varieties that could serve the interests of farmers.

In Caballo Blanco, larger yields based on adoption of GR approaches have not translated into increased income or well-being for most farmers. Livelihoods and testimonies attest to the numerous challenges and pitfalls of contemporary production, especially in relation to remembered practices of the past. At the same time, the 2018 community meetings revealed that many village residents do not see smallholder hardship and displacement as connected to local transitions to current farming practices. Local ideas for improving smallholder livelihoods are based on deepening technocentric approaches and external inputs. Smallholders and community leaders expressed a sense of unfulfilled productive potential as farmers, arguing that with additional state support in the form of inputs, extension, credit, and market access, they, too, could enhance yields and incomes from their parcels. In view of state support for the neighboring large-scale sugarcane plantations, smallholders saw their condition as the result of government corruption, in which the state overlooked their needs while funneling resources to industrial agricultural projects. Participants accordingly argued that a better future was possible through more equitable access to chemical inputs and scientific technologies. It is in this sense that smallholder imaginaries are constrained, by what farmers perceive as possible and not possible. This demand for equality of access and support imagines a different political future while accepting a specific agricultural future.

Our results illuminate Caballo Blanco’s smallholders’ paradoxical desires for continued adoption of “modern” agricultural technologies in their own parcels despite the historic failure of the GR in Guatemala to tangibly improve their livelihoods. The results also uncover a nostalgia for past forms of production but without the ability to imagine a viable path back to those earlier forms or forward to alternatives outside or modifying the GR project. Research participants perceive themselves as trapped by a system that requires continual adoption and implementation of GR approaches to remain viable as producers. The occasional experience of “good” years, particularly as a function of increased commodity prices, perhaps coupled with a brief increase in yields immediately following adoption of GR approaches, likely helps perpetuate the perceived promise of GR production. Their condition illustrates how smallholders’ spatial imaginaries around desired improvements to their productive practices can become constrained by the material realities created by GR in the first place.

The Pacific lowlands’ history as a site of land redistribution and internal colonization came with an initial promise of campesino production of surplus instead of just subsistence or semisubsistencereproduction. This history, with all of its attendant hopes for progress and prosperity, continues to shape current smallholder farming efforts and expectations. For many, hopes of prosperity and progress have been truncated by the recent decades of the advancement of industrial agriculture and what they experience as state betrayal (Nelson 2009), as they see state support directed instead toward large landholders. Smallholder imaginaries of “progress” through more equitable access to the benefits of “modern” agriculture thus have long lines that stretch back to hopes and promises of the region’s initial land redistribution and internal colonization. More recent proximity to industrial agricultural sites, such as the sugarcane plantations, has fed these imaginaries further. At the same time, many are frustrated by their dependence on neighboring industrial production for wage labor opportunities to alleviate the widespread food insecurity farming families experience (Carte et al. 2019).

A vibrant literature exists on the future of smallholder agriculture that explores and evaluates the capacity of different development initiatives, technologies, and mobility to alleviate hunger and poverty in Guatemala and around the world (e.g., Dorward 2009; Dorward et al. 2009; Harris and Orr 2014; Hellin, Cox, and López-Ridaura 2017; Harris 2019; Lopez-Ridaura et al. 2019). Our findings for Caballo Blanco contribute to a wider understanding of the visions for this future among smallholders themselves as significantly varied across diverse contexts. Our focus on these farmers’ imaginaries complements work by Chhetri and Chaudhary (2011) that speaks to the failures in imagination of the GR itself, in not adequately recognizing the knowledge, needs, and contexts of small farmers. We also build on key work on agrarian imaginaries by Wolford (2004), who, with her work on the Movement of Rural Landless Workers in Brazil, illustrated how the unique agrarian histories and spatial environments of specific agrarian production regimes, including sugarcane production, can ultimately inform specific producer logics, desires, and practices for land ownership and use arrangements.

Smallholders in general in Caballo Blanco are caught in a sort of “affective imaginary of defeat” (Copeland 2014), which leaves little room to maneuver toward, for example, a more sustainable and sovereign agriculture (Copeland 2019b). We expose some deep contradictions in smallholders’ spatial imaginaries that arise from the agrarian history and spatial environments of Guatemala and the Pacific lowlands. These farmers perceive themselves as having become trapped, but their understanding of how to escape is shaped by the discourse-turned-material in which they live their lives. Their spatial imaginaries around small-scale production have been disrupted by the material realities emerging from the institutions and practices of neoliberal governance

that have produced the contemporary reality of Guatemala’s industrial agricultural landscape, with hungry (for food, for land, for support) smallholders often in its interstices. Their currently constrained imaginaries have emerged around agrarian production, development, and governance that ultimately reinforce GR logics and practices at the expense of alternatives. This situation presents challenges to any national projects for alternative development but, at the same time, suggests the possibility for liberatory change in smallholder imaginaries in Caballo Blanco with meaningful exposure to alternative discourses and approaches that promise better outcomes for farmers.

Our research illustrates how the GR in Guatemala has colonized both material and idea-tional spaces in Caballo Blanco to produce among smallholders what we call constrained spatial imaginaries. Guatemala’s agrarian histories and production regimes inform specific producer logics, desires, and practices for land ownership and use that build people’s spatial imaginaries in ways that they see little or no escape from their situation (Wolford 2004). Our analysis of the current dynamics, however, does not foreclose the potential for a decolonial future in which smallholders in Caballo Blanco transform their imaginaries. ■
