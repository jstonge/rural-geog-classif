In the United States, low-income individuals and some racial and ethnic minorities have higher rates of transit use and food insecurity. The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), which provides low-income households with money for groceries at authorized retailers, creates a unique food procurement environment for participants. In this article, transit networks' effect on access to SNAP retailers is assessed by calculating transit travel times to all transitable SNAP stores of census block groups (CBGs) in thirty-three metropolitan areas in the U.S. Midwest. Low transit access CBGs are identified and compared to U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) low-income, low-access (LILA) census tracts. Store type transit access ratios are analyzed to further specify disproportionate access to healthy food retailers. Findings indicate low transit access is experienced in fringe-urban, majority white, car-dependent CBGs. Approximately 24,380 people receiving public assistance, 145,636 Black people, 135,731 non-Black Hispanic people, and 108,325 low-income people live within these CBGs, however. The USDA's LILA census tracts are more expansive than this study's low-income, low-tran- sit-access (LILTA) tracts. Despite being classified as rural, LILTA-only tracts had sixty-eight households without a vehicle, on average. Future research and policy should engage populations in suburban and exurban regions to accommodate trans- portation needs of a nonnegligible portion of potentially food-insecure households. **Key Words: food environments, food insecurity, SNAP, transit access, transport poverty.**

Within the auto-centric built environment of the U.S. Midwest, low-income individuals and some racial and ethnic minorities have been shown to have transportation barriers to accessing essential goods and services (Cormier and Craypo 2000; Cristancho et al. 2008; Jang and Kim 2018). Income, race, and ethnicity are considered social determinants of health, a misnomer, as the cause of lower health outcomes in these groups are the systemic inequalities that disadvantage marginal- ized individuals from accessing goods and services for maintaining health (McClure et al. 2019). Among these systemic inequalities is spatial segre- gation, which results in areas of concentrated pov- erty with lower densities of essential destinations like food retailers, thus creating regions with low access to food (Jang and Kim 2018; Havewala 2021). Roughly one in nine Americans are consid- ered food insecure (Coleman-Jensen et al. 2019), but rates of food insecurity are higher in low-income, racial, and ethnic minority groups (Murthy 2016), and these marginalized groups tend to be spatially concentrated due to a myriad of marginalizing sys- temic processes like redlining, urban renewal, and gentrification (Nord, Luloff, and Jensen 1995; Jang and Kim 2018; McClure et al. 2019).

Spatial proximity to retailers is not enough to characterize access to food, as spatial segregation operates in tandem with household factors like transport poverty and financial instability to create poor food environments (Farber, Morang, and

Widener 2014; Shannon 2016; Widener 2018). Indeed, our understanding of access to food might be different when considering access to transit, a more affordable mode of transportation used at higher rates by low-income households (Ver Ploeg et al. 2015). A thorough investigation of access to affordable food retail in the United States should especially consider the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), a federal program sup- plying monetary benefits to low-income households to be used at authorized retailers, which include a variety of establishments from convenience stores to large supermarkets. SNAP participants are only able to use their benefits at participating retailers, who must meet standards for grocery offerings and enroll with the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA). A financially precarious household can benefit from both access to transit and SNAP stores to obtain food.

Given the potentially different food retail envi- ronments experienced by SNAP beneficiaries, it is important to understand whether individuals reliant on public transit face increased challenges to access- ing stores that supply affordable and healthy options. To explore this, we examine access to SNAP retailers, stratified by type, via transit services in thirty-three small to large cities in the U.S. Midwest. In doing so, we provide a novel bench- mark assessment of the role transit systems play in facilitating access to different categories of SNAP food retailers, which might have repercussions forpurchasing behaviors and could inform transportation planning practices.

## Background

The USDA defines food insecurity as “a household-level economic and social condition of limited or uncertain access to adequate food” (USDA 2018). The USDA operationalizes this definition by designating census tracts as low-income, low-access (LILA) tracts, which are low-income census tracts¹ where a significant number or share of the population (500 individuals or 33 percent) does not have a grocery store within one mile in urban areas or ten miles in rural areas (USDA 2017). Although the USDA’s LILA classification provides an extensive mapping of low access to food in the United States, some criticize their measurement for misspecifying areas of low access (Major, Delmelle, and Delmelle 2018). Food environments are complex systems, and how populations navigate them is the result of both supply- and demand-side realities. The USDA only measures supply-side retailer Euclidean proximity and demand-side economic precarity, yet evidence suggests individuals are more concerned about food costs and regularly shop outside of their residential neighborhood (Hendrickson, Smith, and Eikenberry 2006; Shannon and Christian 2017). By focusing on transit and subsidized retailers, and estimating time cost as a proxy for spatial proximity, access to food retail could take on new spatial patterns.

Low-income households have higher rates of transit usage (Ver Ploeg et al. 2015) and transit is often a more affordable option than driving (Curl, Clark, and Kearns 2018; Walks 2018). When transit has been developed to support low-income populations, quality of life and equity have improved for economically marginalized groups (Milan and Creutzig 2017). Low-income populations living in auto-centric built environments could become transit riders if transit access is improved (Yousefzadeh Barri et al. 2021). Although systemically marginalized and low-income households stand to benefit from transit access, conceptualizing access is difficult as individuals might be experiencing physical or economic barriers to accessing transportation networks available to them. This inability to access certain destinations due to failures in the transportation network is called transport poverty (Lucas et al. 2016).

Transport poverty is an umbrella term encompassing several underlying conditions including transport affordability, mobility poverty, and accessibility poverty (Lucas et al. 2016). Due to lack of monetary resources, low-income households might experience transport affordability issues (Murakami and Young 1997), causing them to rely on public transit. If public transit infrastructure is lacking, however, a low-income household might also

experience mobility poverty, thus affecting accessibility to goods, services, and employment (i.e., accessibility poverty). By addressing transport poverty through improving transit access, low-income households would have greater mobility and might have improved access to groceries as a result.

Transport poverty, beyond its implications for economic marginalization, is closely related to social exclusion (Lucas 2012). Social exclusion is a constraint-based process that prevents individuals from participating in normal activities within their society (Preston and Rajé 2007). When investing in transit infrastructure within a social inclusion paradigm, social exclusion can be reduced through increased access to these activities, like grocery shopping (Garrett and Taylor 2003; Preston and Rajé 2007; Lucas 2012). In general, living in urban environments bolsters social inclusion because these environments tend to have more transit infrastructure to facilitate daily mobility (Casas 2007; Allen and Farber 2020). Social exclusion is prevalent among low-income households in car-dominated suburban communities that experience gaps in transit; this population might struggle to access essential activities like employment (Fransen et al. 2015; Allen and Farber 2020; Bastiaanssen, Johnson, and Lucas 2020).

Transportation planning offers a relatively untapped vein for pursuing improved access to food retail. The U.S. Department of Transportation (USDOT) requires state and regional transportation planning authorities to develop performance measures for transit accessibility to jobs and other essential services (DeGood 2020). USDOT performance measures are intended to boost environmental sustainability and include a litany of measures like congestion improvement benchmarks and transit access to essential services (e.g., food retailers). Currently, air quality and safety measures dominate these performance measures, but they present an avenue for measuring and planning for access to food (Singleton and Clifton 2017). Singleton and Clifton (2017) argued that the development of health-related performance measures like accessibility, and the data and analysis tools necessary to support these measures, are needed. Accessibility measures are sometimes included in metropolitan planning organization (MPO) transportation plans but lack engagement with public health needs (Singleton and Clifton 2017). Therefore, accessibility measures could be an important step toward health-conscious transportation planning. Further situating these accessibility measures within an equity framework could reduce disparities in health founded on centuries of economic and racial segregation.

## *Calculating Transit Travel Times*

We calculate transit travel times for all MPO CBGs using the OpenTripPlanner (OTP) scripting application programming interface (OpenTripPlanner Project [2011] 2018). Destinations are all SNAP stores within the MPO and a 3-km buffer of its boundary. We employ a 3-km (approximately 1.9 mile) buffer to control for edge effects. Using a buffer larger than one mile, which is used as a threshold for food environments in urban areas by the USDA, helps our analysis account for the low-density typical of fringe CBGs in the study (USDA 2017). The OTP router calculates travel times from CBG centroids to all SNAP store destinations for each minute in a one-hour window (9:30–10:30 a.m.) surrounding the peak errand time (10:00 a.m. according to the National Household Travel Survey [NHTS]) using a weekday transit schedule. This temporal context aids in accurately estimating accessibility (Farber, Morang, and Widener 2014). Transit calculations include walking times, but our router specifications do not allow more than five kilometers walking. Only trips of one hour or less in one direction are considered. We compute means and variance statistics for the trips recorded to

SNAP stores within the 9:30 to 10:30 a.m. hour window. We tested other peak errand times during routing analysis, but these results indicate there is little variation in access throughout the day.

## Transit Access by Store Type

Because small- and large-format grocers fill different retail niches in the food environment that create dissimilar locational densities (Baviera-Puig, Buitrago-Vera, and Escriba-Perez 2016; Hood, Clarke, and Clarke 2016; Reigadinha, Godinho, and Dias 2017), we created an access ratio using the transit access scores for either type ($C_i$ or $S_i$). Using these ratios, we identify 2,073 outlier CBGs using an IQR approach; all outliers have better access to small retail. Although the population counts are similar to those of the composite transit access score ($A_i$), relative to the other groups examined, Black individuals

have slightly higher counts in outlier CBGs than low-transit-access ($A_i$) CBGs. Black individuals account for roughly 8 percent of the population living in outlier CBGs (i.e., CBGs with a greater difference between $Ci$ and $Si$ scores than the majority of CBGs). For comparison, Black individuals account for about 3 percent of the low- or no-transit-access ($A_i$) CBGs, which is generally similar to the other groups included (Table 4).

When examining the spatial distribution of store type transit access ratios across the study MPOs, we see that outlier CBGs do not follow a clear spatial pattern. Using a local Moran's *I* test, we find little significant clustering. Figure 3 depicts an example of an MPO with significant clustering. A table of global Moran's *I* statistics for each MPO is provided in the Appendix.

## Comparison to USDA LILA Tracts

We compare our transit access scores to USDA LILA census tracts to assess how transit access differs from the USDA's conceptualization of access. Because our transit access scores are calculated at a higher spatial resolution (block groups) than the LILA scores (census tracts), we aggregated our transit access scores ($A_i$) to the census tract level, accounting for population weights. Ultimately, we find thirty-nine census tracts in the thirty-three MPOs to be low income and low transit access (LILTA) using the USDA's low-income criteria and our 0.2 transit access threshold. The USDA classifies 720 tracts as LILA. Table 5 provides counts of populations with higher rates of food insecurity who live in LILA or LILTA census tracts. Of the thirty-nine LILTA tracts, twenty-one of them (54 percent) are also classified as LILA tracts, indicating that individuals located in these tracts might experience especially poor access to food. Roughly 46 percent of LILTA census tracts are not accounted for by LILA, but this translates to small population totals: 1,811 people who identify as Black, 2,476 Hispanic people, 3,196 people below the poverty level, and 501 people receiving public assistance.

Importantly, populations that could be potentially vulnerable to food insecurity reside in many types of CBGs across any given metropolitan region. Even though the percentages of the four groups

examined in this study are not disproportionately large in areas with low transit access to SNAP retail, when considered together, there is a sizable number of people who could be at a disadvantage by not having an adequate transit option for food shopping. That said, operating transit services in less dense regions is often not feasible; for households in low-transit-access CBGs it might be worth developing different mobility or food supply strategies to improve access to affordable food retailers, which we review in the next section.transit authorities could theoretically shift funding away from the city center to reach suburban populations, but this would likely reduce transit accessibility for residents in the city center while catering to CBGs that, as shown here, are generally car dependent. This model of transit planning has already been shown to be economically inefficient and socially inequitable (Garrett and Taylor 2003).

Although it might seem most reasonable for individuals to simply use personal vehicles, low-income households often have tight monetary budgets that can cause or worsen food insecurity. Encouraging financially strained households to take on additional fees associated with car ownership (e.g., insurance) in addition to volatile changes in the cost to operate this mode of transport (e.g., fuel) might only exacerbate less-than-ideal situations when an alternative transportation option is not available. In fact, car payments are becoming a bigger source of debt for U.S. households (Center for Microeconomic Data 2019; Cross et al. 2019; Bliss 2019a). Recent work has found that car-dependent, low-income households are likely to become transit riders if transit access improves (Yousefzadeh Barri et al. 2021). Despite this, the current political environment in many of the states in our study area has shown little interest in substantially overhauling transit systems (Witsil and Lawrence 2016; Kaeding 2021; Dwyer 2022). Beyond increasing funding for transit in suburban communities, more research is needed to support solutions improving transit access in these areas.

First, and perhaps the simplest, is expanding on-demand transit services. Currently, on-demand public transit is available but not necessarily temporally efficient. Boosting infrastructure for these types of services could help populations having difficulty getting to a SNAP store rely on an affordable type of public transit rather than using Uber or Lyft, which are often less cost-effective and have barriers for use (e.g., access to a smartphone). Some cities in Canada and the United States, however, have started subsidizing rideshare providers as a form of public transit (NPR 2016; Bliss 2019b), which is another option for local authorities to consider.

Another possible solution being piloted by the USDA Food and Nutrition Service is a SNAP produce delivery service, which reduces costs associated with getting groceries by private vehicle. Currently available in forty-eight states participating in the pilot, this program could change how households get groceries. The program, which began in April 2019, allows SNAP participants to purchase their groceries from online retailers (e.g., Walmart) and have them delivered to their homes (USDA 2019). SNAP only covers the products participants purchase and not delivery fees, but the convenience of online shopping could help households who suffer from low transit access or temporal restrictions that prevent them from reaching the store. Because this program is

being newly piloted, evaluations are needed to assess its long-term viability.

More interventions exist that could improve the conditions for populations at risk of food insecurity in low-transit-access CBGs, like urban agriculture (Metcalf and Widener 2011; McCauley 2021) and community food-sharing platforms (Martinko 2021). It is crucial, however, to give proper voice and agency to food-insecure individuals so they can inform how to address and foster better access to affordable food retailers. The communities affected by policies aimed at improving food environments should inform which changes would most help them, and policymakers should actively seek out and incorporate these voices to foster more equitable outcomes (Milan and Creutzig 2017).

## Study Limitations

This study has several limitations that future studies should consider and address. First, round-trip travel times and trip chaining should be considered, as our study only evaluates home-to-store transit times. Other studies have considered grocery trips that are chained to a work commute (Widener et al. 2015). Additionally, we employ schedule-based transit travel times, whereas other work has shown large variability between scheduled and actual transit travel times (Wessel, Allen, and Farber 2017). Future studies could better model transit access by using this approach and by considering vehicle travel times. Additionally, store operating hours are not available within the SNAP data set and are not considered during travel time generation, which has been shown elsewhere to affect temporal access to food retail (Widener et al. 2017).

Previous work has identified other regions of the United States as having more food-insecure households than in the Midwest (Nord et al. 2010); by expanding the spatial scope of this study and the MPOs included, more insights could be drawn about transit access to SNAP in the United States. Chicago's MPO especially deserves similar study and is large enough to warrant independent investigation. Finally, other populations such as single-parent households, older adults, and people with disabilities have high rates of food insecurity and should be considered (Sharkey, Johnson, and Dean 2010; Nord et al. 2010).

## Conclusion

In quantifying and mapping populations that have low transit access to SNAP retailers, patterns reflective of transport poverty literature emerged wherein low-density areas on the fringe of urban centers had low transit access scores or no transit access at all. Additionally, we identified more than 2,000 CBGs with substantially higher access to small retail thanlarge grocers. Census tracts with low transit access to SNAP retail were different from the USDA's LILA tracts because they tended to be affluent and car dependent. Despite this, at the regional level, there is a substantial number of potentially food-insecure people with low levels of transit access to SNAP retail.

This study has identified a gap in transit access that local transit authorities and MPOs should seek to fill through tailor-made policies that fit their local contexts and the desires of residents in low-transit-access CBGs. Despite limitations, this study provides an important perspective into the SNAP food retail environment and how metropolitan populations in the U.S. Midwest are able to access them via transit. ■
