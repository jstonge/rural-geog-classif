# Migration Responses to the COVID-19 Pandemic: A Case Study of New England Showing Movements down the Urban Hierarchy and Ensuing Impacts on Real Estate Markets

Peter B. Nelson and Wright Frost

Middlebury College, USA

As the COVID-19 pandemic swept across the United States, the media began reporting stories of people leaving cities for rural destinations, setting off frenzied real estate activity in receiving communities. This article builds a case study of New England using nonconventional data collected from mobile devices as a proxy for population to explore the temporal and spatial patterns of movement down the urban hierarchy since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Two research questions guide the analysis: (1) How have urban–rural migration systems in New England shifted since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic? (2) In what ways have real estate markets been affected by these apparent migration shifts? The analysis reveals shifts of population away from metropolitan core areas of the region and into micropolitan and noncore counties. These population shifts were most pronounced in late summer and fall 2020 with loosening travel restrictions. By the end of 2020, migration systems in New England once more resembled prepandemic patterns. Further, these places down the urban hierarchy consistently showed more substantial increases in real estate activity as reflected in rising prices, reduced inventories, and increased sales volume. These real estate dynamics suggest urban to rural migration during the COVID-19 pandemic might be initiating new waves of rural gentrification. **Key Words:** COVID-19, migration, real estate impacts, urban–rural hierarchy.

COVID-19 first appeared in the United States in early 2020 and quickly spread between cities and into rural regions. In March, public health officials began to issue stay-at-home orders and interstate travel restrictions to limit the virus's spread. These constraints dramatically altered how people lived as school and work went remote, restaurants reduced capacities, and entertainment venues closed. In the early stages of the pandemic, the press began reporting that the crisis was changing not just how, but also where people lived. Articles in major newspapers described migration from cities into rural regions offering refuge for sheltering in place (Evangelou 2020; Fisher, Schwartzman, and Weissenbach 2020; Hughes 2020; Tully and Stowe 2020). Stories highlighted ways new work arrangements and desire for more space motivated the urban exodus. Articles further identified acute impacts on housing markets as new arrivals bid up prices and depleted inventories (Prevost 2020; Tully and Stowe 2020; Bowlin 2021). Stories of pandemic-induced migration are compelling, yet individual anecdotes give no sense of the extent of population shifts beyond specific cases, offer little insight into geographic variation, and do not assess the scope of impacts on housing markets in destinations.

This regional case study uses nonconventional data to document shifting migration systems across New England and impacts on real estate markets since the arrival of COVID-19. Understanding how this unique event affected migration and housing markets is important for several reasons. First, migration between urban and rural regions has been volatile since the 1970s, responding to different shocks

and technological change, and this article contributes to well-established themes examining urban-to-rural migration. Second, similar migration-inducing crises (health, climatic, economic) will undoubtedly emerge in the future, so gauging how such events affect migration is important for future preparation and response. Finally, the empirical analysis uses nonconventional data to examine pandemic-induced migration shifts, highlighting the possibilities big data hold for asking new geographic questions.

The remainder of this article contains four sections. A literature review examines how the pandemic could transform urban–rural migration systems documenting ebbs and flows of urban–rural migration over five decades, discussing how exogenous shocks alter migration systems, and highlighting ways heightened rural in-migration can affect real estate activity. We then describe the unique data used in the analysis. The results section presents temporal and spatial changes in settlement systems and real estate markets emerging during the pandemic. We conclude with key insights and identify important unanswered questions.

## Literature Review

### *Temporal Dynamics in Rural–Urban Migration Systems*

Migration between urban and rural regions across the United States has long interested geographers,and for two centuries, rural-urban migration has been most common. Today, the United States is nearly 80 percent urban, whereas in 1930, it was evenly split between urban and rural (P. B. Nelson 2021). Despite the prevalence of rural-to-urban migration, these flows have reversed at times over the last fifty years, and certain rural areas today enjoy counterstreams of urban in-migrants. In the 1970s, demographers noticed extensive rural population growth, coining the decade a “rural renaissance” (Beale 1976, 1977). This renaissance was driven by a confluence of factors including the Arab oil embargo, regional economic restructuring, and residential preferences (Frey and Johnson 1998). The renaissance waned in the 1980s, yet growth returned during the 1990s “rural rebound,” closely tied to amenity destinations and technologies enabling remote work (Beyers and Lindahl 1996; McGranahan 1999; Beyers and Nelson 2000). Although the rebound was widespread, growth was particularly pronounced in metropolitan-adjacent counties and amenity regions of the Rocky Mountains, Upper Great Lakes, and Southeast. Many northeastern nonmetropolitan counties experienced out-migration during the 1990s (Johnson and Beale 1994).

The rebound of the 1990s has subsided, yet growth persists today in select amenity destinations (Gosnell and Abrams 2011). Amenity migration raises socioeconomic and ecological concerns (Krannich and Greider 1990; Theobald, Gosnell, and Riebsame 1996; Smith and Krannich 2000; Krannich, Petrzelka, and Brehm 2006; Chi and Marcouiller 2012; Harner and Benz 2013). In many destinations, urban-to-rural migration affects real estate markets initiating rural gentrification. In-migrants often arrive with accumulated wealth and purchase or build expensive housing (Ghose 2004; P. B. Nelson and Hines 2018). Rising housing costs, displacement, and precarious living conditions follow in rural gentrification's wake (L. Nelson, Trautman, and Nelson 2015). Flows of urban emigres into rural regions during the pandemic are likely to substantially affect housing markets.

## Migration Response to External Shocks

Demographic history shows that various shocks have influenced migration over time. Migration is an economic bellwether, as households often move when economic conditions change, and large shocks to local, regional, and global economies can induce migration. Well-known regional restructuring in the United States shifted investment and employment from the Rustbelt to the Sunbelt in the latter half of the twentieth century. These geographic shifts coincided with expanding service sector employment and declining manufacturing. Migration systems changed accordingly with net migration from Rustbelt to

Sunbelt of nearly 4 million in the 1970s (Bluestone and Harrison 1982). Economically induced migration has been particularly acute for certain cities. Detroit's population peaked in 1950 at 1.85 million, yet by 2010, it was barely 700,000, a 62 percent decline (McDonald 2014). Buffalo, Cleveland, and Gary have also seen population decline driven by out-migration (Cowell 2013; Hackworth 2015; Schindler 2016). Population shifts play out unevenly in specific neighborhoods. Since the 1970s, majority Black neighborhoods in Cleveland, Pittsburgh, and Detroit have suffered disproportionate out-migration (Hackworth 2021).

Clearly, economic factors have influenced migration streams at different scales, yet push-pull forces shaping migration extend beyond economics. Changing environmental conditions and acute natural hazards can dramatically alter migration systems. Globally, estimates indicate up to 1 billion climate refugees (Myers 1997; White 2011; Bose 2016). Within the United States, scholars have identified the ways acute events like Hurricane Katrina and wildfires across the intermountain West have influenced migration across communities distinguished along lines of race and ethnicity (Fussell, Sastry, and VanLandingham 2010; Li et al. 2010; Nawrotzki et al. 2014; DeWaard, Curtis, and Fussell 2016; Winkler and Rouleau 2021).

The COVID-19 pandemic has the potential to affect migration systems in several ways. Shifting to remote work arrangements might renew interest in suburban living as households working from home flee “gentrified neighborhoods for upscale suburbs and nearby small towns” (Florida, Rodríguez-Pose, and Storper 2021, 13). Survey work in the United Kingdom further confirms a majority of workers who shifted to telework arrangements during the pandemic indicate a desire to continue working from home “sometimes” or “often,” although not entirely. Up to 15 percent of these individuals now working from home would consider relocating their primary residence to a more rural location (Reuschke 2021), although given the partial nature of the work-from-home arrangements, such moves would be constrained to rural locations with proximity to their urban employers. Denham (2021) anticipated similar migration responses in Australia potentially accelerating sprawl into amenity regions on the urban periphery. These pieces discussing how the pandemic could shape migration into suburban and nonmetropolitan destinations also express concerns about how new migration flows will affect real estate markets in sending and receiving regions.

The types of migration responses just discussed are precisely those reported in the popular press. The COVID-19 pandemic, however, has the potential to affect migration systems in other ways as well. Notwithstanding the rural renaissance and the rural rebound, migration flows have generally been directed from rural to urban areas, and this isparticularly the case for the migration of young adults (Carr and Kefalas 2009). Regions like the Great Plains have suffered from persistent population loss for decades, as the young leave the countryside in pursuit of greater opportunities in the city. When the pandemic hit in early 2020, it is likely that rural youth emigration slowed (or stopped altogether) as opportunities drawing young adults to the city disappeared. Similarly, young adults who had recently arrived in the city likely returned home when they shifted to remote work or lost their jobs entirely. Thus, the COVID-19 pandemic could potentially affect migration systems by accelerating flows from city to suburbs and periurban areas as people shifted to work-from-home arrangements. At the same time, the pandemic also likely slowed rural-to-urban migration, particularly in areas long suffering from youth out-migration. The following New England case study draws on unconventional data to answer two primary questions,

1. How have urban-rural migration systems in New England shifted since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic?
2. In what ways have the region's real estate markets been impacted by these apparent migration shifts?

## Data and Methodology

The decennial census through 2000 and the American Community Survey (ACS), beginning in 2009, ask migration questions, and these data along with the U.S. Census Bureau annual estimates series provide primary data for geographers researching migration. Unfortunately, these conventional data are poorly suited for exploring the migration response to COVID-19. The decennial census no longer asks a migration question. To date, the 2019 ACS is the most recent release available, so the ACS does not yet provide coverage of years affected by the pandemic. Moreover, the migration information collected in the ACS only includes all counties in its five-year sample, so any future ACS migration data will pool responses from samples taken before, during, and after the pandemic. Finally, the population estimates series reports net migration annually from 1 July through 30 June. The pandemic was rapidly evolving from week to week and month to month through 2020 and beyond, so the annual net migration estimates from the Census Bureau do not allow for examination of migration responses on a finer grain temporal scale.

To answer our first research question, we use location information collected from mobile devices by private firms. Scholars are increasingly relying on such data to better understand spatial phenomena, including the impacts of the pandemic. Individuals

with mobile devices (knowingly or unknowingly) provide location information through various apps. SafeGraph is one firm providing place of interest (POI) information on retail establishments, schools, hospitals, and so on, which they market to clients examining foot traffic frequenting their stores. SafeGraph estimates their database includes approximately 10 percent of all mobile devices in the United States. We use SafeGraph's Monthly Patterns database, which reports the number of devices residing in each census block group in the United States (SafeGraph 2022). Each mobile device in SafeGraph's database is assigned a residential usual home location at the census block group level by determining where the device "pings" most frequently between 6 p.m. and 7 a.m. over the last six weeks. The home location is updated monthly for each device in the database, and SafeGraph only reports locations for devices when an internally determined confidence score exceeds a minimum threshold.¹ For this analysis we compile these counts from their Monthly Patterns database from January 2018 through January 2021, so our database includes records from before the pandemic as well as location information during its first year. SafeGraph data can shed light on population change and migration. Mobile devices and population are highly correlated across space and down the urban hierarchy. Figures 1 and 2 plot county-level population and mobile devices for July 2019. Correlations are extremely high in both metropolitan and nonmetropolitan counties (Rho of 0.99 and 0.97, respectively), and hold at the county scale regardless of region.

out-migration. We complete this analysis at the county and tract scales. For counties, we aggregate using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's (CDC) National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) classifications differentiating three sizes of metropolitan areas, micropolitan areas, and noncore nonmetropolitan counties (CDC 2017). The NCHS classification further distinguishes metropolitan “fringe” counties for metropolitan regions with populations exceeding 1 million to identify sub-urban counties. We summarize the tract-level analysis by generalized versions of the U.S. Department of Agriculture's (USDA; 2019) Rural-Urban Commuting Areas (RUCA). RUCAs were initially developed after Census 2000 and classify tracts into urban core, rural high commuting, and autonomous rural categories using criteria similar to that for county-based metropolitan classification (Cromartie 2016). RUCAs allow analysts to unpack intracounty phenomena including migration into rural areas within metropolitan counties. For both the county and tract-level analyses, we aggregate devices by urban classification before computing shifts, and in all instances, results are normalized by the overall number of devices in each area to control for differently sized areas. We report results through the end of 2020, before COVID vaccines became widely available.

## Results

As the pandemic progressed through 2020, migration systems shifted toward small metropolitan, micropolitan, and noncore areas in New England. Figure 3 presents YoY change in mobile device shifts from January through December 2020. Figure 3 shows moderate variability across the urban hierarchy ranging from a 7 percent decline for metro core counties to a 9 percent increase for metro fringe counties during the initial months of 2020 as the pandemic was first emerging. In April, shifts across all levels of the urban hierarchy in New England became negative. The moderate-in-magnitude shifts during the initial months of 2020 indicate that migration systems at this time were largely

similar to those before the pandemic. Shifts remained moderate and negative through June 2020, ranging between -2 percent and -10 percent. During this period, the consistently negative shift changes indicate either increased out-migration or slowing in-migration. Shifts remained negative through June and July 2020, and large metro core counties experienced the steepest declines in shifts from June 2020 until October 2020. By October 2020, for these most urban counties the 2019-2020 shift was 25 percent lower than the corresponding 2018-2019 shift. At this low point, the analysis indicates an aggregate drop in device shifts for the largest metro core counties corresponding to 29,014 fewer devices. In contrast, for micropolitan and non-core counties in New England, shifts began to increase by late summer, peaking in November 2020, when they were 7 to 12 percent higher than in the corresponding period one year earlier (i.e., 6,517 and 3,726 devices for micropolitan and non-core counties, respectively). By December 2020, shifts across the urban hierarchy converged once again, indicating migration systems at the county scale in December 2020 resembled those from before the pandemic, although micropolitan and noncore areas displayed shifts that were 5 percent higher and large metro fringe areas that were 7 percent lower than prepandemic levels.

There are several possible ways the pandemic could be reshaping migration systems. The spring months typically mark the return of snowbirds who winter in warmer climates. Stories of snowbirds delaying a return north have appeared in the news media in other regions (Duarte 2020), and New England is a node in snowbird circulation. Therefore, one possible cause of consistently small negative shifts through spring 2020 is an altered rhythm of seasonal migration. In addition, at the outset of the pandemic, many states issued varying degrees of stay-at-home orders (Moreland et al. 2020) and travel restrictions. On 3 April, Maine began requiring a fourteen-day quarantine for all out-of-state travelers (Mills 2020), yet by 1 July, visitors to Maine from select states were exempt from these restrictions, making it easier for some people to travel or move to Maine (Fay 2020). In contrast, Vermont initially required a fourteen-day quarantine

for anyone arriving from an origin with caseloads exceeding a specified threshold (Scott 2020). With rapidly rising caseloads through summer 2020 across the United States, Vermont's policy effectively mandated quarantine for all out-of-state arrivals. Vermont's travel restriction was slightly relaxed in November 2020 before it was revoked completely in February 2021 (Paquette 2021). Thus, all states across the region had rather severe travel restrictions in place through midsummer 2020 when device shifts are consistently negative, and these restrictions simultaneously slow in-migration and likely limit out-migration. After July 2020, travel between some states in New England became slightly easier while remaining highly restricted for others. Figures 3 and 4 both show that shifts began to rise in late summer and early fall as some states loosened travel restrictions, and the rising shifts are most pronounced in places lower in the urban hierarchy.

The analysis thus far suggests that New England experienced shifting migration systems during the

first ten months of the pandemic. Through spring and early summer 2020, migration systems slowed compared to prepandemic levels, but by late summer and fall places further down the urban hierarchy had positive shifts reflecting increased in- or decreased out-migration. Further, the timing of these shifts from state to state appears to be influenced by variability in state-level travel restrictions. These migration shifts can affect housing markets in small towns and rural regions, inflating prices and accelerating real estate activity (Ghose 2004; Golding 2016). More people arriving increases demand and fewer people leaving reduces inventory. Additionally, historically low interest rates coupled with expanding short-term rental platforms like AirBnB (Hoffman and Heisler 2020; Cocola-Gant and Gago 2021) put further pressure on real estate markets throughout 2020. Next, we examine real estate dynamics to determine the extent to which markets in places along the urban hierarchy were differentially affected. Although this case study focuses on New England, many of these pressures in real estate markets are present across the United States.

Figures 6 through 9 present the temporal change in the four real estate indicators summarized by NCHS classification, and the patterns presented in these figures clearly show increasing pressure on New England's housing markets. These pressures are most acute in places further down the urban hierarchy. Furthermore, these figures indicate that the accelerated real estate activity coincided with the device shifts described earlier. From January 2020 until early spring, sales volume (Figure 6) across the urban hierarchy was at or above 2019 levels, although volume was trending slightly downward. In March 2020, the downward trend in sales steepened, indicating slower home sales relative to 2019, and this timing corresponded with the moment when all levels of the urban hierarchy began to demonstrate declining device shifts (Figure 3). In summer 2020, however, home sales increased, and by mid-July volumes across the urban hierarchy were higher than in 2019. By fall 2020, areas with the largest YoY increases in home sales were noncore and micropolitan regions with volumes at least 50 percent higher than they were in the corresponding month in 2019.

Trends in median sale price (Figure 7) are similar. Although more variable in rural areas, during early 2020 homes at all levels of the hierarchy were selling for prices 5 to 12 percent higher than in 2019. From April 2020 through early summer, prices declined, but they remained above 2019 levels in all areas except large metro core counties.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Small towns and rural areas have long been perceived as safer, healthier places to live, and this rural idyll has been linked to past rural in-migration (Bunce 2003; Bell 2006; Abrams and Gosnell 2012; Gkartzios 2013). Footloose individuals and households, particularly in the 1990s, identified such attributes as motivations for urban-to-rural migration, and for some, technology and new work-from-home possibilities enabled the migration streams forming the rural rebound (Beyers and Nelson 2000; Deller et al. 2001; Barraclough 2003; Brehm, Eisenhauer, and Krannich 2004). Urban-rural migration streams have also been shown to respond to external shocks. Scholars have speculated that the pandemic might spark renewed interest in rural living, particularly in areas with some proximity to urban centers, and they highlight the ways such migration shifts could affect real estate markets (Denham 2021; Florida, Rodríguez-Pose, and Storper 2021; Reuschke 2021). The pandemic has certainly had lasting impacts altering how we learn, work, and recreate. This exploratory and descriptive analysis also indicates that the pandemic is further reshaping migration systems. The case study results using mobile phone data indicate that migration systems did shift as the pandemic stretched into late summer 2020, and these shifts were directed toward areas down the urban hierarchy. Simultaneously, real estate indicators show that these smaller areas have experienced dramatic real estate impacts. The volume and price of real estate transactions have increased dramatically in New England's small metro, micropolitan, and noncore counties, depleting inventories and creating more pressure on already tight housing markets.

The specific timing of the population shifts and accelerated real estate activity is also noteworthy. Early in the pandemic, public officials restricted travel, and during spring and early summer 2020, places at almost all levels of the urban hierarchy showed negative shifts in mobile devices, indicating an overall slowing of migration compared to pre-pandemic levels. As restrictions were lifted, positive shifts returned by late summer, particularly in small towns and rural regions, and positive shifts were more widespread and earlier to arrive in states where travel restrictions were relaxed sooner.

Although it is clear from this analysis that micropolitan and noncore rural counties across New England have experienced significant transformations since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, several important questions remain. Many rural communities across New England have suffered population stagnation for decades, so it is possible that new arrivals seeking rural refuge could bring demographic vitality into the region. Will these population shifts be permanent, or will urban emigres return to their origins once the pandemic subsides? Could new flexible work arrangements create a larger cyclical population moving periodically between urban and rural locations? The analysis of real estate indicators hints at this possibility, because

as the number of homes sold in rural regions increased there was not a concurrent slowing of activity in the more urban parts of the region. Home sales and prices have increased albeit more slowly in the metro core counties, but at the same time, inventories in metro counties have rebounded. There is clearly a complex combination of factors not limited to COVID-19 shaping housing markets across New England and the United States.

Finally, the analysis relies on mobile phone locations, an untested source of migration data, and we acknowledge that little is known about how well these data actually approximate migration. The fine-grained spatial and temporal scales provided in these data have the potential to reveal new dimensions of migration inaccessible through conventional migration data sources, and this analysis has shown how these data can illuminate month-by-month demographic responses to specific external shocks. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data used here, however, we know nothing about actual movement of any individual device from one location to another. Thus, although results indicate decreasing devices in New England's metropolitan core, we know nothing about where or if these devices have actually moved. We only know that there have been positive shifts of devices toward small metro, rural, and micropolitan regions. SafeGraph does maintain a costly database of individual devices. With individual device-level data, one could identify urban-rural migration streams pairing origins and destinations. We could identify rural destinations for devices leaving affluent neighborhoods, and such areas could face migration-induced gentrification. Alternatively, streams of mobile devices leaving lower income neighborhoods and arriving in depressed rural regions might reflect the reversal of persistent rural-to-urban migration of younger more precariously positioned workers who might have lost jobs when the pandemic hit. Finally, the temporal scale (monthly) of individual device-level data would allow analysts to identify different and potentially new forms of periodic cyclical movements between city and countryside. Such cyclicality has long been acknowledged but has yet to be extensively spatially and temporally examined (McHugh, Hogan, and Happel 1995). Clearly, the global COVID-19 pandemic has significantly affected mobility and migration, and emerging sources of geospatial data provide new opportunities to better understand theconstantly evolving landscapes of human movements. ■
