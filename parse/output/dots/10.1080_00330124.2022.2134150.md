# Human and Physical Dilemmas in Small-Scale Irrigation Development: Evidence from Climate-Sensitive Northern Ghana

Hanson Nyantakyi-Frimpong

University of Denver, USA

G. Thomas LaVanchy

Oklahoma State University, USA

Madeline M. Kelley

Arizona State University, USA

Irrigation is widely accepted as an effective adaptive management strategy for small-scale farmers of traditional rain-fed crops in areas experiencing climate change. These changes include timing of onset of the rainy season and the amount and intensity of rainfall—both issues of concern to rural farmers in northern Ghana. Despite obvious benefits, it is not apparent if farmers in this area perceive irrigation as a viable or attainable option to deal with potential food insecurity related to climate change. In this article, we examine three main issues: (1) dry spells during the early parts of the farming season, (2) impacts of these dry spells on farming systems, and (3) farmer adaptation through different agricultural water management practices. A geographic information system and remote sensing analysis using Landsat 8 and field Global Positioning System data supports findings from seventy-two in-depth interviews with smallholder farmers. Results from this integrated assessment reveal sociocultural and physical barriers to manual irrigation, including labor needs, water source proximity, and size of operational land holding. Increasing climate variability in this region will necessitate adaptation by local farmers; however, we argue that irrigation projects should not be initiated without detailed understanding of local sociocultural contexts. Effectively moving water from source to crop will require integrative knowledge of local hydrogeology and socio-cultural norms. **Key Words:** agriculture water management, dry spells, Ghana, hand irrigation, small-scale farmers.

Irrigation is often viewed as a means to secure livelihoods of small-scale farmers in regions with semiarid or seasonally dry climates, particularly in its ability to facilitate expansion of land under cultivation (Narita et al. 2022; Tatlhego et al. 2022). Rain-fed agriculture is increasingly seen as inadequate given the observed and projected changes in the hydrologic cycle as a result of global warming (Moges and Bhat 2021; Rosa 2022). Thus, adoption of irrigation by small-scale farmers is considered an effective adaptation practice and has attracted a significant amount of research focus (Smith et al. 2014; Acheampong et al. 2018; Mango et al. 2018). Despite a strong corpus of research and resulting policy recommendations to promote irrigation by small-scale farmers in Africa, various barriers exist to adoption and implementation, producing increased vulnerability to poverty and food insecurity (Bjornlund et al. 2020; Food and Agriculture Organization 2021; Von Grebmer 2021).

The purpose of this study is to explore sustainable agricultural water management options for smallholder farmers in northern Ghana from both a physical and cultural perspective.¹ We are particularly interested in (1) dry spells during the early parts of the farming season (between April and

May), (2) the impacts of these dry spells on farming systems, and (3) how farmers adapt to these conditions through different agricultural water management practices. Overall, our study addresses this question: What is the potential of using small-scale irrigation to manage dry spells in dry land environments?

A dry spell is defined here as a period of multiple consecutive days with no measurable rainfall (Agbazo et al. 2021). The fundamental differences between a dry spell and drought are presented in more detail by Şen (2015) and Mishra and Singh (2010). Dry spells have direct impacts on agriculture because when they occur frequently, evaporation losses are greater than when rainfall is temporally even. The frequency and duration of dry spells also indicate the degree to which plants are exposed to stress. In this respect, both the amount and the timing of rainfall are critical to farmers’ success. Agricultural water management is used in this article to refer to the strategic activity of planning, developing, and managing water resources for agricultural purposes (Fox and Rockström 2003; Acheampong et al. 2018). Approaches for agricultural water management depend on the nature of the source, as well as economic and social factors. Activities forrainwater regimes include influencing rainwater flows to collect water directly, or for the purpose of maximizing infiltration into the soil and underlying aquifer. Water thus becomes available during the dry season vis-à-vis reservoirs or wells (groundwater)—each having their respective advantages and disadvantages.

By exploring how farmers adapt to dry spells through different agricultural water management practices, this study makes two main contributions. First, we provide a deeper understanding of socio-cultural barriers to successful irrigation by highlighting factors linked to gender norms and household division of labor. We stress that irrigation projects should not be initiated without a detailed understanding of the sociocultural context of a place. Although this is not necessarily a new argument, it bears repeating, given the troubling failure rate of most irrigation schemes in sub-Saharan Africa (Bjornlund et al. 2020). Second, we demonstrate that crop type strongly influences the nature and viability of dry season irrigation. Because staple crops constitute the bulk of food consumed and have a lower cash value than vegetables, they require a larger spatial extent for sufficient production. Increases in the size of agricultural fields, however, lead to challenges with respect to water source proximity and how to effectively move water from the source to crop. This larger footprint then becomes untenable for manual irrigation, or requires mechanization, which has associated capital and fuel costs. In this way, the location of the water source becomes a critical factor in the viability of dry season irrigation for many farmers.

The rest of the article is organized into four main sections. The next section describes the research methods and the geography of the case study area. Then, research findings are presented in two parts. The first part focuses on geographic information systems (GIS) and remote sensing (RS) findings on irrigable fields, as well as average distances to water points. The second part provides qualitative research results that add depth and context to the GIS and RS analysis. The final section provides a conclusion highlighting what the findings mean for managing dry spells through small-scale irrigation in Ghana.

## Materials and Methods

### *Study Area*

This study was conducted in the Northern region of Ghana (Figure 1), an area located within the southern fringe of the West African Sahel and part of the Guinea-Sudano-Savanna agro-ecological zone. It is among the most food-insecure and poverty-stricken regions in Ghana (Dittoh et al. 2013; Balana et al.

2020). Livelihoods are mainly derived from climate-sensitive rain-fed agriculture, with annual crop yields far below global averages. Dominant soil types include savanna ochrosols and groundwater laterites. Both soil types afford only a shallow crop rooting zone (0.4 m), thus limiting agricultural potential. Principal crops include maize (Zea mays), finger millet (Eleusine coracana), sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), groundnuts (Arachis hypogaea), cowpea (Vigna unguiculata), and Bambara beans (Vigna subterranean).

The study site has a unimodal rainfall regime concentrated between April and August when the bulk of agriculture-related work occurs (Figure 2). Mean annual rainfall for this area is 1,078 mm for the period from 1986 to 2021, varying from a minimum of 695 mm to a maximum of 1,580 mm. More recently, erratic distribution of rainfall and delays in the rainy season pose significant challenges to agricultural production, and subsequently, produce risks to poverty and health (Bawa 2019; Xu et al. 2019). Mean annual temperature is between 23°C and 35°C. Open water evaporation (pan evaporation) approaches 2,600 mm per year, exceeding total rainfall for the area (Gyau-Boakye 2001). This dynamic creates water deficiency for crops during the dry months, thus limiting the ability of farmers to grow outside the rainy season in the absence of irrigation. This situation is exacerbated by the shallow crop rooting zone, particularly as variability in timing and duration of rainfall increases.

The main source of domestic water for this area is groundwater, manually accessed through shallow hand-dug wells or deeper boreholes with handpumps (Gyau-Boakye 2001; Benyarku 2017). Despite a large reliance, groundwater development is limited in northern Ghana due to its hydrogeological environment. Our study area is underlain by consolidated sedimentary rocks (interbedded mudstone, sandstone, conglomerate, and some limestone) of the Middle Voltaian subprovince, with little or no primary porosity (Gill 1969). Groundwater occurrence is largely through secondary porosity resulting from faulting, fracturing, and weathering, and is generally only understood with adequate seismic investigations. In the absence of such studies, siting of wells is generally accomplished through trial-and-error, contributing to a high rate of unsuccessful boreholes. Forkuor et al. (2013) determined the success rate for obtaining water from boreholes in our study area to be less than 50 percent, the lowest groundwater yielding potential of northern Ghana.

## Water Point Mapping

Field work also included water point mapping (WPM) of water sources in proximity to each community. The purpose of the WPM was to geolocate and characterize the spatial distribution of water availability for crop irrigation. A total of forty-six water sources were identified, then geolocated using the iPhone app MotionX-GPS®. Water points included boreholes, hand-dug wells, standpipes, and dams. In addition to location, elevation and depth to water table attributes were recorded for each water point and integrated into a GIS.

## GIS and Remote Sensing

The spatial extent of agricultural fields and their relationship to water sources were determined and analyzed through RS and GIS methods. Land use and cover surrounding each of the nine communities in our study were determined using high-resolution, multispectral data from the Landsat 8 (L8) Operational Land Imager sensor. We also used land-cover maps (300-m spatial resolution) derived from the ESA Climate Change Initiative.

Google Earth Engine was used to import L8 images from the 2015 rainy season (June–October) and calculate the median NDVI for each pixel. A cloud mask was applied to include only clear pixels and images.² Similar to Anim et al. (2013), we determined an NDVI range of 0.1 to 0.4 to be approximate for representing agriculture in our land-use, land-cover data set (L8-NDVI-LULC). As a conservative approach, we compared our findings with land-cover maps from the ESA Climate Change Initiative (300 m spatial resolution) and clipped our L8-NDVI-LULC to the extent that ESA land-cover maps determined to be cropland.

Once agricultural land was spatially defined for each community, water points were brought into the GIS to analyze accessibility for agricultural irrigation. We define accessibility as the relative ease by which the location of agricultural fields can be reached from a source of water for the purpose of irrigation. This approach is taken from Wang's (2006) work on GIS-based measures of spatial accessibility (see also Wang 2015; Park and Goldberg 2021). Although manual irrigation of crops is widely used by small-scale farmers across sub-Saharan Africa, the quantity of water and the distance from water source to crop are often prohibitive factors. Given the labor-intensive nature of carrying water cans or buckets, Smith et al. (2014) recommended water sources be no more than 50 m away from the area to be irrigated. For the purpose of this study, we used this distance to define potentially irrigable area. This was accomplished by generating a 50-m buffer around each water source in our GIS. Potential irrigable area was thus defined as the intersection of NDVI-derived agriculture with the 50-m walkability buffer.

## Results

This section is organized according to key themes that emerged from the in-depth interviews and were interrogated further using RS and GIS. Three of the strongest themes in the interviews were (1) farmer perceptions of dry spells, (2) the associated impacts of dry spells on smallholder agriculture, and (3) irrigation potentials for agricultural water management. We first present our RS and GIS spatial findings, before adding nuance from the themes emerging from our qualitative research findings.

### GIS and Remote Sensing Findings

Based on our NDVI classification of pixels in the L8 data set, maps of the spatial distribution of agriculture were produced with a 30-m grid for each of the nine communities chosen for analysis (Figure 3).

The amount of land used for agriculture varied widely for the nine communities (3–81 percent), with four having over 68 percent of their total land dedicated to agriculture (Figure 4).

We observed a clustering of communities with respect to percentage of surrounding agriculture land. Each of the five communities due north of the city of Tamale (Tindang, Totienyili, Kpanya, Zoggu, and Nyeko) had greater than 50 percent of their surrounding area dedicated to agriculture, whereas all four communities due east of Tamale (Zobogu, Zakariyili, Tugu, and Botingli) had less than 50 percent used for agriculture (Figure 1 and Figure 4). A comparison between the two clustered groups can be observed in maps of Tindang (> 50 percent) and Tugu (< 50 percent) in Figure 3. Although beyond the scope of this study, potential explanations for the differences include proximity to major roads, population size of communities, and variation in soil suitability. There does not appear to be any correlation between amount of land under cultivation and the number of water points proximate to communities.

The number of water points also varied among communities, with Totienyili having none and Tugu having ten. The community of Tindang had the second highest number of water points (nine) and the largest amount of agricultural area (578.47 ha). Using data on water points and agricultural area, we calculated irrigable field area based on the 50-m walkable zone around each water point as outlined in the methods section (Table 1). Based on the 50-m walkable zone recommended by Smith et al. (2014), the communities of Botingli, Totienyili, and Zobogu had zero area that could be manually irrigated, and Tindang had the most (4.05 ha). Each of the communities had less than 1 percent of their total agricultural land accessible to manual irrigation.

When asked about the impacts of increasingly unpredictable dry spells, farmers outlined several effects. They claimed these climatic variations have led to poor yields, a reduction in pasture for grazing livestock, poor livestock health, food insecurity, and reduced incomes. All of these were said to be exacerbating existing poverty and the inability to achieve broader development objectives in the study area (e.g., see Bawakyillenuo et al. 2016; Balana et al. 2019). Among the various climatic stressors mentioned in the interviews, farmers were particularly concerned about dry spells in the middle of the crop growing season, from late April to early August. More than 80 percent of participants revealed that a dry spell during the crop growing season often leads to reduced yield. These crop-related effects were said to have increased significantly over the past ten years. For example, a seventy-four-year-old farmer summarized a recurring concern raised in most of the interviews:

> Dry spells are too common these days. It will rain continuously this week and you're thinking it's probably time to start planting ... . But then, you plant and the rain will stop for about three to four weeks. In that case, you lose all the seeds. This has been the case for the past six years or more. That is why there is too much food insecurity in this area.

It is important to note that some farmers mentioned specific years, including 1996, 2007, 2011, and 2016, when prolonged dry spells led to poor seed germination or total crop failure. These years align closely with Henchiri et al.'s (2021) analysis of dry spells, years with precipitation anomaly, and El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) in Ghana.

Although the issue of distance was stressed consistently and has also been emphasized in existing research (e.g., Dittoh et al. 2013; Shah et al. 2013; Van Koppen et al. 2013; Villholth 2013), we found a paradox in the interview data. Repeatedly, we observed that even farmers with water points inside their fields were still not practicing hand irrigation. The following quotation represents a typical response in most of the interviews:

> Nobody irrigates even if a borehole is inside their field and the seedlings are dying due to prolonged dry spells. Throughout my fifty-seven years as a farmer, I've never thought of doing that.

Perhaps this paradox serves to reinforce the earlier point that smallholder hand irrigation is not a common cultural practice. Moreover, although many participants acknowledged the importance of distance, gender division of labor was another noteworthy concern raised in the interviews with men. For example, in the following interview excerpt, a male participant stressed the issue of distance, but equally emphasized the importance of gender-related responsibilities within households:

> Also, the hauling of water is not an activity for men; it's for women and girls. I would lose a lot of respect among my fellow men if seen hauling water across the community to irrigate my field ... . So, the distance is a factor, but the division of household work is also a major factor.

Here, beyond the issue of the 50-m walkable zone recommended by Smith et al. (2014; see Table 1), we see how manual irrigation is shaped by preexisting gender and cultural norms.

Finally, farmers commented on seasonality in the availability of some water sources. In particular, boreholes and shallow wells were said to dry up at the peak of the dry season, or during the transition from the dry to the wet season. As a sixty-year-old farmer remarked,

> All the community boreholes dry up during the long dry season. This drying condition extends to the early part of the rainy season when we are experiencing dry spells. For irrigation purposes, the main problem is that the water points are useless at the point when they are needed the most.

As previously noted, the variation in groundwater availability within our study area is attributed to the hydrogeological conditions of the area. Therefore, the potential for manual irrigation is configured by the interlinked processes and realities of distance to wells, existing sociocultural norms, and the hydrogeological conditions of the study area.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Two clear themes emerged from our interactions with farmers. First, increased climate variability is presenting significant challenges to successful farming, thus increasing food insecurity within all communities in our study area. The second theme, related to the first, is the contingent nature of irrigating crops to stabilize food security. This includes cultural barriers to successful irrigation (gender roles and community cooperation), water shortages, complicated local hydrogeology, distance from fields to wells, and costs associated with sourcing and distributing water for irrigation.

Although irrigation is increasingly viewed as a necessity for rural farmers to deal with climate variability, progress in this form of adaptation remains globally stagnate. Mutambara et al. (2016) noted that smallholder irrigation schemes across Africa have largely underachieved, despite significant investment and assistance from public and international development organizations. Factors include farmers' participation and involvement, capital cost recovery, institutional gaps, insufficient maintenance, and the need for recurrent rehabilitation investment. Climate change will undoubtedly require a renewed effort, as well as critical thought to how irrigation systems will be conceived, managed, and maintained.

### Heterogeneity of Success

As previously noted, minimal irrigation is currently undertaken within the study area. Given the absenceof naturally occurring surface water, groundwater and small reservoirs represent the only sources of water for potential irrigation schemes. Four of the nine communities we surveyed had reservoirs (known locally as dams), although none used the water for irrigation purposes. These reservoirs collect surface water runoff during the wet season and have the capacity to retain water through the end of the dry season. Despite their potential to mitigate the effects of dry spells or extend crop production into the dry season, we found that reservoirs in our study area were only used to water livestock and supply domestic needs during the dry season when shallow wells were dry. When asked why the reservoirs were not used to support irrigation, several community members cited lack of pumps and infrastructure to facilitate such irrigation.

Acheampong et al. (2018) found a different situation in the Upper East region of northern Ghana. There, farmers successfully organized and used reservoirs to produce crops throughout the year. Their example is somewhat limited, however, in that their usage and land tenure varied substantially from our area of study. There, land plots were claimed at the time reservoirs were installed, and the principal use of irrigation was for market crops, rather than staple crops. Despite these differences from our study area, a notable point for consideration is that performance and productivity of the small reservoir irrigation schemes was directly linked to contextualized methods and management structures. Additionally, a major difference appears to be the market value of the irrigated crop. Vegetables such as tomato, onion, pepper, and okra command a sufficient market value so as to support the effort and cost associated with irrigation.

Sekyi-Annan et al. (2018) did identify scenarios in the Upper East region with comparable conditions, although their sample size was limited (n = 2). Their study considered a whole-system approach, whereby (1) reservoir performance, (2) conveyance and distribution network efficiency, and (3) field-level water use efficiency were considered. Summarily, they found water availability to be a deterrent to success in one example, and malfunctioning water delivery infrastructure in the second example. Their study only examined the technical aspects of reservoir-based irrigation schemes and did not speak to the attitudes and opinions of farmers with respect to management and maintenance.

One of the challenges with reservoir-based schemes is spatial proximity of agriculture fields from water sources. Widely dispersed smallholder fields necessitate additional delivery infrastructure and subsequent management coordination. This is potentially mitigated by using multiple wells within the area of interest. Such an approach is further advantageous in that groundwater is typically more consistent in supply and less polluted than reservoir

derived water. No research has been published, however, on the use and efficacy of groundwater irrigation schemes in the Northern region of Ghana. In fact, very few data are available on groundwater quantity for this region. Gumma and Pavelic (2013) provided a broad picture of groundwater potential for irrigation within Ghana, with most observations occurring in the Central, Volta, and Upper East regions of the country. They classified the area of our study as moderate to poor for groundwater potential. A more comprehensive picture on groundwater-based irrigation is available for the Upper East region. Namara et al. (2011) noted 61 percent of farmers surveyed practiced irrigation, with 91 percent of them using shallow groundwater. As in other examples already noted in this article, irrigation was confined to market vegetable cultivation, which has appreciably smaller land footprints and related distances to water. Further, the market value of vegetables such as tomatoes and peppers makes it economically feasible to employ seasonal labor for irrigation, further reducing the physical burden of farmers to produce crops. Collectively, these elements contrast starkly with the infrastructure and labor constraints necessary when scaling up for staple crops.

## Opportunities within the Study Area

Given the larger track record of underachievement of smallholder irrigation and the specific cultural and geophysical barriers noted for our study area, what potential options and pathways exist for positive adaptation to climate variability experienced in the Northern region of Ghana? Despite acknowledging that crops are failing and food insecurity is increasing, farmers are reluctant to adopt irrigation measures. This is partially understood from a cultural perspective. (“we have never done this”) and partly from a physical perspective (as demonstrated by our RS analysis showing < 1 percent irrigable land). Like elsewhere across sub-Saharan Africa, the nine communities in our study area are subject to poor health, unemployment, and inadequate community infrastructure. Collectively, these undermine adaptive capacity to climate change and subsequent food insecurity. As food security further erodes, so, too, does adaptive capacity.

An obvious starting point is the need for additional water sources. Nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) have for several years targeted this region for borehole installation and the development of smallholder agriculture (Kwao and Amoak 2022). Our WPM of water sources revealed no less than six NGOs involved in the twelve surveyed boreholes. Additional wells within the area could augment domestic needs, plus provide spatially appropriate sources for irrigation. The complex hydrogeology of this area, however, is challenging to the budgets andstrategies of NGOs interested in water security. Typically, these organizations allocate few resources to the technical siting of a well, instead relying on incomplete data sets and broad hydrogeological assumptions. As noted earlier, this strategy results in a less than 50/50 success rate. Further, the complex hydrogeology dictates location of wells incoherent with irrigation strategies. These challenges aside, focusing on water augmentation amounts to a sectorial approach that excludes the social adaptive capacity of vulnerable subsistence farmers. Thus, sourcing additional water presents a “tangential” solution to the problem.

As the findings of this article demonstrate, the social aspects of water management are, in fact, critical to successful adaptation. These include labor availability in situations where water is manually lifted for hand irrigation. Given these findings, a single policy strategy that relies on investments in small-scale irrigation alone will almost surely fail over time. Raising farmers’ awareness of agro-ecological methods of water conservation practices, such as rainwater harvesting, would be critical for improving farming systems. Farmers in the study region already possess rich local knowledge on soil and water conservation, as well as other agro-ecological practices (Jabik 2022). This local knowledge is an opportunity that could be tapped to address dry spells and agricultural water management. For example, local farming techniques that conserve water, including zai, are already being adopted on a small scale in many communities in the Upper West and Upper East regions of northern Ghana (Danso-Abbeam et al. 2019; Beyuo 2020; Nyantakyi-Frimpong 2020). Other common soil and water conservation techniques include the use of ground mulch, zero-tillage, and the cultivation of crops with relatively short growing cycle (Beyuo 2020; Yomo et al. 2020). Building farmers’ capacity to exchange knowledge on these practices could help address water needs during periods of dry spells.

Similarly, although complex hydrogeological conditions dictate where irrigation schemes are located, farmers also need to be actively involved in this process. Given the scale and complexity of contemporary agricultural problems, such challenges can no longer be solved by linear transfers or top-down approaches, in which technologies developed by “specialists” are passed on to farmers. This top-down approach is deeply problematic and poorly equipped to deal with the problems currently faced by farmers (IPES-Food 2016; Azhoni et al. 2018). It also contrasts sharply with resilience studies in climate change research, which call for colearning, sensitivity to local context, the ability to learn from mistakes, and the recognition of local knowledge (Altieri et al. 2015; Nagothu 2016). Colearning and the integration of local knowledge can support sustainable water management practices as an adaptation to climate change (Nyong

et al. 2007; Altieri et al. 2015; Bezner Kerr et al. 2018). The World Bank has recently produced a document showcasing the importance and positive impacts of farmer-led irrigation development (Izzi et al. 2021). Organizations promoting small-scale irrigation would benefit from adopting the participatory approaches outlined in this document.

A final note concerns whether the findings presented here are reflective of general concerns among farmers in northern Ghana. Overall, dry spells are big issues across northern Ghana (Mavritsakis 2021) and indeed the entire West African Sahel region (Froidurot and Diedhiou 2017; Agbazo et al. 2021). The same applies to gender norms, household division of labor, and implications for agricultural water management. Many case studies by human geographers have shown that northern Ghana is a gender- relationally restrictive sociocultural setting, with implications for human-environment interactions (Kansanga et al. 2019; Vercillo 2022). Although our study sample is relatively small (n = 72), our data come from nine communities selected at random across a wide geographic terrain. From these nine communities, the same cross-cutting concerns emerged. Based on this level of evidence, we would argue that our findings are reflective of the general problems facing small-holder farmers in the dryland areas of Ghana. ■
