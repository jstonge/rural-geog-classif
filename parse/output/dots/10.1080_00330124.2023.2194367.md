# Geographies of Land Ownership Change in the Rural United States: Challenges, Methods, and Possibilities

**Lindsay Shade** ID

*University of Kentucky, USA*

**Levi Van Sant** ID

*George Mason University, USA*

Both academic and popular sources have recently pointed to important changes in rural land ownership across the United States: from consolidation and financialization to racialized dispossession. Although land and land ownership are foundational themes in geography, and a wide range of disciplinary attention focuses on land ownership, much of the basic empirical information about U.S. land ownership remains obscured—due in large part to difficulty accessing and analyzing property data. This article draws on our experiences with a participatory action research project called the Appalachian Land Study to outline some of the challenges of studying the geographies of land ownership in the United States as well as some possibilities for new directions. We briefly situate this kind of research in the context of the overlapping political, economic, and ecological crises of the present, and argue that geographical studies of land ownership in the rural United States have many important applications across the discipline and beyond. **Key Words:** GIS, land, mixed methods, ownership, property, rural.

Who owns the land? It seems a simple enough question. It is, however, one which is alarmingly difficult to answer.

—Massey (1980, 263)

In early 2021 a surprising story in the business press jumped to more popular media: Bill and Melinda Gates were now the largest private farm-land owners in the United States. The former was lauded as a “sustainable agriculture champion” with a long-standing interest in climate change and dubbed “Farmer Bill” (Villarreal 2021; O’Keefe 2021). Others wanted to know more. Exactly how much farmland do the Gateses own? Do they own more land that is not in agricultural production? Where are these landholdings? As Massey (1980) highlighted in the epigraph, these kinds of questions are “alarmingly difficult to answer” (263). One report estimates that the Gateses own 108,845 hectares (268,984 acres) in the United States stretching across nineteen states, 90 percent of which are agricultural land (O’Keefe 2021). These numbers are only estimates, though. There is no publicly accessible centralized data source about land ownership in the United States. Even with scores of journalists and researchers working to uncover the details of the Gateses’ landholdings, it is difficult to know the answers with much certainty, let alone analyze broader trends such as gendered, racialized, and corporate patterns of land ownership and use.

Drawing from our experiences in coal, oil, and gas affected areas of Central Appalachia and in the

more agriculture-oriented Shenandoah Valley of Virginia, we discuss some of the main challenges of rural land ownership research. Broadly, these challenges include administrative barriers to accessing and using records, political gatekeeping, and the proliferation of legal instruments that govern rights to land. As we outline these challenges, we discuss participatory approaches to studying land ownership that can help overcome these barriers and lead to meaningful application of land ownership data to real-world problems.

Although the challenges are many, land ownership research has the potential to unveil key dynamics of persistent inequalities. Land ownership provides political influence for the propertied classes and is a finite asset and a mechanism for the transfer of generational wealth, as well as a crucial source of public revenues (Du Bois 1992; Christophers 2018). As the material basis for society and economy, land carries tremendous cultural significance—it is central to environmental knowledge production, identity formation, and ideological struggles, which are all mediated by the legal systems that govern land ownership and access (Sundberg 2004; Mollett and Faria 2013). Systems of property rights that confer “ownership” also articulate a crucial linkage between capitalist accumulation, state-territorial governance, and citizenship (Polanyi 1944; Blomley 2016; Jacobs 2020). An interrogation of land ownership patterns can support more empirically grounded theorizations of political-economic, cultural, and ecological dynamics while also being immediately relevant to material struggles for land justice (Williams andHolt-Giménez 2017; Horst and Marion 2019). A range of place-based projects, from managerial approaches for environmental quality to more radical struggles such as movements for reparations, can benefit from a grounding in the empirical geographies of land ownership.

Previous studies of U.S. land ownership have made important public and scholarly contributions that suggest their potential to reveal structural inequalities and affect policy (Geisler and Popper 1984; Goldschmidt 1978). For instance, the Appalachian Land Ownership Taskforce (ALOT)—the inspiration for the contemporary Appalachian Land Study discussed herein—analyzed parcel ownership and taxation rates across Appalachia in the 1970s. In an early example of participatory action research, teams of scholars, students, and community members documented how coal and timber corporations siphoned off much of the region's wealth while contributing little to local tax revenues (ALOT 1983). More recently, researchers with the Anti-Eviction Mapping Project and the Urban Praxis Project have analyzed urban real estate speculation and the geographies of predatory landlords.¹ Another recent example, the Land Grab Universities project, mapped the ways that Indigenous dispossession funded U.S. public higher education through the land grant system.² Similarly, the Federation of Southern Cooperatives collaborated with Alcorn State University to track land loss in the Black Belt (Federation of Southern Cooperatives/Land Assistance Fund [FSC/LAF] and Alcorn State University 2018). These land ownership research projects, predominantly led by grassroots organizations, have contributed to scholarly debates, popular education, community organizing, and policy work (GRAIN 2008; Scott 2009; Gaither 2016; Akers and Seymour 2018; Penniman 2018; Lomawaima et al. 2021).

Each of these studies highlights diverse methodological challenges ranging from rapidly changing dynamics of divestment and reinvestment to inadequate and inconsistent record keeping. We address a gap in the literature by focusing on the challenges and possibilities of empirical land ownership research in the rural United States, drawing from our particular sites but with an eye toward broader application. We hope that our discussion will spur more research on rural U.S. land ownership because, at the most basic level, much less is known about rural U.S. land ownership when compared to urban areas. Because the two are inherently linked, studying rural land ownership can help flesh out a relational and comparative analysis of broader trends, such as gentrification, agricultural intensification, energy transitions, and financialization of farm and forest lands. There is also increasing attention to the place of rural politics in the broader U.S. political landscape (Van Sant and Bosworth 2017); much of

this work suggests the importance of land ownership (Ashwood 2018; De Lara 2018).

We begin with an overview of the Appalachian Land Study and how the legacy of participatory action research in Appalachia informs our work, which has been collaboratively designed and implemented through distributed networks over the past five years. We then situate our approach within broader theoretical engagements with the contemporary dynamism of rural U.S. land ownership, which leads us to a discussion of the challenges and barriers we have encountered in our efforts to document land ownership trends and their impacts on public revenues. There are important place-based divergences across the rural United States, but our discussion draws out methodological issues that are broadly applicable to land ownership research in rural resource-based economies.

## Background: The Appalachian Land Study

From early land grabs by absentee speculators to later waves of timber production and energy extraction, scholars of Appalachia have long addressed how regional land tenure dynamics intersect with resource extraction economies (Gaventa 1982; Salstrom 1994; Banks 1995; Billings and Blee 2000). As part of a larger story of U.S. enclosure, colonization, and industrialization, these scholars have documented eighteenth- and nineteenth-century land grabs by East Coast speculators that established contemporary land ownership paradigms in Appalachia and entrenched the domination of extractive industries within legal and bureaucratic regimes for governing land and minerals (Rasmussen 1994; Dunaway 1995; Stoll 2017).

A key theme of this literature is how the structure of land and mineral ownership shaped class formation, producing and then sustaining systemic inequalities (ALOT 1983; Dunaway 1995). In particular, a groundbreaking participatory study that emerged from grassroots constituencies after the onslaught of surface mining in the region in the late 1970s showed that corporate and absentee ownership shifted historically diverse Appalachian economies toward mono-economies based on primary resource extraction, with negative effects on public revenue systems, local government capacity, and economic development outcomes (ALOT 1983; Stoll 2017). ALOT was organized through county, state, and regional committees that collaborated to produce reports about land ownership, property taxes, and socioeconomic inequality for each of the six states included in the study: Kentucky, West Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, Alabama, and Virginia (ALOT 1983). Our work is a direct descendent of the ALOT, and a new collective of scholars and stakeholders formed in late 2016 to update and expand the study.³The collective is made up of scholars, environmental justice activists, teachers, retired coal miners, and other frontline community members. The first author, along with other collaborators, launched the Appalachian Land Study through a two-year collaborative process, and continues to lead the initiative to date.⁴ The second author became involved in 2019 and has adapted the methodology to the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia. The new study has made several methodological changes, given the availability of more advanced technologies, such as geographic information systems (GIS), but we nonetheless face similar barriers as our predecessors, particularly with quality and accessibility of records. We have also added an emphasis on the legal and cultural institutions that sustain local and regional land ownership inequalities at a critical juncture in the ongoing restructuring of the Appalachian economy. Our approach also reflects a theoretical shift in academic action research toward systemic transition and regenerative economies currently being led by frontline communities (Ponce 2022; United Frontline Table 2020). Whereas ALOT was influenced by the explanatory power of world systems theory for understanding development and underdevelopment as linked processes, the current Appalachian Land Study is similarly animated by efforts to understand persistent inequalities in the context of rapidly changing global commodity markets.

Coal industry production and employment have plummeted in the region over the past decade, but new forms of resource extraction are ascendant: investments in natural gas, woody biomass, timber, and the purchase of large tracts of forest land by absentee financial institutions (Spence et al. 2013; Randle, Barlow, and Gunnoe 2015). There has also been a recent push by national and international conservation organizations to promote solar development on former mine lands. For example, a recent strategy report from The Nature Conservancy argues that governments and nonprofit organizations should partner with legacy land barons seeking new sources of revenue to attract solar energy companies who may be interested in leasing land (The Nature Conservancy 2020). These developments highlight the role of land access and ownership for energy and economic transitions (Taylor et al. 2014; Tarus, Hufford, and Taylor 2017). It is within this wider context that the Appalachian Land Study Collective produces participatory analysis of land ownership trends as well as the local political and cultural dynamics that influence land ownership (Young 2017).

## *Shifting Geographies of Rural U.S. Land Ownership*

Shifting geographies of ownership, divestment, and development in Appalachia parallel wider trends that

have been documented in geographic scholarship. As the opening anecdote about Bill Gates's investment in agricultural land suggests, the geographies of U.S. land ownership are in flux (Fairbairn 2020). This dynamism extends beyond agricultural land ownership. It includes virtually all land-use types, from landscapes of rural extraction to deindustrial sites and metropolitan gentrification. Economic geographers have highlighted the ways that the recent dynamism in U.S. land markets is driven by efforts to create a financial fix for the crises of neoliberal capitalism: rounds of urban reinvestment following the 2008 housing crisis (Shelton 2018), the financialization of timberlands across the countryside (Gunnoe 2014), and regional reconfigurations of housing and labor markets (the birth of so-called “Zoom towns”) in response to the COVID-19 pandemic (Florida and Ozimek 2021). Of course, as geographers have long indicated, these rounds of capital investment are structurally paired with disinvestment and “organized abandonment” (Massey 1994; Gilmore 2007). In the rural United States, these “sacrifice zones” (Shade 2015; Edelman 2021) are scattered throughout hollowed-out coal communities in Appalachia and cutover timberlands of the Deep South to the arid ranchlands of the West. A related body of work emphasizes the increasing importance of accumulation via land rents, as opposed to commodity production (Andreucci et al. 2017). Kay (2017), for instance, highlighted the ways that conservation easements on working lands are an increasingly common form of rural rentierism. Although the precise effects of these processes on empirically documented patterns of landholding are not well known (although see Bailey et al. 2021 for a case study), economic geographers highlight increasing rates of land consolidation as well as absentee and institutional ownership (Epstein, Haggerty, and Gosnell 2022).

Meanwhile, research on Black and Indigenous geographies highlights the longue durée of colonial and racial dispossession of land that continues in the present, with property ownership systems as both a rationale and modality of ongoing dispossession (Woods 2017; Bhandar 2018; Palmer 2020). It is in and through this context that the economic geographies outlined earlier are (re)articulated (Sommerville 2018; Curley 2021). Recent studies highlight a range of mechanisms that sustain settler-colonialism through financial and state-territorial control of landed property: institutionalized forms such as direct state violence (Dunbar-Ortiz 2014) and U.S. Department of Agriculture discrimination (Wright et al. 2020), as well as predatory private lending, the compulsion of the market, and interpersonal threats of violence (Desmond 2016). Work on the colonial and racial dynamics of land dispossession also highlights how the dispossessed draw onand rearticulate long-standing traditions of resistance, refusal, and survival (Estes 2019; Reese 2019).

Given these dynamics, and the generalized crises of climate change and COVID-19, it is not surprising that political struggles related to land use and ownership are also shifting and intensifying (Malm 2020; Sultana 2021). There are significant innovations in managerial approaches to land, such as the spread of conservation easements and increased non-governmental organization promotion of community land trusts. At the same time, populist White settler occupations and Indigenous and Black-led movements for reparations suggest a punctuated rearticulation of long-standing struggles over land (Lapena 2016). The outcomes of these various struggles remain uncertain, but it is clear that the politics of land is an increasingly explicit and prominent part of the U.S. political landscape.

Despite the empirical significance of land ownership data to a wide range of research on these and related geographic concerns, researchers are faced with significant challenges to gathering, processing, and using land ownership data: political stonewalling, data accessibility and quality, evolving property laws and attendant fragmentation of estates, and more. In what follows, we outline some of our experiences with these challenges, as well as our efforts to address them through participatory methods developed in collaboration with rural stakeholders. We highlight three areas where we hope our methods might inform others with similar goals: participatory planning and design, approach to estimating ownership and taxation disparities, and applications of GIS and spatial statistics.

## *Designing a Regional Land Study: Methods and Challenges*

Following the model of its predecessor, the Appalachian Land Study employs participatory methods. Participatory action research facilitates participation, action, and reflection by those most affected by the research and its themes (Fals-Borda 1987). The Appalachian Land Study draws on the transformative potential of people's connection to land and place, which opens pathways for political dialogue in areas where civic space has been severely undermined by two centuries of corporate domination. Although a comprehensive account of the two-year participatory planning process is not feasible in the space of this article, we want to highlight how collaborative study design can ground the objectives and methods of land ownership research in people's lived experiences—a methodological advantage that also requires significantly more time and resources to implement.

Our process began with an online survey, widely distributed through organizational and academic contacts and listservs, to determine interest and

priorities. Based on the results, we organized a day-long forum attended by sixty-five academics and community activists in late 2016, which involved concept mapping and discussion about the overall vision, stakeholder identification, other relevant research, lessons from the 1979 study, and coordination and implementation. Many other meetings, conference calls, and e-mails over a listserv with more than 200 participants followed. These all shaped the study's goals and methods, with early emphasis on establishing shared values to guide the vision and framework. In these early stages, professional facilitation and participatory exercises such as timelines, pile sorts, and power mapping, drew out people's common knowledge, concerns, and questions. From 2016 to 2018, five regional retreats were held in Kentucky, Tennessee, West Virginia, and Virginia, which each brought together between twenty and sixty community leaders and scholar-activists from across the region to learn about key issues such as the expansion of natural gas infrastructure and financialization of forestland ownership, as well as diverse methods such as corporate research, oral histories, and researching local tax books (Appalachian Land Study Collective 2017, forthcoming).

The first convening in 2016 began with issue identification, in which participants brainstormed about key questions and studied the earlier ALOT reports, whereas subsequent convenings established guiding values and principles and defined research objectives. At each convening, paid note-takers kept detailed notes, which collaborator Betsy Taylor of the Livelihoods Knowledge Exchange Network coded for key themes using NVivo qualitative coding software. Synthesis from these coded notes pointed to emerging research questions, which Taylor and Shade drafted and shared with the wider collective for feedback. In 2020, the objectives in Table 1 were finalized with input from community-based collaborators who attended a regional peer exchange retreat in Harlan County, Kentucky. The third objective was added during that retreat, after community partners noted that the emphasis on social change was not explicit in the formal research questions. During the same meeting, collaborators also noted that interview methods we suggested made communities feel “researched upon.” To address the concern, a community leader from southern West Virginia and Shade collaborated on a revised interview guide and training featuring more conversational approaches. This iterative process of dialogue, coding, and synthesis has shaped the framing, objectives, and methods for the Appalachian Land Study.

Furthermore, working at multiple scales with diverse groups is time intensive; as noted, our research questions were crafted over a number of years. Not only has this been challenging for academics who are incentivized to publish rapidly, but the political urgency of understanding who owns land in the context of extreme economic distress and shifting economic landscapes must be balanced with longer term community engagement. People also have changing priorities and responsibilities—for instance, the COVID-19 pandemic significantly affected our ability to carry out community-based work—and so active participation and leadership in a long-term study is constantly shifting.

Still, based on conversations with leaders of the 1979 ALOT who advocated for strengthened community engagement in a new land study, we have prioritized grassroots involvement.⁵ Awareness of the study continues to spread horizontally, bringing new organizations, communities, and individuals into the collective, filling gaps where other members have shifted their attention to other personal or community needs. As work has shifted to more local implementation, the organizing structure evolved to a smaller collective of individuals from Tennessee, Virginia, West Virginia, and Kentucky who are connecting quantitative data about land ownership and public revenues with qualitative analysis and pragmatic policy proposals (Counts 2020). For example, Terran Young, a journalist from southwest Virginia who completed a fellowship with the land study, collected oral histories about the erasure of Black communities in Wise County, Virginia, which she presented alongside land ownership data in a series of community dialogues (ALSC forthcoming).⁶ Local leadership such as this facilitates relationship building with decision makers and is also important for gaining data access, where county officials are more responsive to their local electorate. Young’s work in Wise County resulted in an improved county assessment office Web site with more transparent information about how assessments are done. Since then, a grassroots group called the Southwest Virginia New Economy Network formed and worked with the land study to create and share maps

and data during public meetings in seven counties about the role of land in economic justice, resulting in a collaboration with a Virginia state legislator on a draft tax reform proposal that would target the largest landowners (Thomas and Kelly 2022).

The land study’s participatory methods have brought rural stakeholders to engage in critical conversations about who controls and benefits from land, while also critically interrogating the records themselves. For example, the Appalachian Land Study teams in West Virginia found that tax maps in some counties align closely with residents’ lived knowledge of where property boundaries should be located, whereas both boundaries and assessed values in other counties (particularly those with a legacy of high rates of surface mining) diverge widely from what participants would deem “accurate.” A number of factors—ranging from local corruption to third-party privatization of assessment and public records—can affect the quality and availability of public land records. Corporate interests and regional power brokers frequently use opacity to maintain or expand their ownership. Resident research collaborators in both northern and southern West Virginia report, for instance, that land companies use their pull with local officials and the inherent changes in topography that occur as a result of mining and fracking operations to quietly appropriate land from adjacent properties and expand their holdings (Notes from Appalachian Land Study peer exchange at Nettie, West Virginia, June 30, 2021; Interview, Doddridge County, West Virginia, July 16, 2014). In short, the necessity of collaborating with local communities is essential not only to make the research accessible and actionable, but also to “ground truth” records based on local knowledge (Pickles 1995). We work with local teams of residents and public officials, where possible, to review records and elicit contextual details, as described in the next sections.

For example, our work in Mingo County, West Virginia, found that 85 percent of resident landowners controlled just 3.5 percent of the parcels, and the top ten legacy landholding and energy extraction companies hold 45 percent of deeded property but shoulder less than 5 percent of the county's property tax burden (Figure 1).⁷ These data were shared in a series of public events in the county, which spurred story sharing between older participants about changes in corporate structures and mining technologies in their lifetimes. As Mickey McCoy, a resident of Martin County, Kentucky, put it:

> People need a land study because a lot of times they haven't had the oral historian in the family who has passed it on like my grandmother passed it on to me. So, they need to know where this can be found; it needs to be gathered and collected. (Interview, June 11, 2021)

These data can be surprisingly challenging to access and analyze in underfunded rural counties. Because tax maps contain spatial representations of ownership and are increasingly available as GIS files, we use property valuation assessment (PVA) records for quantitative and spatial analysis of ownership. PVA offices are charged with assessing the value of private property and sending quarterly tax bills based on locally established formulas. They maintain physical tax books that contain key details about properties, including parcel size, land use, building structures, and market values. Most counties also have digital PVA records in the form of spreadsheets, which can be accessed through open records requests in most cases. These spreadsheets are often not accompanied by metadata, however, and typically require significant effort to parse. They also do not include unique taxpayer identifiers, so linking multiple property holdings to a single entity can be challenging, especially considering that many corporations also operate under multiple names, subsidiaries, or both. Therefore, acquiring data and preparing them for analysis is a cumbersome andtechnical task. Because PVA records list taxpayers, rather than the owner on the deed, these records can only be considered a proxy for ownership, but in most cases, it is reasonable to assume that the taxpayer is also the owner (see also Randle, Barlow, and Gunnoe 2015; Macaulay and Butsic 2017).

A number of issues complicate the use of PVA records for the study of land ownership. The quality and availability of these records is highly variable: There is no standardization between counties or even within counties—as record administrators change, so, too, do the standards and practices of record keeping. In the example of Mingo County mentioned earlier, we found that numerous parcels held by one of the largest landholders were recorded as being zero hectares in official deed records. Interviews with county officials revealed that parcel size was routinely recorded inaccurately to simplify accounting in connection with a tax incentive program for managed timberlands. Comparison of this landholder's parcels in other states and counties revealed a similar pattern of inaccurate parcel size and valuation numbers. Moreover, many counties and states do not have GIS or sophisticated databases. We have found partnerships between professional academic researchers and locally informed rural residents essential to gathering, interpreting, and translating land records into useful maps and statistics that can serve as a springboard for public discussions about land ownership and public revenues.

Shade typically requests and processes data using a combination of Microsoft Excel and QGIS, and where feasible, works with community-based stakeholders and courthouse officials to further ground truth the records and analysis. We review summary statistics about concentrated ownership and assessed values, including parcel maps when available, and look for discrepancies between records and peoples' direct knowledge of corporate practices and property relations. In southern West Virginia and southwestern Virginia, maps and figures resulting from that process have been used as a basis for dialogue in a series of public forums about economic transition (for media coverage, see Justice 2019; Workman 2019; Counts 2020). Still, the political sensitivity of questions of land and taxation have led to reluctance among some team members to approach county courthouses. We found through inside relationships that, when nonresidents visit the courthouse to gather this information and ask for clarity about records, workers are often grilled by their supervisors about researchers' intentions after we leave (Interview, Mingo County, West Virginia, December 14, 2018).

Political stonewalling is further compounded by underfunding of counties, a direct consequence of undertaxation of land in extraction zones. Previous studies in the Appalachian region found that the

share of local tax burden is borne disproportionately by smallholders compared to corporate entities who hold the majority of land in coalfield counties (ALOT 1983; Spence et al. 2013). Our research has identified an antagonism between state revenue offices that seek to consolidate land records and create public GIS portals and local county offices that are resistant to making property valuation data easily accessible. There are myriad possible reasons, but one issue is that counties are incentivized to limit records access, as they can sell data to third-party vendors as one strategy to make up for shortfalls in local revenues (Horn 2021). Some counties have also resisted sharing data for public research purposes such as our study, despite being covered by open records laws, and have sought to charge exorbitant fees to comply with data requests, ranging from $5,000 to $20,000 per county, per tax year. This problem has significantly stalled our work in Kentucky, leading to further inequalities in data access in which resident stakeholders are unable to access records that are hoarded by private data vendors who sell the data to speculators and developers. This also creates an ethical conundrum for our work: We want to maintain positive relationships with county officials and avoid undermining their interests, but also make data publicly accessible for social and environmental justice purposes, which could likewise inadvertently subsidize the cost of business for land speculators (Otts 2019).

## GIS and Spatial Statistics

On the other end of the spectrum, states such as North Carolina and Tennessee have implemented robust, centralized state GIS systems. The consistency and quality of data in North Carolina has facilitated more rapid and complex statistical analyses. Shade worked with a graduate student at Appalachian State University to gather PVA data from twenty-nine western North Carolina counties, code for absentee ownership based on zip code, and calculate a Gini coefficient and Lorenz curve to estimate ownership inequality in each county. The student also completed econometric modeling of the relationship between high rates of concentrated absentee ownership and several socioeconomic variables, including county poverty rate, median household income, infrastructure investment, level of education, tax revenues, and mortality and disease rates (Meadows 2018). Building on Shade's (2017) prior work and the collective knowledge of land study researchers in both academic and grassroots venues, we also crafted an interview guide to gather qualitative data from local tax assessors. These interviews provide a richer context through which to interpret quantitative findings, and are also important for documenting the qualitative dimensions of property as a product of mundane human activities,such as record-keeping practices, which are integral to both the emergence and maintenance of systems of landed property (Rose 1985; Bhandar 2016).

We see many possibilities for using GIS and spatial statistics in our work, and are in the process of organizing more than 20 million PVA records covering four states into an interactive digital mapping system. As interactive maps have become more popular through mainstream media sources, we aim to deliver on an expectation of comprehensive data accessible through a well-designed interface. These products require significant skills beyond those possessed by most academic geographers, and as discussed earlier, can be challenging to create due to discrepancies in data quality and access. We anticipate building digital tools around narratives that can inspire further political engagement and action in local and state venues, but that will always be accompanied by additional community-based and qualitative efforts.

Van Sant’s work in the Shenandoah Valley also combines GIS and qualitative methods, but has a slightly different research trajectory. The COVID-19 pandemic forced them to change their plan to develop a wide network of community collaborators before beginning mapping of land ownership in the Shenandoah Valley. Instead, with the help of Shade and undergraduate research assistants, they initiated the quantitative phase of research virtually during the COVID lockdown. Using similar methods, they mapped rates of absentee ownership and degree of ownership concentration in the ten-county region. As public health restrictions have eased, they have developed research partners and are beginning qualitative research using the maps as prompts for interviews and focus groups. This kind of “map elicitation” method has multiple benefits: First, as mentioned earlier, it can help improve the mapping through a process of ground truthing; second, it provides an intimate forum for the spread of scholarship to community members; and third, the response of research participants to the maps and interview questions provides important qualitative data about the ways that community members interpret and experience land ownership change. Given the many challenges researchers face when attempting participatory land ownership research, we highlight this approach as a variation that nevertheless integrates qualitative methods and GIS.

Beyond the authors’ projects, GIS and spatial statistics about land ownership can inform a multitude of scholarly and public advocacy endeavors. For example, Khadka et al. (2021) recently examined how changes in land ownership structure correlated to changes in land use and land cover over sixteen years in three Kentucky counties. Similarly, extension faculty at the University of California created the CalLands portal to track land ownership and land-cover statistics (Macaulay and Butsic 2017).

As early as 2005, Fraser, Gayawali, and Schellas (2005) examined how rates of African American land ownership in the Black Belt correlate with forestry practices and indicators of socioeconomic well-being. Linking the increasingly powerful technologies offered through GIS packages with land ownership research can inform cutting-edge environmental and social policy research, bridge quantitative and qualitative subdisciplines in human-environment research, and provide accessible storytelling tools in public scholarship initiatives like the Appalachian Land Study.

## Conclusion

We have argued that critical engagement with land records and participatory methods that prioritize the insights of rural people in resource-based economies can both document and respond to the dynamics of U.S. land ownership. Although we have focused on Appalachia, the methods are more broadly applicable. A firm grasp on who owns the land can inform a range of economic, environmental, and cultural geographic inquiries. From rapidly changing land and commodity markets to mundane legal transactions such as mergers and acquisitions, land ownership is always in flux and therefore inherently challenging to document. We attempt to deal with this complexity by approaching records as material artifacts, while using available data to elicit public engagement about entrenched inequalities in rural environments. In this moment of overlapping political, economic, and ecological crises, we are hopeful that more land ownership research will engage diverse publics with important questions about who can access, make decisions about, and benefit from land. ■
