# "What Is Your Primary Language?": Spatial Considerations of Primary Language Identification in a Multilingual Rural Region

Yujia Pan, Jiazhen Sun, Ling Bian, Pierpaolo Di Carlo, and Jeff Good

*University at Buffalo, USA*

In small-scale multilingual societies, an individual uses a large number of languages and usually identifies one of them as the *primary language*. Although empirically observable, primary language has been significantly underexplored, particularly the spatiality of its identification. It is not clear whether and how place is related to why one language might be seen as being more significant than other languages. This study predicts the primary language used by multilingual individuals in an indigenous rural region of Cameroon based on two types of place: the residence of multilingual individuals and the birthplace of their family members. The research goals are supported by a random forest model using spatial-sociolinguistic data of the region. Results show that primary language can be predicted at a high accuracy. The residence of multilingual individuals and the birthplace of their family members capture the spatiality behind primary language identification. This work adds to the current discussion of the space-language connection and is valuable for maintaining the endangered languages in small-scale rural societies. **Key Words:** multilingualism, place, primary language, small-scale rural society, space-language connection.

In a multilingual environment, individuals routinely use three or more languages, a phenomenon referred to as *multilingualism* (Kemp 2009). The phenomenon has been a central topic of sociolinguistic inquiry, but most studies have focused on urban environments (Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan 2010; Lüpke 2016; Vaughan and Singer 2018). Multilingualism received much less attention in indigenous rural societies, in particular small-scale societies. These societies are characterized by notable linguistic diversity and extensive individual-level multilingualism. Multilingualism in small-scale societies is referred to as “small-scale multilingualism” (Lüpke 2016). It has been widely observed across continents and was likely the norm in human prehistory (François 2012; Singer and Harris 2016; Cobbinah et al. 2017; Chernela 2018; Di Carlo, Good, and Ojong Diba 2019).

Among the large number of languages used, individuals typically identify one of the languages as their *primary language*, and this will often be the one that is most frequently used. A primary language is tightly linked to individuals' linguistic identity, sense of community belonging, and gain in social capital in comparison to other languages that individuals use. Although empirically observable, the concept of primary language has been significantly underexplored. At present, we lack even baseline knowledge of its characteristics, the driving factors behind its existence, and its potential implications in a range of contexts.

The identification of a primary language is inevitably related to multilingual acquisition, which is the process where individuals acquire the capacity to use

multiple languages (Dressler 2015; Ellis 2016). In the context of small-scale multilingualism, multilingual acquisition includes the acquisition of the primary language. The set of acquired languages collectively makes up an individual's language profile, which is a core part of their linguistic repertoire (Gumperz 1964). There has been active discussion of multilingual acquisition in the linguistic literature, but rarely from a spatial perspective (Paul 2020).

Spatial considerations are as essential as sociolinguistic considerations for understanding multilingual acquisition but are often seen as a backdrop within which multilingual acquisition takes place (Blommaert, Collins, and Slembrouck 2005; Veselinova and Booza 2009; Di Carlo and Pizziolo 2012). Even when spatial factors are acknowledged, a simplified approach to conceptualizing space is employed (Ambrose and Williams 1991; Breton 1993; Williams 1996; Di Carlo 2022). For example, a multilingual region is often presented as a homogeneous area on a map, as found, for instance, in standard linguistic reference sources such as Ethnologue (Eberhard, Simons, and Fennig 2022). More recent studies have substantially deepened our understanding of the role space plays in multilingualism, such as how multiple languages coexist in space, diffuse through geographical pathways, and migrate across regions (Prochazka and Vogl 2017; Ranacher, Van Gijn, and Derungs 2017; Hiippala et al. 2020; Paul 2020; Ranacher et al. 2021; Väisänen et al. 2022). These studies put space at the forefront of multilingualism research.

Published by Taylor & Francis Group, LLC.In the literature, various factors have been discussed that are believed to drive multilingual acquisition, including social, cultural, historical, political, economic, religious, and environmental considerations (Hua and Wei 2005; Canagarajah and Wurr 2011; Nicholls, Eadie, and Reilly 2011; Paul 2020; Westergaard 2021). Among them, community influence and kinship influence are two widely acknowledged considerations (Grenier 1984; Chiswick, Lee, and Miller 2005; Kharkhurin 2008; Paradis 2008; Skutnabb-Kangas et al. 2009; Chevalier 2012; Paradowski and Bator 2018).

In small-scale rural settings, multilingual acquisition is essentially a spatial experience fused with social, cultural, linguistic, and environmental sentiments. Two types of place, the residence of multilingual individuals and the birthplaces of their family members, implicate the influence of community and kinship from the lens of spatiality.

In the residential community where multilingual individuals live, their languages are typically acquired through daily activities with friends, neighbors, and other people with whom individuals interact. The influence of the residence place often leads to a high level of acquisition and frequent use of the acquired languages (Hoffmann and Ytsma 2004; Hua and Wei 2005; Paradis 2008). Hereafter, we term languages acquired from the residential community *residence languages* to capture their spatial essence.

In the home, multilingual individuals acquire languages from family members (Barron-Hauwaert 2003; Braun and Cline 2010). Close family members, such as parents, acquired their languages early in life at their birthplaces. Due to movement and intermarriage between communities (or between regions), languages used by parents at home are often different from those acquired by multilingual individuals from their residential communities. The influences from parents are high in quality and frequency (Barron-Hauwaert 2003; Braun and Cline 2010; Unsworth 2016; Diskin 2020). Spouses, also important family members, affect individuals' multilingual profiles as well, although only later in their lives (Chiswick, Lee, and Miller 2005). We refer to languages acquired from family members' birthplaces as *birthplace languages* to express their spatiality.

Among many underexplored spatial considerations in small-scale multilingualism, it is not clear how place is related to why one language might be seen as being more significant than other languages in the linguistic identity of an individual (Lüpke and Storch 2013). It is also not clear what role the two different types of place play in the choice of primary language. Answering these questions is a key first step in understanding the spatiality of primary language identification.

To fill the research gaps, we aim to evaluate the impacts of place on primary language identification

in a rural region of Cameroon, whose societies show extensive small-scale multilingualism (Lüpke 2016; Di Carlo, Good, and Ojong Diba 2020). The objectives of this study are twofold: (1) to predict the primary language of multilingual individuals based on the two types of place, and (2) to evaluate the impact of the two types of places on primary language identification. To support these research goals, spatial-sociolinguistic data of multilingual individuals in the region are analyzed using a random forest classification model.

Results of this work provide a baseline understanding of primary languages, the spatiality behind their identification, and interactions between language and space. The examination of linguistic identity at the individual level in small-scale societies, in particular, advances the systematic exploration of this domain as previous work has examined it primarily at the community level (Sutton 1997; Slotta 2012). In addition, this study informs policies in language preservation for many small-scale multilingual societies whose endangered linguistic ecologies are an immediate and prominent concern (Pakendorf, Dobrushina, and Khanina 2021).

## Literature Review

### Primary Language vs. Mother Tongue

It is common for a multilingual individual to treat one of the languages they know as their *mother tongue*, *first language*, or *native language*. Much work within linguistics is based on the idea that a category like native speaker is inherently valid, despite its clear shortcomings when applying this concept to distinctively different societies (Davies 2003).

Moreover, we should emphasize that in rural environments, the sociolinguistic situation can be highly complex. Due to the embeddedness of an individual within multiple networks of sociolinguistic affiliation, the applicability of the terms like mother tongue, first language, and native speaker has been contested (Lüpke and Storch 2013). In the same vein, the meaning of a term such as *primary language* arising from indigenous rural societies cannot be immediately seen as equivalent to its meaning in urban contexts.

Aligning our understanding of a notion like primary language in a rural context to its closest urban equivalent is also a concern for decisions around language policy and planning (Lo Bianco 2010) that often fail to take into account the complications of an individual's multiple sociolinguistic affiliations (Paolillo 2006). Such concerns extend beyond the discipline of linguistics when studying the relationship between linguistic diversity and other social factors, whether in urban or rural contexts. These concerns have not received adequate considerationin multidisciplinary research, which often adopts overly simplistic approaches to linguistic identity (Esteban, Mayoral, and Ray 2012).

### *Small-Scale Multilingualism*

From a regional perspective, residents consider the local languages to be more or less equal in status in their society, with none of them being more socially prestigious or having any overall higher value than any other (Lüpke 2016; Pakendorf, Dobrushina, and Khanina 2021). From an individual-level perspective, multilingual individuals routinely use a large number of local languages, and their multilingual profiles differ from each other because the profiles typically emerge out of these individuals' own distinctive trajectories of community and kinship influences.

This form of multilingualism contrasts with the much more studied forms of multilingualism

typically found in urban environments (Extra and Yağmur 2004; Mc Laughlin 2009). In urban settings, languages are typically assigned to different positions within a prestige hierarchy, which affects multilingual acquisition and use (Blommaert, Collins, and Slembrouck 2005). The language of the state is normally at the top of the hierarchy and used in high-prestige domains, including official settings. The arrangement of other languages depends on specific local factors and will typically be quite low within the hierarchy. Results from studies of urban multilingualism, therefore, should not be extended directly to indigenous rural societies.

### Study Area

The study area, Lower Fungom, is a rural region of Cameroon (Figure 1). Geographically, Lower Fungom is part of sub-Saharan Africa, covering


**Figure 1** *The study area of Lower Fungom.*approximately 240 square kilometers in a hilly area with rugged terrain (Di Carlo and Pizziolo 2012; Di Carlo and Good 2014). The region is dominated by a tropical monsoon climate, forest-savanna mosaic vegetation, and well-distributed rivers and streams. Infrastructure in the region is underdeveloped. There is no centralized electricity or running water, and travel is primarily on foot.

There are thirteen village-chiefdoms in the region, i.e., polities headed by a chief and traditionally politically independent. The core settled areas of the villages are generally found on gentle hilltops, ranging between 600 m and 1,000 m, for purposes of defense (Di Carlo and Good 2014). These villages tend to occupy the spatial extent necessary to sustain the livelihood of residents. The population size varies between villages, ranging from 100 to more than 1,000. No official census for Lower Fungom is available. The village population size is estimated by extensive field observations and other sources (Appendix A). The current village populations are unknown due to movements of people out of the region as a result of civil conflict in Cameroon.

At the regional level, Lower Fungom has long been known to be culturally distinctive and linguistically diverse (Hombert 1980). There are two different characterizations of the region's linguistic diversity. The scholarly linguistic classification categorizes the speech varieties of the region's thirteen villages into eight distinct languages (varieties). It also recognizes that, even when two villages are classified as speaking the same language, each village is associated with a distinctive variety (i.e., its own dialect). Many of the languages and varieties are endangered (Good et al. 2011; Di Carlo 2018).

By contrast, local residents treat each of the thirteen village-chiefdoms of the region as speaking its own “language,” summing to thirteen local languages. Language names are often given that are the same as the village names among residents.

We adopt the perception of local residents in this study as it is directly relevant to our goal to assess the impact of place on primary language and because it also reflects how residents understand their own multilingual profiles. Also widely used in Lower Fungom are two additional varieties associated with the village of Mekaf and its associated hamlet of Small Mekaf, both near Lower Fungom in the neighboring region. These fifteen languages are analyzed as the primary languages in this study.

At the individual level, the highly multilingual residents use six languages on average, with the extreme being nineteen languages (Table 1). The languages include the residence languages, birthplace languages, and languages from other villages inside or outside Lower Fungom (Di Carlo and Pizziolo 2012; Esene Agwara 2013; Di Carlo 2015).

## Data Preprocessing

To prepare for the random forest analysis, we derive two sets of features from the two data sets (Table 3).

One set of features corresponds to the multilingual individual data set. The residence village of the multilingual individuals is expressed as a nominal feature, and their gender is expressed as a binary feature where 1 indicates male and 0 indicates female. The second set of features corresponds to the family member data set. The birth village of the family member is expressed as a nominal feature. The languages they use are expressed as a series of binary features, one feature for each language used by each family member, where 1 indicates the use of the language and 0 otherwise. The gender of the family members is expressed as a binary feature where 1 indicates male and 0 indicates female. The features in this set are divided into paternal, maternal, and spousal subsets. All features used in this study, totaling 124, are listed in Appendix C.

## Methodology

We enter the two sets of features into the random forest model to predict the primary languages. Next, we evaluate the contributions of spatial-linguistic features.

### *Random Forest Classification Model*

The random forest model is a machine learning method to predict observations into classes based on features, where the features represent the driving factors behind the classification (Breiman 2001). By design, the model consists of an ensemble of decision trees. The prediction process includes atraining phase and a testing phase. The training phase captures the relationship between the features and the classes, and the testing phase predicts the class for observations using the relationships obtained during the training phase. During training, each tree uses a series of randomly selected features to predict the class. The process iterates until the results converge to a point where the predicted classes closely approximate the actually observed classes. The majority vote from the entire ensemble of tree predictions is used as the final prediction (Breiman et al. 1984; Bishop 2006; Gislason, Benediktsson, and Sveinsson 2006).

Among all machine learning methods, the random forest model is well known both for its tolerance of small sample sizes and its ability to make use of a large number of features (Qi 2012; Luan et al. 2020). The model also identifies the contribution of each of the features and helps differentiate their role in the prediction, which is especially valuable in cases where there are a large number of potentially predictive features (Izmirlian 2004; Tagliamonte and Baayen 2012; Fassnacht et al. 2014; Jiao et al. 2020). We deem the random forest model appropriate for this study due to several considerations. First, our sample size is limited given the scarcity of available data on primary languages in communities of the kind that are the focus of this study. Second, because of the study's focus on a highly multilingual region, the family members use diverse languages, and this inevitably results in a large number of features that need to be considered (Table 3). Third, the extent of the contribution of each feature identified by the model provides an explicit measurement of the impact of place-based features on primary language identification.

## Model Implementation

We implement the model with a number of critical parameters. These include the division of training set versus testing set, the number of decision trees, and the maximum number of features for a tree. First, for the division between the training and testing sets, we adopt the common 80 percent versus 20 percent rule (Breiman 2001; Gholamy, Kreinovich, and Kosheleva 2018). For both sets, data are stratified according to the number of speakers of the fifteen primary languages (Appendix B).

Second, for the number of decision trees, we select forty-five based on two rounds of experiments designed to maximize the prediction accuracy while minimizing underfitting and overfitting. The first round of experiments uses the number of trees ranging from 10 to 100, with an increment of 10. The minimum value of 10 is used to accommodate the small size of the data set, and the maximum value of 100 is used based on the typical choice discussed in the literature (Latief et al. 2019; Wibowo et al.

2020). The model performs best when the number of trees is forty and fifty. Subsequently, the second round of experiments focuses on a narrower range between forty and fifty trees with a refined increment of one. The best result is observed with a setting of forty-five trees.

For the third parameter, the maximum number of features, we set the number as eleven after experimenting with three options recommended in the literature (Kyriakides and Margaritis 2019). The first option uses the total number of features, which is 124 features in this case, as the maximum for a tree. The second option uses the logarithm base 2 of the total number of features for a tree, which is seven in this case. The last option uses the square root of the total number of features, which is eleven features in this case, and this option delivers the best results. The other two options either reduce the intended randomness of the ensemble design (the first option) or render a number too small to support the prediction (the second option). The best model performance during the experiments and selection process is confirmed using leave-one-out cross-validation.

## Model Evaluation

We assess prediction accuracy using the F1 score and a confusion matrix. The F1 score integrates precision and recall, expressed as the harmonic mean of the two (Powers 2011). The precision accounts for the number of correct predictions among all predictions, and the recall accounts for the number of observations correctly predicted among all observations. The F1 score ranges from 0 to 1, with 1 meaning a perfect prediction and 0 an ineffective prediction. In comparison to F1, the confusion matrix reports the overall accuracy, commission errors, and omission errors of prediction. The overall accuracy is expressed as the percentage of correctly predicted primary language classes over all observed language classes. The commission error is the percentage of observations committed to incorrect language classes. The omission error is the percentage of predictions omitted from correct language classes. Both errors range between 0 and 1, and a lower value indicates a better prediction. We use both the F1 score and confusion matrix as they evaluate the model performance from different angles.

To evaluate the impact of features on primary language identification, the model quantifies their contributions by the Gini importance index embedded in the random forest model. For a given feature, its contribution is expressed as the decrease in Gini impurity if the feature is removed from the model (Strobl, Malley, and Tutz 2009). The values of all feature contributions sum to 1 (100 percent), and the features are ranked according to their contribution values. In this study, given the total of124 features, the average value of feature contribution is 0.008, which serves as a relative reference point when evaluating the contribution of the 124 features.

To compare the results of the random forest model with a statistically oriented analytic, we use a multinomial logistic regression analytic to predict the primary languages. The regression evaluates the contribution of features through a statistical relationship between the features and the primary languages. The fifteen classes of primary languages are the dependent variable for the regression, and the 124 features serve as independent variables. The results of multinomial logistic regression are also assessed using the F1 score and confusion matrix.

# Results

## Prediction Accuracy of Primary Language

For our first research objective, predicting primary languages, the F1 score achieved by the random forest model is 0.894, and the overall accuracy is 88.9 percent. The confusion matrix is displayed in Table 4 and commission errors and omission errors are displayed in Table 5.

Results of the multinomial logistic regression model achieve an F1 score of 0.789 and an overall accuracy of 77.8 percent, both lower than those of the random forest model. The regression predicts most primary languages correctly yet yields twice as many misclassifications as the random forest model does (Tables 6 and 7). The large number of classes of dependent variables and the vast number of independent variables involved in this study are overwhelming in comparison to the number of observations. Regression analysis of this situation could produce low prediction accuracy (Augustin, Cummins, and French 2001; Couronné, Probst, and Boulesteix 2018) and is considered less preferable for this study. Because the random forest model

achieves better results than the logistic regression model, we focus on the results of the random forest model in the rest of the article.

The two types of place, the residence of multilingual individuals and the birthplace of their family members, seem to capture the impact of spatiality underlying primary language identification.

The misclassifications are mostly due to proximity effects. ## Place Matters

The top-ranked group in Table 8 (Group 1) contributes the most to primary language identification: paternal birthplace (contribution 0.123), multilingual individual's residence (contribution 0.091), maternal birthplace (contribution 0.082), and spousal birthplace (contribution 0.054). These are exclusively place-centric features and imply a profound impact of the two types of place on primary language, yet their impacts differ. Of the four places, we first discuss the residence of multilingual individuals (ranked second). We then discuss the paternal, maternal, and spousal birthplaces (ranked first, third, and fourth, respectively) together as they represent the impact of family members' birthplaces.

The spatial characteristics of this small-scale society influence the identification of primary language. As is predicated in local cultures, there are prevailing place-centric localist attitudes that tightly associate a village with a distinctive language. This motivates residents to identify the residence language as their primary language to integrate into the local community. Communal ritual events held in a village, which are central to the cultural and religious life of residents in the chiefdom polities, are exclusively performed in the residence language. The institutionalization of these languages directly or indirectly reinforces their use (Di Carlo 2018). Given these place-centric spatial-social circumstances, it is not surprising that the individual's residence language is often the primary language.

Of the three sets of family members, the father's birthplace plays the most critical role in primary language identification. The majority of the societies of Lower Fungom are patrilineal in terms of social and cultural heritage and hierarchy (Di Carlo 2018). Multilingual individuals might prefer or feel compelled to use their father's birthplace language at home and in the extended family. The importance of the mother's birthplace is prominent and ranked third in the top group. Similar to the father's birthplace language, the mother's birthplace language is frequently used at home. Multilingual individuals typically want to maintain social ties with their

mother's family. This is an incentive for them to acquire and use the mother's birthplace language as needed. Yet, given the patrilineal structure of societies in Lower Fungom, it is understandable that the mother's birthplace contributes less than the father's to primary language identification.

The spouse's birthplace, ranked the last in the top group, is found to have noticeable, but clearly lower, importance than the parents' birthplace for primary language identification. Due to the linguistic diversity of the region, the spouses' birthplace languages are often different from the residence language of the multilingual individuals. The spouse brings their birthplace language later into the multilingual individual's life, and the impact on the individual's primary language is not as clear as that of parental influence, except for the difference by gender.

Typically, women move to live in their husband's villages after marriage. That is, in addition to patrilineality, the region is also characterized by virilality. In this study, approximately half of the multilingual individuals are male, and the other half are female. If multilingual individuals are male, they might keep their primary language from before marriage and they might comprehend but not speak the primary language of a spouse (i.e., a wife). Alternatively, if multilingual individuals are female when they relocate to the residence of their spouse (the husband), they tend to accommodate the primary language of their spouse by learning it and using it daily (Esene Agwara 2020). It is understandable that the impact of spouses is identifiable, although not as strong as that of the parents.

Group 2 is the distant second in the ranking (Table 8) in terms of contribution values (0.029–0.008). Of the thirty-three features in this group, thirty-one are the birthplace languages of family members. The remaining two are gender features, one for multilingual individuals and the other for their spouses. The gender features represent the impact of the spouse's birthplace language. The Group 2 features explicitly highlight the prominent role of the birthplace of family members inidentifying primary languages via the form of birth-place languages.

Group 3 includes the remaining eighty-seven features (Table 8). Within the group, the majority of features are languages from other villages inside or outside Lower Fungom used by family members. They have a marginal impact on primary language identification due to their low presence in the daily lives of multilingual individuals.

These results substantiate the role of place in the identification of primary languages, showcasing the spatiality of primary language identification. The misclassified cases, despite being errors, reflect the influence of place in terms of spatial interactions between the villages insofar as some of them involve villages that are spatially proximate to each other and whose members, as a result, have relatively intense social interactions.

## Conclusions

This study aims at two objectives. First, we predict an individual's primary language in a small-scale multilingual environment. Second, we evaluate the impact of two types of place—residence of multilingual individuals and birthplace of their family members—on primary language identification. Both objectives are supported using the random forest model. We found that place-centric features, the residence of multilingual individuals and the birthplace of their family members, contribute the most to primary language identification. Birthplace languages of family members also show a discernible influence on primary languages.

The study establishes a baseline level of knowledge of primary language and is a critical step toward many more unexplored research issues. Research questions that arise from this work include understanding the role of place in maintaining the linguistic ecosystem of small-scale societies. For example, what spatial factors could predict other languages of identity as well and how spatial characteristics of specific language communities might be relevant to an individual's multilingual profile? Addressing these issues will likely provide insights into how to maintain the use of endangered languages associated with small-scale multilingualism.

The findings also substantiate the impact of place on multilingualism and contribute to current discussions regarding the connection between space and language. Presently, the GIScience community is actively discussing multiple conceptualizations of place, such as location (in Euclidean space), locale (with reference to an ad hoc origin), place identity, and sense of place (Adams 2017; Shaw and Sui 2020), as a continued exploration of the meaning of place following earlier discussion on the topic (Tuan 1977; Agnew 2011). These conceptualizations could

stimulate a broad range of explorations into the role of place in sociolinguistics and geography. ■
