
# Conservation or Decarbonization? Small Hydropower and State Logics of Green Development in China

Through an analysis of small hydropower (SHP) in China, I argue that logics of green development offer a framework for analyzing why, how, and to what intended effect the state pursues different green agendas over time and space. For decades, China's central and provincial governments framed SHP as a model of green development, but in 2016 they instituted SHP restrictions due to ecological impacts and electricity waste, a situation blamed on local officials haphazardly approving too many plants. This interpretation, however, ignores a major shift in state logic for promoting SHP, first for conservation-based development in rural areas and then for low-carbon development in urban areas. These logics—which I abbreviate as conservation and decarbonization—are based on different political-economic problems that green development is meant to solve for the state, different places targeted for intervention, and different distributions of benefits and costs. Using this framework, I argue that a shift to decarbonization in the mid-2000s incentivized cash-strapped local governments in rural western China to build as many SHP plants as possible to export electricity and build local industries, leading to a subsequent “bust.” I illustrate this trajectory using case studies of three prefectures in Yunnan province. This article thus enriches scholarship on state-nature relations by theorizing the role of the state in shaping dominant discourses and practices of green development across space and their uneven outcomes. Key Words: China, conservation, decarbonization, green development, renewable energy.

Con base en un análisis de hidroenergía pequeña (SHP) en China, sostengo que la lógica del desarrollo verde presenta un marco para analizar porqué, cómo y con qué efecto previsto persigue el estado diferentes agendas verdes a través del tiempo y el espacio. Durante décadas, los gobiernos central y provincial de China enmarcaron la SHP como un modelo de desarrollo verde, pero en 2016 ellos instituyeron restricciones a las SHP debido a impactos ecológicos y al desperdicio de electricidad, situación de la que se hizo responsables a funcionarios locales por aprobar a la topa tolondra demasiadas plantas. Sin embargo, esta interpretación ignora un cambio importante en la lógica del estado para promover las SHP, primero por el desarrollo basado en conservación en áreas rurales, y luego por desarrollo bajo en carbón en las áreas urbanas. Estas lógicas—que yo abrevio como conservación y descarbonización—se basan en diferentes problemas político-económicos que se supone han de ser solucionados por el desarrollo verde para el estado, diferentes lugares seleccionados para intervención y diferentes distribuciones de beneficios y costos. Usando este marco, sostengo que un cambio hacia la descarbonización a mediados de los años 2000 incentivó a los gobiernos locales de la parte rural de China occidental, cortos de dinero, a construir tantas plantas como fuese posible para exportar electricidad y construir industrias locales, lo cual terminó en un subsiguiente “fiasco”. Ilustro estas trayectorias usando estudios de caso de tres prefecturas en la provincia de Yunnan. De ese modo este artículo enriquece laerudición en las relaciones estado-naturaleza, teorizando el papel del estado en la configuración de los discursos y prácticas dominantes de desarrollo verde a través del espacio, y sus inequitativos resultados.

Long seen by China's leaders as developmentally backward and ecologically degraded, western China has undergone a dramatic economic and environmental transformation in the last two decades. Beginning with the Great Western Development Strategy in the early 2000s, to the current Belt and Road Initiative, the central government has sought to bring the western provinces up to the developmental standard of the industrialized east. This effort is spearheaded by state mining and energy investments (Tian 2004). It also entails subsidies for large-scale ecological restoration and forest protection initiatives to combat land degradation (Bennett 2008). A 2010 national policy to set aside “ecological function zones”—many of them in western China (China State Council 2016)—highlights the tension between state economic and environmental concerns. Indeed, China’s leaders see issues of political stability and gross domestic product (GDP) growth as predicated on an economically dynamic and ecologically sound western “frontier” (Yeh 2009).

Small hydropower (SHP) exemplifies the changes taking place in western China. In China, SHP is defined as <50 MW installed capacity, compared with the definition of <10 MW in many other countries (United Nations Industrial Development Organization and International Center on Small Hydro Power 2016). The first SHP plant in China began operation in 1911, and by the 1970s SHP generated electricity for millions of rural households (Kong et al. 2015). As of the end of 2015, more than 47,000 plants were in operation, with a combined installed capacity of 74.3 GW, nearly a quarter of China's total installed hydropower capacity (EPS China Data 2018). China's central and provincial governments have promoted SHP as a model of green development since the late 1990s, based on the argument that SHP replaces peasant firewood collection with electricity use, thus protecting forests and improving productivity through rural electrification (Cui and Qu 2008). Since the mid-2000s, authorities have also framed SHP as a renewable energy that contributes to national carbon emissions reductions. Indeed, before it was overtaken by wind energy in 2015, SHP was China's most widespread

renewable energy technology, and China's leaders still uphold SHP as a green example that other countries can learn from (Harlan 2017).

Beginning in 2016, however, government SHP policy experienced a complete reversal. In mid-2016, the governments of Yunnan and Sichuan provinces in western China—which are ranked first and second nationally in total installed SHP capacity—announced restrictions on SHP that effectively halted approvals for new plant construction. These provincial restrictions were followed by the central government's 13th Five Year Renewable Energy Development Plan (2016–2020) that reduced the target for new construction of small and medium installed hydropower capacity to a mere 5 GW (National Development and Reform Commission [NDRC] 2016b). Paradoxically, the reason given for these restrictions was to “protect the ecological environment” and to “promote regional sustainable development,” which are precisely the outcomes that SHP is supposed to deliver (People’s Government of Yunnan Province 2016). More specifically, state media outlets promoted the official line that private investors allied with local officials haphazardly constructed too many SHP plants in the 2000s and early 2010s, causing ecological impacts like soil erosion and streamflow reductions and eliminating the environmental and livelihood benefits that SHP is supposed to provide (Yu 2015; Guo 2016; Zhao 2016). In the central and provincial governments’ view, then, SHP failed as a green development scheme because investors and local officials placed profits and economic growth ahead of environmental protection, leading them to overdevelop SHP.

This article makes two related arguments. The first is that the “boom and bust” of SHP—its rapid expansion in the 2000s and early 2010s and subsequent restrictions—cannot simply be understood as a green development initiative that failed due to poor local implementation, as state-controlled media narratives suggest. Instead, the boom in SHP construction was a local reaction to a shift in central and provincial government policy that transformed SHP's role from one of conservation-baseddevelopment to low-carbon development. The linchpin of this policy change was the 2006 Renewable Energy Law, which forced plants to connect to the grid and mandated that utilities purchase SHP electricity. As a result, instead of developing SHP to meet household energy needs and conserve forests, private investors and local governments built facilities to sell electricity to the grid and to power local industries. In certain areas of Yunnan, local officials approved thousands of larger scale (15–50 MW) SHP plants as an industrial development strategy. This strategy generated local tax revenue but also caused local ecological problems and electricity wastage due to a lack of transmission infrastructure in western China. The central and provincial governments now want to halt SHP because it generates far too little usable renewable energy in relation to other potential sources, like large hydropower, solar, or wind. These restrictions, in turn, devalued SHP infrastructure and led to local economic “busts” in western China.

The second argument in this article is that this shift from conservation-based development to low-carbon development is key to analyzing questions of why, how, and to what intended effect the state pursues different green agendas. To answer these questions, I put forward the framework of logics of green development—defined as the political–economic rationale, locus, and intended beneficiaries of state approaches to balancing economic and environmental concerns, which vary across time and space. These logics are rooted in beliefs and assumptions about the cause(s) of socioenvironmental problems and the role of the state in solving them. Conservation-based development, hereafter conservation,¹ gained global prominence in the 1990s and is embodied in schemes like protected areas, integrated conservation and development, and payments for ecosystem services. Conservation is framed as a way to break the “downward spiral” of poverty and degradation by improving ecological conditions (e.g., soil health, forest cover, and wildlife diversity) and providing income generation opportunities (Duraiappah 1998). The broader state rationale for conservation, however, is often to extend territorial control and preserve resources that enable economic growth to continue. Although conservation might indirectly enable capital accumulation through enclosure, it is not itself a productive activity and is usually subsi-dized by the state or multilateral institutions.

Moreover, because resources are mainly located in rural, “undeveloped” regions—along with the poor who are perceived to be degrading them—the chief geographical focus of conservation is rural.

Low-carbon development, or decarbonization, emerged in the mid-2000s and is embodied in initiatives like clean tech, renewable energy, green buildings, and ecocities. Decarbonization is framed as a way to decouple economic growth from harmful emissions (both greenhouse gases and other pollutants) through industrial upgrading and technology, thus improving air quality and reducing catastrophic impacts of climate change (Teng and Jotzo 2014). The broader state rationale for decarbonization, however, is also to maintain national and international political legitimacy and grasp economic opportunities in globally booming low-carbon industries (Newell and Paterson 2010). Although decarbonization is often initially subsidized by the state, there is an expectation that low-carbon industries will become a major driver of capital accumulation (Gibbs and O'Neill 2017). Moreover, because economic growth is concentrated in urban, industrialized regions, the chief geographical focus of decarbonization is urban.² Decarbonization offers a more direct means for the state to facilitate accumulation than conservation and is oriented toward urban and national elites who hold political–economic power, so it often “wins out” at the expense of rural livelihoods and environmental protection. The privileging of decarbonization, in turn, induces new struggles over land and resources and generates “boom and bust” economic cycles in “undeveloped” regions that deepen rural–urban inequalities.

This framework, I suggest, helps fill a need in nature–society geography to theorize the role of the state in shaping dominant discourses and practices of green development across space and their uneven outcomes. Critical geographers tend to view green development as one way for capital to avert ecological crisis and sustain accumulation (Castree 2008; Ekers and Prudham 2017), with particular focus on the neoliberalization and financialization of conservation (Sullivan 2013) and carbon offsets (Richards and Lyons 2016). Yet although this work highlights the state as a regulator and facilitator of accumulation—including through territorialization (Scheba and Rakotonarivo 2016; Bluwstein and Lund 2018)—it overlooks why the state promotes and pursues particular green agendas and how their intendedoutcomes might differ. Literature on state-nature relations (Harris 2012; Loftus 2018) partly fills this gap by emphasizing the coconstitutive relationship between the biophysical world and the state, which undergoes “eco-state restructuring” (While, Jonas, and Gibbs 2010) as states prioritize some discourses and strategies over others. Although this scholarship theorizes why and how green agendas shift over time, it pays less attention to their shifts over space, particularly between rural and urban regions. This article brings these two literatures together in the context of China, with a state that actively promotes and pursues green development but remains peripheral to theorization in nature-society geography. Identifying and analyzing these state logics can help us better understand who wins and who loses in current forms of green development and the potential for equitable alternatives.

## The State of and in Green Development

Since the 1987 release of the World Commission on Environment and Development *Our Common Future* report (World Commission on Environment and Development 1987)—also known as the Brundtland Report—the idea of green development has been widely adopted by governments, multilateral institutions, and the public (Adams 2009).³ Although opinions vary as to how green development should be achieved—and whether major socio-political change is needed (Bina 2013; P. Ferguson 2015)—its most prominent metanarrative is that economic growth, environmental protection, and social equity can be realized simultaneously through market instruments, technological innovation, and some state regulation (Banerjee 2003).⁴ Over time, though, the main focus of green development discourse and practice has shifted alongside changing political-economic contexts and the emergence (and recognition) of particular socioenvironmental problems and solutions that are emphasized over others, which in turn shape whether or not green development is perceived to succeed or fail. This section traces this shift in green development agendas—from poverty and environmental degradation to the governance of climate change—and geographers’ critiques of these discourses and practices as vehicles for capital accumulation. It then turns to recent geographical work in nature-state relations to theorize the

role of the state in green development over time and space, setting the stage for the framework to follow.

## Shifting Green Agendas

Arguably the first major global issue framed as a green development challenge was the perceived linkage between poverty and environmental degradation, primarily in the rural Global South. This concern has origins in neo-Malthusian beliefs about population-induced resource exhaustion (Meadows, Meadows, and Behrens 1972), bolstered by the more recent view that degradation and scarcity occur because the poor are “unable or unwilling” to protect the environment (see Duraiappah [1998] for a review). In this interpretation, poverty and degradation reinforce each other in a “downward spiral” (Dasgupta 1995)—a view clearly stated in the Brundtland Report (World Commission on Environment and Development 1987, 6)—and reversing the spiral requires policies and programs that simultaneously lift incomes and encourage sound resource use. Since the 1980s, these have included integrated conservation and development programs (ICDPs; Marcus 2001), community-based natural resource management (CBNRM; Nelson and Agrawal 2008), and, most recently, payments for ecosystem services (PES) schemes that pay local people to protect resources (e.g., water or sloping land) for the benefit of downstream users, the largest program of which is in China (Bennett 2008). Although these initiatives are considered an improvement on “fortress conservation,” they are criticized in both conservation and development quarters for often failing to protect ecosystems and resulting in inequitable outcomes that do not benefit the poorest and most vulnerable households (Sunderland, Ehringhaus, and Campbell 2007; Nelson and Agrawal 2008; Gauvin et al. 2010).

Today, though, the focus of green development discourse and practice is the governance of climate change. The need to reduce greenhouse gas emissions was apparent before the 1992 United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change but rose to prominence in the mid-2000s in reaction to concerns over oil dependence and the coming climate crisis. In this context, the discourse of green development came to be synonymous with a green, low-carbon economy (Bina 2013; P. Ferguson 2015). In urban and industrialized regions—including urbanChina—climate change governance has taken the form of policy support, investments, and public-private partnerships for clean technologies and infrastructure (Chang and Sheppard 2013; Gibbs and O'Neill 2017). Market-based carbon trading has also gained popularity and, indeed, has been linked to solving poverty and degradation through “offset” schemes like the Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) that are designed to mitigate emissions through small and medium renewable energy and carbon forestry (Sutter and Parreño 2007).⁵ Offset schemes are criticized, however, for financing projects that would have been built anyway, providing no net emissions reductions, and offering benefits (like electricity or payments for protecting forests) that local communities are unable to access, afford, or use (Boyd 2009; Blom, Sunderland, and Murdiyarso 2010).

## Critical Perspectives on Green Development

Critical geographers and social scientists, meanwhile, look beyond analyses of “success” and “failure” to examine what green development discourses and practices do for those in power. Scholars argue that the discourse of poverty and degradation blames environmental problems on poor, “backward” peasants and a lack of property rights—a neocolonialist framing that legitimizes certain practices (Banerjee 2003). Critics of the dominant discourse of climate change governance maintain that it privileges those who stand to reap the most benefits from a green, low-carbon economy without changing the underlying structure (P. Ferguson 2015). Scholars also point to the growing role of market mechanisms and finance in both contexts—such as in PES, renewable energy, and the CDM—as evidence of how resources are “neoliberalized” and enrolled in circuits of capital (Igoe and Brockington 2007; Corson 2011). Indeed, for geographers, green development constitutes one kind of “socio-ecological fix” for capital to displace, defer, or normalize ecological contradictions inherent to capitalism (Castree and Christophers 2015; Ekers and Prudham 2017). From a critical perspective, then, green development discourse and practice is yet another strategy for accumulation—a lens through which we must view claims of success and failure.

A large body of empirical work analyzes how capitalist actors further their aims through green development programs and the consequences for

communities and environments. In rural contexts, studies detail numerous incidents of “green grabbing” in which local people lose access to resources that they are framed as having degraded (Benjaminsen and Bryceson 2012; German, Unks, and King 2017). In urban contexts, a growing literature documents how privately funded ecocities exclude lower income and minority residents (Chen 2013; Caprotti 2014). Research on carbon forestry and CDM renewable energy also highlights similar dynamics at work (Cavanagh and Benjaminsen 2014; Lyons and Westoby 2014). Nonetheless, scholars note that using green development as an accumulation strategy—whether by conservation (Büscher and Fletcher 2015) or decarbonization (Bumpus and Liverman 2008)—faces resistance from social movements and from the materiality of resources themselves (McAfee and Shapiro 2010; Bigger and Dempsey 2018). In this sense, then, green development that “fails” (or, in Dempsey and Suarez’s [2016] term, is “arrested”) is that which does not fulfill the promise of capitalist profits and accumulation.

Where is the state in these analyses of green development? Though not necessarily at the forefront, a close reading of this work reveals the state to be a key regulator and facilitator of green development interventions, with benefits accruing to state actors through rents and fiscal transfers. Moreover, empirical studies detail how states use protected areas and schemes like ICDPs to territorialize land and resources that might not have formerly been under their control (Véron and Fehr 2011), which is also the case with utility-scale renewable energy (Dunlap 2018) and carbon offsets (Scheba and Rakotonarivo 2016). Yet though this literature highlights how the state benefits from green development, it tends to focus on the growing influence of capitalist actors rather than the state itself (Robbins 2008). Moreover, in treating the state’s role in solving poverty/degradation and climate change governance in similar ways, this work overlooks the differences in why the state promotes and pursues them. Indeed, despite evidence that state capacity is being challenged under globalization (Eakin and Lemos 2006), the state is still central to shaping green development discourses, practices, and outcomes in many parts of the world, thus helping determine whether or not particular interventions (like SHP) succeed or fail. Theorizing the state’s role in doing so requires engagement with the literature on the “nature” of the state and its environmental strategies.## Nature-State Relations and Green Development

At the foundation of geographical work on state-nature relations—and to the framework in this article—is that the state is not “monolithic, coherent, or temporally and spatially stable” (Robbins 2008, 205). The state apparatus is comprised of sometimes aligned, sometimes competing groups and hierarchies, both within and between central and local bureaucracies (Sharma and Gupta 2006). It is porous to, but not completely controlled by, external influence from multinational institutions, finance capital, and civil society (Goldman 2001; Eakin and Lemos 2006; Robbins 2008). Indeed, for Jessop (1990), the state is better understood as a social relation that reflects the balance of power between competing interests across society, which shift over time and space. Nonetheless, Jessop (1990) argued that states are fundamentally biased toward some interests and strategies over others, such that power is always disproportionally distributed across factions.

Recent scholarship pushes this theorization forward by analyzing the state in relation to nature—a socionatural relation—as a way to understand both its composition and its environmental strategies (for a comprehensive review, see Loftus 2018). For Harris (2012), building on Mitchell (1991), the state as a discrete object (an “effect”) is continually produced and reified through discourses and practices of environmental transformation. Through these discourses and practices, moreover, the state governs nature and maintains and extends its authority (Beer 2017) in ways that facilitate territorialization and accumulation (Whitehead, Jones, and Jones 2007; Parenti 2015).

Thus, we can understand discourses and practices of green development as coconstitutive of the structure and power of the state, which shift over time and space.

While, Jonas, and Gibbs (2010), implicitly drawing on work on the environmental state (Goldman 2001), proposed an ecostate restructuring framework to analyze temporal shifts in state discourses and practices. They defined ecostate restructuring as the “reorganization of state powers, capacities, regulations, and territorial structures around institutional pathways and strategic projects, which are ... viewed as less environmentally damaging than previous trajectories” (While, Jonas, and Gibbs 2010, 80). This restructuring, they argued, has occurred in “waves” in capitalist states in the Global North: from pollution control in the mid-1960s to late

1980s, to sustainable development or market environmentalism from the mid-1980s to mid-2000s, to the “harder-edged” approach of carbon control from the mid-2000s onward. Although each wave of restructuring entails new state strategies, it does not necessarily replace the former wave but “is layered on to it as part of the accretion of functions within the state” (While, Jonas, and Gibbs 2010, 82). As a result, they argued, environmental policies in any given spatial and temporal context may align, overlap, or come into conflict with each other.

Where ecostate restructuring stops short, however—and what logics of green development seeks to analyze—is why, how, and to what intended effect the state’s green agendas change over time and space and what their (often conflicting) outcomes are. This gap is due, in part, to the focus of ecostate restructuring (and most state-nature relations literature) on industrialized capitalist states and urban and regional development. In this work, rural and/or industrializing regions are implicitly positioned as places that experience uneven outcomes of shifting state green agendas, rather than places that the state explicitly targets for socioenvironmental improvement. Understanding shifts between, and uneven results of, state green agendas over these spaces is key to theorizing the role of the state in shaping green development as a national and global phenomenon. This is the work that logics of green development aims to do.

## Logics of Green Development

This section advances the state logics of green development framework. I use the term *logic* because it captures not only the rationale, locus, and intended beneficiaries of state approaches to balancing environment and development concerns but also the ideologies and beliefs that underlie them. These beliefs concern the perceived cause(s) of socio-environmental problems that are identified as important and whether and how they should be solved—what Banerjee (2003), in a critique of sustainable development discourse, referred to as a Foucauldian example of a “regime of truth.” Indeed, critical development scholars have long shown how powerful actors produce hegemonic knowledge as a mechanism of political-economic control (Escobar 1995; Yeh 2009). Logics of green development train this critical approach on the state apparatus, and—following J. Ferguson (1994)—asks what discourses,practices, and their underlying beliefs do for the state over time and space, even if they are perceived (like SHP) to have failed.

Let me describe each logic in turn, which are displayed on a spectrum in Figure 1. The first, conservation-based development, is grounded in the belief that environmental degradation is a consequence of a lack of development—which refers both to poverty and to the absence of land tenure and resource use regulations. The rationale the state gives for promoting conservation is to simultaneously protect and/or rehabilitate the environment (through soil loss prevention, increased forest cover, grassland health, and/or wildlife diversity) and improve livelihoods, which we see in the stated aims of solutions like ICDPs and PES. As critical studies of these programs have shown, states also use conservation as justification for increasing control over land, which restricts access for (often historically marginalized) groups who depend on resources for their livelihoods (Véron and Fehr 2011). Thus, although conservation often improves environmental protection and benefits some rural residents—just as SHP provided electricity for villages in China—it must also be seen as a territorialization strategy that extends the state’s legitimacy and enables access to resources for continued economic growth. States might also seek to facilitate accumulation through conservation or restoration, such as through conservation finance, but

these activities resemble rent-seeking more than productive activity (Andreucci et al. 2017). As Kelly (2011) argued, conservation is better viewed as an act of primitive accumulation, which does not directly produce profits but can indirectly force users off the land and into new labor arrangements.

A logic of low-carbon development, in contrast, is based on the view that environmental harm and climate change are the consequences of the wrong kind of development, understood as unbridled industrial emissions and fossil fuel dependence. The rationale the state gives for promoting decarbonization is to simultaneously reduce emissions and drive economic growth, which we can see in the stated aims of initiatives like renewable energy subsidies, green buildings, and ecocities. Yet, as critical studies of climate change governance have shown, states also use decarbonization as justification for funneling resources and capital into new infrastructure projects, especially those that are predicted to generate economic returns in the global green economy (Newell and Paterson 2010). Thus, although decarbonization offers short-term benefits for some (mainly urban) residents through improved air quality, it must also be seen as an accumulation strategy that maintains state legitimacy by combining environmental protection with economic growth. Moreover, because low-carbon infrastructure facilitates or generates commodity production—such as electricity from renewable energy

I emphasize three points regarding the framework. The first is that although conservation-based development and low-carbon development describe dominant state logics—or ideal types—the specific discourses and practices that the state employs will often differ between contexts. Bina (2013), for example, identified multiple discourses of green, low-carbon growth present in official United Nations documents, from business-as-usual capitalism to more efficient growth through technology (what P. Ferguson [2015] called “green growth” and the “green economy”). Similarly, some discourses of poverty and degradation emphasize that peasants are poor resource managers in general (see Leach and Fairhead 2000), whereas others stress the socioenvironmental and institutional conditions that pressure peasants to degrade the land (see Duraiappah 1998). Green policies and practices, too, can range from more market-oriented PES and carbon trading to “harder-edged” (While, Jonas, and Gibbs 2010, 77) taxes and regulations. These discourses and practices could be present at the same time, within the same state apparatus. The point of logics of green development is not to downplay this variety but to recognize shared ideological foundations, justifications, and intended results of green agendas between these contexts.

The second point is that, although logics tend to be expressed in discourse and practice emanating from higher levels of government, they are subject to translation, negotiation, and contestation by local governments. Urban municipalities, for example, promote “sustainable city” strategies that can travel beyond the scale or boundaries of urban governance (see Bulkeley and Betsill 2005). Rural governments pursue utility-scale solar and wind generation to reverse the fortunes of the local economy (Murphy and Smith 2013). Local governments in more remote regions might also resist conservation-based development in favor of extractive industries (Zinda 2014). Logics of green development are thus discursive and material manifestations of often aligned, at times conflicting priorities of more and less powerful levels of government—some priorities of which might not be “green” at all.

Third, conservation-based development and low-carbon development are not necessarily discrete in

practice; hence the gray area in Figure 1. Rather, as While, Jonas, and Gibbs (2010) argued in regard to “waves,” logics can be layered onto each other in ways that align, overlap, and conflict. CDM projects, for example, profess to deliver both conservation and decarbonization (Canadell and Raupach 2008); ecocities claim to do the same (Chang and Sheppard 2013). Yet examples also abound of conflicts between conservation and decarbonization, such as local impacts of large-scale renewable energy (Mulvaney 2019), biofuel plantations (Fletcher et al. 2011), and extraction of rare earth metals for clean technologies (Klinger 2018). Indeed, even when conservation and decarbonization are supposed to align, the reality is that one is privileged over the other, such as by planting monocultures for carbon sequestration instead of preserving forest biodiversity (Putz and Redford 2009). Logics of green development bring these overlaps and conflicts to the fore.

Why do logics of green development matter? They matter, I argue, because they highlight the different functions that conservation and decarbonization perform for the state and the rural-urban inequalities that often result. Indeed, low-carbon development has the effect of plugging rural areas into broader geographies of commodification and extraction for the national and global economy—from renewable electricity, to minerals, to conventional steel and concrete, to labor power. As a result, decarbonization drives new rounds of rural dispossession and subjects rural economies to booms and busts linked to commodity price and subsidy fluctuations. Moreover, privileging low-carbon development can have the perverse effect of harming local ecologies and reducing biodiversity (Thomas et al. 2013). Logics, then, help us make sense of the distribution of socioenvironmental costs and benefits of green agendas and how this distribution is shaped by the state, a task I now turn to in the case of SHP in China.

In each prefecture, I interviewed the director or deputy director of four bureaus associated with SHP policy and management: Water Resources, Environmental Protection, Development and Reform Commission, and YPG. I then chose one county in each prefecture and interviewed officials in these same bureaus in the county government. In total, I interviewed twenty-two officials (I was unable to visit two bureaus in Yuxi). Following these interviews, I visited approximately thirty SHP plants across the three prefectures and interviewed the on-site manager. Interviews asked about central and provincial government policies (including subsidies); processes for siting, financing, and approving SHP plants; SHP operational requirements and challenges; and the environmental and social impacts of SHP. Analysis focused on similarities and differences between responses of government officials and SHP managers and between respondents in different prefectures.

Relying on interviews is always a risky approach to data collection, because respondents might emphasize some issues over others, withhold information entirely, or be untruthful, particularly in the case of interviewing Chinese officials (Heimer and Thøgersen 2006). Further complicating matters is that hydropower is considered a politically sensitive subject in China, especially for foreign researchers (Magee 2006). To facilitate interviews, then, I

introduced my research as concerned with rural renewable energy, which holds a positive connotation in China. Once I built rapport in the course of the interview, officials were generally more willing to offer opinions (often critical) on SHP policy and management. These interview data were then used to inform policy analysis of central and provincial government regulations, reports, and speeches related to SHP.

## SHP for Conservation-Based Development

The next three sections analyze changes to China's SHP policy since the late 1970s and their implementation in Yunnan. I identify the broader state logics of green development that shape the primary function of SHP at different times and places. I show how these logics emerge out of central and provincial government recognition of the need for green development—partly in response to global trends—but also how they are translated, adopted, and (at times) contested by the local governments in Yunnan for their own purposes. As will become clear, the state's discourse and practice of using SHP to drive green development—first for forest protection, then as a renewable energy—is grounded in logics of conservation-based development and low-carbon development.

When the People's Republic of China was established in 1949, only 3.6 MW of SHP was installed in the entire country. Plants were mainly composed of very small (typically <50 kW) wood or wood-iron turbines built out of spare agricultural parts and placed in a stream. During this period, and until the 1970s, the main purpose of SHP plants was to generate electricity for household lighting and agriculturalgoods processing. In some areas, plants were coupled with water management infrastructure to provide drainage and irrigation functions. Although SHP was promoted by the central and provincial governments, most SHP plants were managed by local governments, which enabled plants to produce electricity directly for households and incentivized local officials to reinvest earnings in new projects (Peng and Pan 2006). By the end of the 1970s, installed capacity reached over 6,000 MW, providing electricity for more than 150 million people (C. Cheng et al. 2015). These SHP plants were installed in both eastern and western China.

Beginning in the late 1970s, the beginning of China's reform period, central government policy began to promote SHP construction for rural electrification in rural, mountainous regions that were not yet connected to the grid. While investment in eastern China continued, more plants were constructed in the western provinces, including Yunnan. In 1982, the State Council allocated ¥100 million in yearly subsidies for 109 rural electrification counties, mainly in areas suitable for SHP. These subsidies were later extended to additional counties in 1990 and 1995 and matched with provincial rural electrification funds (Hangzhou Regional Center 2009). The case study prefectures of Nujiang, Wenshan, and Yuxi included counties that were part of this program. According to yearly reports from the Yunnan Bureau of Water Resources and Development and Reform Commission, these funds were used primarily for rural grid and SHP plant construction. Some local governments in China also experimented during this period with a shareholding structure for SHP plants, which allowed local people to purchase shares and earn revenue (although shares were mainly held by cadres, local leaders, or both). At this point, SHP was not yet considered an environmental protection technology, because the need for green development had only recently entered the national discourse.

By the mid-1990s, however, China's leaders began to express openly the need to reconcile economic growth and the environment—outlined in the government's Agenda 21 blueprint (China State Council 1994)—and instituted policies targeting poverty and degradation in western China in the 9th (1996–2000) and 10th (2001–2005) Five-Year Plans (NDRC 1996, 2001). SHP was included in the 10th Five-Year Plan alongside the Sloping Land

Conversion Program, National Forest Protection Program, and other subsidized initiatives targeting poor mountainous areas. SHP was considered a supplement to these larger programs because it could help replace peasant firewood collection with stable electricity, thus protecting forests and preventing soil erosion in the upper watersheds of major rivers. This approach was solidified in a 2002 central government program called SHP Replace Fuelwood that subsidized electricity prices for select SHP stations by one-half, a commitment of ¥127 billion (~US$19 billion) over five years (H. Cheng and Li 2003). SHP construction grew rapidly in the lead up to and after this program was announced, totaling 23.5 GW at the end of 2000. Local governments in Yunnan were keen to promote and pursue this program because it guaranteed fiscal transfers for SHP (e.g., Yuxi's Xinping county issued a glossy report on its marquis project; see Xinping County People's Government 2011). Through this program, then, we see a major shift in state logic (and associated policies) for SHP: from a concern primarily with poverty alleviation alone to one of conservation-based development.

What are the outcomes of using SHP for conservation? Tang et al. (2012) calculated that SHP Replace Fuelwood conserved 340 million hectares of forest in western China, a number that must be viewed with skepticism, due a history of unreliable statistical data in China, but that nonetheless points to the significance of the program. Just as important, however, was the role of SHP and other conservation schemes in extending state control over land and resources—what Yeh (2009), following Vandergeest and Peluso (1995), called “internal territorialization.” Indeed, Yeh (2009) and Zinda (2014) showed that forest protection, grassland conservation, and national park-making initiatives had the effect of separating resource-dependent communities from environments the state deemed to be “degraded,” and communities were sometimes resettled in villages or cities where they were more “legible” to the state. The SHP Replace Fuelwood program was smaller in scope than these other initiatives but was part of efforts to resettle mountainous (and fuelwood-dependent) peoples into areas better connected to the grid and the state bureaucracy. Its outcomes, then, were an increase in forest area under protection and a corresponding expansion of state power in rural regions like those in Yunnan.## SHP for Low-Carbon Development

In the early to mid-2000s, however, China's SHP policies began to change. The country was experiencing energy shortages due to rapid economic development, which the central government sought to rectify by exploiting new energy resources, particularly hydropower. Some of this need was fulfilled through state investment in large dam cascades with dedicated transmission lines to the Pearl River Delta in Guangdong (Magee 2006). In 2002, however, the central government announced a complete restructuring of the electricity sector along market principles. This restructuring had three main elements (Wang, Tseng, and Zheng 2015). First, power generation was separated from transmission, with state-owned generation enterprises encouraged to adopt commercial behavior and compete against each other. Second, independent grids were centralized under two grid state-owned enterprises: the State Grid Corporation of China, which manages the power grid in most of the country, and the China Southern Power Grid (CSPG), which includes Yunnan (the YPG is a subsidiary of the CSPG). Existing power supply bureaus at the provincial, prefecture, and county levels were absorbed into their respective grid enterprises and managed as quasi-commercial entities. Third, all existing rural, county, and prefectural grids were forced to connect to provincial and national grids, so that electricity could be more freely transferred between different administrative territories. This latter reform includes a policy of “forced connection” of power plants to the grid, implying that SHP plants would no longer operate independently for a single power supply area.

The release of the 11th Five-Year Plan (2006–2010) at the end of 2005 introduced what was perhaps the defining change to SHP policy: the new nationwide push to develop renewable energy. Growing numbers of protests against environmental pollution, along with international climate negotiations and economic opportunities, convinced many in the government of the necessity of establishing renewable energy targets in the 11th Five-Year Plan (and subsequent plans). In 2006, the government also released the Renewable Energy Law, which stipulated that grid operators must purchase electricity from renewable energy plants. A 2007 amendment titled “Administrative Provisions on Renewable Energy Power Generation” further mandated that grid operators construct ancillary grid access systems

for renewable projects, including SHP (Zhang, Andrews-Speed, and Ji 2014). From this period onward, SHP developers could apply for discounted bank loans earmarked for renewable energy and expect the local grid to construct substations and transmission lines to areas with high hydropower potential. Together with energy sector reform, these new renewable energy policies capped a gradual shift in the function of SHP that had begun in the early 2000s. SHP was no longer solely used for poverty alleviation and forest protection, dependent on central and provincial government subsidies. It had become its own “low-carbon industry” (Harlan 2018) and one that made up 93 percent of all renewable energy installed capacity nationwide by the end of 2006 (excluding large hydropower).⁷ Indeed, from the mid-2000s onward, even SHP plants that were not part of the SHP Replace Fuelwood program were considered “green” because of their role in national carbon mitigation.

In Yunnan, the provincial government was quick to react to energy sector reforms, calling for rapid growth of SHP in the 2003 document “Decision to Accelerate the Development of Medium and Small Hydropower” (People’s Government of Yunnan Province 2003). The Yunnan government also sought to attract domestic and foreign investors to develop SHP in Yunnan. Investors from eastern China traveled to Yunnan province at the behest of local governments to construct new SHP plants. From 2003 onward, Yuxi prefecture and its county-level governments offered tax incentives to SHP investors and quickly moved projects through the approval phase. Wenshan and Nujiang prefectures followed suit. Due to some local autonomy in grid management in Yunnan, local governments could adjust feed-in tariff rates to attract investors. Investors themselves saw tremendous profit potential in Yunnan’s undeveloped SHP resources—one investor stated that he would partner with others to construct plants over an entire cascade. Because of this new investment, Yunnan’s installed SHP capacity boomed, growing from 2.5 GW to 8.5 GW between 2000 and 2010—a more than threefold increase in ten years (EPS China Data 2018).

Local governments in Yunnan had two main reasons for attracting SHP investment and approving new construction. First, all electricity generated by SHP is taxable, and the majority (>50 percent) of tax revenues accrue to the prefecture or countygovernments. Because SHP plants during this period were financed by private investors, local governments did not need to use their own funds—they only needed to facilitate the approvals and loan process (if investors applied for loans at a local bank branch). Moreover, energy sector reforms and the refurbishment of rural grids provided the necessary infrastructure for transmitting electricity from remote prefectures and counties to the national grid, which was forced to purchase SHP electricity. SHP thus presented a potential long-term tax revenue stream for local officials. Second, SHP offered a source of cheap electricity for local industrial development. In Yunnan, industrial development took the form of mining and mineral processing facilities. Extractive industries are highly energy intensive and are often located near SHP resources, which reduces transmission losses. The central government promoted SHP as an energy source for mining in the 1980s, and the Yunnan government revived this policy in 2002, calling for “integrated SHP and mining operations” (Qi 2003). SHP promised a path toward an industrial economy for Yunnan’s rural regions.

Local officials in Yuxi prefecture began to rapidly approve SHP plants following the 2003 Yunnan government announcement. The same year, the large provincial state-owned mining enterprise Kunming Steel began operations in the Dahongshan mine in Yuxi's Xinping County, located adjacent to several streams suitable for hydropower generation. This mine is almost exclusively supplied by electricity from SHP. Although installed SHP capacity grew rapidly across the prefecture in the mid- to late 2000s the Yuxi government was not completely dependent on SHP-led industrialization, because the prefecture already boasted well-developed tobacco processing and manufacturing operations. Wenshan and Nujiang prefectures, by contrast, had few industries and no large hydropower plants in the mid-2000s and saw in SHP the opportunity to build a rural industrial economy. From 2005 to 2010, both prefecture governments rapidly approved new SHP plants and small-scale mining and mineral processing facilities, primarily for silicon, zinc, copper, and tin. Although I was unable to obtain specific tax revenue figures, local officials mentioned that the contribution of SHP to the local economy was extremely high. Coupled with extractive industries using SHP electricity, the prefectures of Wenshan and

Nujiang—and many other regions of western China—transformed their economies from small-scale agriculture to net energy and mineral exporters. By 2010, installed SHP capacity in all case study prefectures had more than tripled.

## The SHP Bust

Even as the central government continued to promote SHP in the 12th Five-Year Plan (2011–2015), other policy changes were beginning to limit further SHP expansion in western China. Chief among these were new state subsidies for solar photovoltaic (PV) and wind technologies, which were considered too expensive for domestic use in the 2000s. In 2010, the central government unveiled the “Decision of the State Council on Accelerating the Fostering and Development of Strategic Emerging Industries” that identified “new energy” as an emerging economic sector (Zhang, Andrews-Speed, and Ji 2014). The list of “new energy” technologies in the document, however, did not include SHP. Similarly, in 2012, the central government released the 12th Five-Year Plan for Renewable Energy Development that specified favorable policies and subsidies for solar PV and wind but not SHP. Contradictory reports emerged about the state of national SHP resources: Whereas the Ministry of Water Resources released findings in 2012 that only 33 percent of SHP potential had been exploited, hydropower experts in Beijing suggested privately that few economically feasible sites remained, due to their remoteness and declining profitability. SHP was slowly losing its appeal as China’s de facto renewable energy of choice.

In Yunnan, the gradual reversal of official support for SHP was being felt in other ways. In 2010, the provincial government announced that all “illegal” plants that had not passed a proper environmental impact assessment (EIA) were to be shut down, resulting in a loss of revenue for some local governments (including Nujiang and Wenshan). Local environmental protection bureaus were given more power to hold up projects that did not pass their EIAs. A 2014 YPG regulation also mandated that new SHP applications must show the ability to operate more than 4,500 hours per year or they would not be approved. Most significant, however, was the Yunnan government’s decision to set feed-in tariff prices at the provincial level, giving prefecture governmentslittle wiggle room to entice new SHP investors with high electricity rates. By 2014, the provincial SHP feed-in tariff was set at ¥0.175/kWh during the wet season and ¥0.25/kWh during the dry season—the lowest price of any province in China. As a result, plants that might have earned back their initial investment in three to five years in the mid-2000s were suddenly faced with a ten- to fifteen-year time frame. Although suitable SHP sites still existed, they were no longer profitable to exploit without strong local government support and the promise of a high rate of return. Local governments lobbied the provincial government for higher feed-in tariffs but to no avail. Investment began to dry up.

In 2016, the central government's gradual reversal of support for SHP was finally written into national and provincial policy. The first step occurred in March, when the Yunnan government announced that no further SHP plants would be approved in the upper Nu River basin, most of which is in Nujiang prefecture (Li 2016). The reason given in the announcement was that SHP had been "overdeveloped," a common phrase used in several media articles that led up to the decision. The consequence of overdevelopment, the government said, was the loss of soil and vegetation on steep hillsides, the same problem that the SHP Replace Fuelwood program was meant to solve. Then, in July, the Yunnan and Sichuan governments simultaneously announced that all future SHP plants would need to be approved at the provincial level. This policy, "Some Suggestions for Strengthening the Use and Management of Small and Medium Hydropower," also set new standards for minimum plant size, operating time, generation efficiency, and postconstruction revegetation that were widely seen by investors and local governments as precluding any new SHP development (People's Government of Yunnan Province 2016). Finally, in the 13th Five-Year Plan for Energy released at the beginning of 2017, SHP was given only slightly higher targets for new installations, all to be met by plants already funded and in the planning or construction stages (NDRC 2016b). The SHP boom in western China was over.

What caused this reversal in central and provincial government SHP policy? The claim that all of the “good” SHP resources had been exploited, repeated constantly in reports and my own interviews, had relatively little influence over this decision, because the SHP industry was and is still willing to invest in new

plants. Indeed, the existence of “good” resources is a function not just of physical geography but also of existing infrastructure (especially transformers), the feed-in tariff price, and political will. Similarly, the Yunnan government’s assertion that SHP had caused soil erosion, loss of riparian and aquatic biodiversity, and local forest cover—although true in many cases (see Hennig and Harlan 2018)—was an odd position to take, given that it continued to promote large hydropower, mineral extraction, and processing. At the national scale, the 13th Five-Year Plan announced 60 GW in new large hydropower construction and still listed the Nu River basin as a potential “hydropower base” for a thirteen-dam cascade, even as it cut support for new SHP expansion (NDRC 2016a, 11). Finally, although my own research in Yuxi found that SHP often reduces farmers’ irrigation water access, this issue only concerned local water and agricultural officials, not provincial leaders. Even up to this period, SHP was still officially considered a pro-poor, green technology that improved farmers’ livelihoods, and no mention was made of its negative social impacts in the decisions to restrict further construction.

Rather, the main reason for restricting SHP was that the central and provincial governments considered it a flawed renewable energy technology. This assessment was made for two main reasons, according to my interviews. First, due to Yunnan's mountainous terrain and limited transmission connectivity, much of the electricity generated by SHP during the wet season could not be "exported" outside of the county or prefectural grid. This was especially the case in Wenshan, Nujiang, and other prefectures with semiautonomous grids and a single high-kilovolt transmission line. Moreover, 90 percent of SHP plants in China are diversion-type run-of-river, without a reservoir at the head of the cascade. This makes SHP power generation unstable over daily and seasonal time frames, leading one former YPG employee to call it "garbage electricity." Second, solar PV and wind were (until 2018) highly subsidized by the central government and their technology has improved over the past decade. China's leaders see these "new energy" technologies as a key to China's competitiveness in the global green economy. SHP, in contrast, is viewed as an old and "backward" technology that is not based in scientific management. Moreover, the industries that SHP supports—mainly small-scale mining and mineral processing—are now being closed or consolidated into larger facilities that the state believes are more efficient (Woodworth2017). SHP thus has no place in a “modern” decarbon-
izing China.

Conclusion

States and institutions worldwide are pursuing green development, but how it is interpreted and implemented shifts over time and space. This article offers a framework for analyzing these shifts and their associated logics using the case of SHP. I identify two logics of green development through which China's leaders view western China: as a poverty-stricken rural region in need of conservation-based development and as a source of renewable energy to be used for low-carbon development. For the central and provincial governments, SHP was originally designed to produce electricity for poor rural households, reducing their need to collect firewood and making them legible to a territorializing state. From the mid-2000s, SHP was also deployed as a renewable energy, which offered a pathway for local industrial development and capital accumulation. Although it generated significant tax and industrial revenue for local governments in Yunnan—many of which built their economies on SHP—it has since been demoted as a model of green development by the central and provincial governments. SHP is now problematic because it produces unstable electricity prone to grid congestion instead of a steady energy supply, which impedes profitability and capital accumulation. Judged against a low-carbon development logic, then, SHP in western China fails.

This analysis offers theoretical and empirical
insights for studies of state-nature relations and
green development. First, this article demonstrates
that the state is central to how green development is
conceptualized in discourse and implemented in
practice and that these shift over space as certain
agendas are prioritized over others. I use the frame-
work of state *logics* of green development, rather
than simply “modes” or “strategies,” to capture domi-
nant beliefs and assumptions about why, how, and to
what intended effect the state pursues environmental
governance at all. This framework, I suggest, helps
to advance theorization of nature-state relations in
geography by highlighting shifts in state discourse
and practice across space. Moreover, by showing
how logics target different environments and popula-
tions—and how logics are translated, negotiated, and
contested locally—this article helps link the

intentions of state green development agendas to
their uneven spatial outcomes. This article trains its
analysis on China, a strong state that actively pur-
sues green development, but its framework can also
be applied to other contexts in which states have
more or less power vis-à-vis other societal interests.

Second, this article reveals why and how conser-
vation-based development and low-carbon develop-
ment—logics and associated policies that would
appear to be complementary—will often conflict.
These conflicts are not simply (or at all) due to fail-
ures in implementation but of entirely different state
logics for what green development is supposed to
achieve, and how, and for what intended result.
Conservation, I argue, is used to extend state territo-
rial control in rural regions, a process I described
in the case of SHP and forest protection.
Decarbonization, in contrast, is mainly used to
restructure the economy, a process that (like SHP)
requires new resources and drives new industrializa-
tion processes as rural regions are plugged into
broader geographies. As this article reveals, a logic
of decarbonization offers more opportunities for capi-
tal accumulation than conservation and is more
directed toward urban and national elites, making it
more attractive to the state and capital. Such accu-
mulation might be rapid for a period of time but can
drop precipitously if (or when) commodity prices
fall, state subsidies are reduced, or new technologies
come to market. These rural-urban dynamics and
outcomes of conservation and decarbonization are
essential to understanding the uneven spatial impli-
cations of state-led green development.
