# Heat Waves and Road Traffic Collisions in Alabama, United States

Connor Y. H. Wu


Department of Geospatial Informatics, College of Arts and Sciences, Troy University, USA

The effects of heat waves on traffic collisions require investigation to improve traffic safety during extreme heat events. A time-stratified case-crossover design was used to examine associations between heat waves and traffic collisions in Alabama between May and September from 2009 to 2018. We derived a heat wave index, defined as the daily mean temperature greater than the 95th percentile for two or more consecutive days, by meteorological data from Phase 2 of the North American Land Data Assimilation System. We obtained traffic collision records from the Alabama Department of Transportation. A nonsignificant and negative association between traffic collisions and heat waves was noted, with a 1.4 percent decrease (95 percent confidence interval [CI] [-3.1 percent, 0.4 percent]) in traffic collisions on heat wave days compared to non-heat wave days. Similar results were found when the analysis was stratified by driver-related factors (i.e., gender, age, race, employment status, and driver residence distances), vehicle-related factors (i.e., vehicle usage), and collision-related factors (i.e., rural or urban roads, speed limits, and intersections). A significant and positive association was observed on heat wave days without precipitation, however (23.5 percent increase; 95 percent CI [7.3 percent, 42.3 percent]). In conclusion, traffic collisions were not associated with heat waves in many collision-related conditions in Alabama. Key Words: Alabama heat, case-crossover design, climate change, heat waves, traffic collisions.

Climate change is recognized as a contributing factor to increasingly frequent, intense, and prolonged extreme heat events (Meehl and Tebaldi 2004). Compared to other regions of the United States, the Southeast experienced the most widespread increase in heat wave days from 1979 to 2011 (Smith, Zaitchik, and Gohlke 2013). In Alabama, each ZIP code had an average of approximately 125 heat wave days (defined as one day or more with a maximum daily heat index of >80°F), which accounts for 82 percent of the 153 days from May to September (Kent et al. 2014).

Extreme heat events are considered significant contributing factors to the increased frequency and severity of road collisions (Qiu and Nixon 2008). Approximately 24 percent of road traffic collisions in the United States every year are related to adverse weather conditions (Goodwin 2003). Moreover, extreme heat events are likely to coincide with other adverse weather conditions, such as precipitation, intense solar radiation, and high humidity, posing an increased threat to traffic safety. For example, a sharp temperature rise likely affects atmospheric water storage and thus the magnitude, frequency, and intensity of rainfall (Shahid and

Minhans 2016). Temperature changes can also affect humidity; high humidity can cloud windows and windscreens and reduce visibility (Al-Harbi, Yassin, and Shams 2012). In addition, human work capacity declines in high-temperature environments (Kjellstrom et al. 2009; Basagaña et al. 2015), which can increase accident risks (Rowlinson and Jia 2015) and cause heat-related illnesses (e.g., heat exhaustion or stroke) under conditions of prolonged exposure (Arcury et al. 2015). Numerous studies have shown that driver performance declines in hot environments. People might become irritable and drowsy, tend to miss signals, drift out of their lane, and make large steering adjustments (Zhou et al. 2014; Basagaña et al. 2015). High road surface temperatures could also cause vehicle breakdowns (e.g., flat tires) and make roads soften or buckle, which could lead to collisions (Vajda et al. 2014).

Using vehicle air conditioning is common when driving in a hot environment. Data on vehicle air conditioning use in Alabama during our study period are not available, even though previous research showed that vehicle air conditioning use was estimated at between 43 percent and 49 percent in the United States (Farrington and Rugh 2000).Actually, people used air conditioning in car cabins considerably depending on climate, time of day, time of year, type of vehicle (including vehicle color), outdoor or indoor parking, occupant clothing, current occupant activity levels, length of trip, vehicle speed, and personal preference (Farrington and Rugh 2000). Air conditioning, however, might not protect drivers from the effect of heat waves because thermal comfort in a vehicle cabin can be affected by air velocity, environmental radiation, and clothing insulation (Hermans et al. 2006; Hodder and Parsons 2007; Farzaneh and Tootoonchi 2008). Moreover, high temperatures increase traffic volumes (Cools, Moons, and Wets 2010), and the effects of exposure to the high temperature outdoors can be indirect and time-lagged, such as dehydration (Grandjean and Grandjean 2007) and low sleep quality in the summer months (Radun and Radun 2006).

Experimental studies have consistently documented the adverse effects of heat on driving performance (Walker, Ackland, and Dawson 2001; Daanen, van de Vliert, and Huang 2003). The association between temperature and road traffic collisions has not been defined, however (Theofilatos and Yannis 2014; Islam, Alharthi, and Alam 2019). Previous findings can be summarized into three general types: One indicates that the temperature-associated collision risk is marginal (Theofilatos and Yannis 2014; Liu et al. 2017) or not statistically significant (Usman, Fu, and Miranda-Moreno 2010), another suggests an association between low temperatures and a greater number of road traffic collisions (Lee et al. 2014; Zhao et al. 2019), and another indicates that increased temperatures pose a risk to traffic safety. For example, the collision risk increased by 2.9 percent (95 percent confidence interval [CI] [0.7 percent, 5.1 percent]) and 3.4 percent (95 percent CI [0.9 percent, 5.9 percent]) during heat wave days in Catalonia, Spain, and the continental United States, respectively (Basagaña et al. 2015; Wu, Zaitchik, and Gohlke 2018).

Methodologically, studies have focused on how absolute temperatures lead to road traffic collisions (Brijs, Karlis, and Wets 2008; Malyshkina, Mannering, and Tarko 2009; Bergel-Hayat et al. 2013). Acclimation is an important determinant of human activities associated with heat exposure, however. For example, people who live in a warmer climate are able to tolerate higher temperatures compared to those who live in a colder climate.

Therefore, it might be more meaningful to define periods of unusual warmth (i.e., heat waves) based on deviations from average local conditions rather than absolute temperature thresholds. Heat waves have numerous quantitative definitions. According to some studies, heat wave definitions based on relative or mean daily temperatures are a simple metric and are predictive of mortality and preterm birth (Anderson and Bell 2011; Kent et al. 2014). Thus, heat wave days were defined as two or more consecutive days on which the mean daily temperature exceeded the 95th percentile. We also present mean daily temperature greater than the 90th, 98th, or the 99th percentile for two or more consecutive days. We show their relevant results in the Supplemental Materials.

Some studies have focused on road traffic collisions associated with heat waves or extreme heat events using temperature data from the nearest weather stations or the weather stations within a specific area, such as the ZIP code, county, or climatic region (Basagaña et al. 2011; Basagaña et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2017). The density and spatial distribution of weather stations, however, which vary between urban and rural areas, and the geometric shape and size of the specific areas might result in measurement errors at traffic collision locations. To minimize measurement errors, we used address-level data (i.e., traffic collision locations) and gridded air temperature data (spatial resolution 12.5 km²) to evaluate whether the collisions occurred in a location experiencing a heat wave.

Existing research, which used heat wave indexes based on gridded temperature data to investigate the effect of heat waves on traffic safety, primarily focused on fatal collisions (Wu, Zaitchik, and Gohlke 2018). Because the factors behind fatal and nonfatal collisions are different (Batool et al. 2018), whether these findings apply to both fatal and non-fatal collisions is unclear. This study addressed both fatal and nonfatal collisions to assess the association between heat waves and traffic safety.

This study area was set to the state of Alabama. Road traffic collisions are the fifth leading cause of death in Alabama (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2018). The total number of road traffic collisions in Alabama increased from 133,219 in 2014 to 159,925 in 2018. The collision fatality rate in the state was higher than the national rate, and the collision-related economic loss increased by56.13 percent between 2009 and 2018 (Alabama Department of Transportation [ALDOT] 2019).

The objectives of this study were to examine (1) whether road traffic collisions occur at a higher frequency during heat waves in Alabama and (2) whether the associations are different when the analysis is stratified by driver-related factors (i.e., gender, age, race, employment status, and driver residence distances), vehicle-related factors (i.e., vehicle usage), and collision-related factors (i.e., rural or urban roads, intersections, and speed limits).

# Materials and Methods

## Traffic Collision Records

We obtained the records ($N=493,829$) of drivers involved in road traffic collisions in Alabama for the warm months (May–September) of 2009 through 2018 from ALDOT. Each record included the driver's demographic information (gender, age group, race, and employment status), collision date, road functional class, collision location (with the latitudinal and longitudinal information), self-reported vehicle usage, and collision-related factors (i.e., rural or urban roads, whether occurred at the intersection, and speed limits) reported by the police. We selected records with valid latitudinal and longitudinal information (492,500 records, approximately 99.7 percent of total records) for analysis.

## Heat Wave Indexes

Heat waves are measured using different approaches without a widely accepted definition (Smith, Zaitchik, and Gohlke 2013). In this study, heat waves were defined as the daily mean temperature above the 95th percentile for two or more consecutive days (i.e., HI95th) because researchers have found this relatively simple metric to be predictive of adverse health outcomes (Anderson and Bell 2011; Kent et al. 2014). We used three other daily mean temperature-based heat wave indexes (HIs), which involved using 90th, 98th, and 99th percentile thresholds (i.e., HI90th, HI98th, and HI99th), to confirm our results made by HI95th (Supplemental Table S.1). In the first step, all historical daily temperatures in the warm seasons (1 May–30 September) from 2009 to 2018 at the same grid cell were ranked to calculate its percentile

threshold for that specific grid cell. Then, we marked a heat wave day (taking HI95th as the example) for the grid cell if its daily mean temperature on that day was higher than the 95th historical daily mean temperature at the same grid cell for two or more consecutive days. We mapped the 95th percentile threshold grids ($N=915$) in Figure 1. The meteorological data in this study, including air temperature, solar radiation, and precipitation data from Phase 2 of the North American Land Data Assimilation System (NLDAS-2), have a spatial resolution of 12.5 km² (Smith, Zaitchik, and Gohlke 2013; Wu et al. 2019). In this study, this metric was redefined using latitudinal and longitudinal information from the traffic collision records. We matched the collision coordinate information to the NLDAS-2 grid cell data and then, we determined whether the collision dates were on heat wave days.

## Driver-, Vehicle-, and Collision-Related Factors

A time-stratified case-crossover design, which is suitable for assessing short-term exposure with acute outcomes, was applied in this study to examine the association between heat waves and road traffic collisions (Crouse et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2017; Wu, Zaitchik, and Gohlke 2018; Zhan et al. 2020). This study design was proposed by Maclure (1991) and frequently used in environmental health studies (Basu, Dominici, and Samet 2005; Janes, Sheppard, and Lumley 2005a, 2005b; Crouse et al. 2012; Tong, Wang, and Guo 2012). In case-crossover designs, each collision-involved driver serves as his or her own control; therefore, known and unknown time-invariant confounders, such as demographic factors (e.g., age, gender, and socioeconomic status), individual characteristics (e.g., lifestyle and physiological status), seasonality, and overlap bias, are inherently adjusted by study design. In other words, the case-crossover design emulates a retrospective non-randomized crossover study. In this design, each case event (a traffic collision) is matched with a counter-factual control exposure period. In the time-stratified sampling design, all days that are on the same days of the week and within the same month as the case days were selected as control periods. For example, days of the week within calendar months could define strata so that the exposure for a case occurring on Monday, 6 July 2015, would be compared with exposure occurring on all other Mondays in July (i.e., 13, 20, and 27 July 2015). Each case therefore contributed up to five observations in the analysis, including one for the case period and one to four for the control periods. Case-crossover data are analyzed in the same manner as a matched case-control design, using the case and matched time-stratified control periods as a stratum in conditional logistic regression models. In the stratified analysis by tertiles of daily mean solar radiation and daily total precipitation, we only kept the strata with the case and its control days that had the same weather condition and excluded those strata if not all control days had the same weather condition with the case day (Table S.4 summarizes the percentages of strata included in the stratified analysis). Analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Conditional logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95 percent CIs for associations between the outcomes of interest and heat wave days. The regression model

in this study only has one independent variable, which is whether the day of the traffic collision was the heat-wave day or not. All other driver-, vehicle-, and collision-related factors were used to stratify the analysis. For instance, gender was used to stratify all cases into two groups: male and female drivers involved collisions. Then, the time-stratified case-crossover analysis was used to examine the effects of heat wave days on traffic collisions on each group, respectively. This study did not use two or more factors to stratify the cases in one single conditional logistic analysis; therefore, there is no multicollinearity issue in the analysis. To summarize statistical results, associations were reported as the percentage change in the odds of the outcome on heat wave days compared to that on non-heat wave days [percentage difference = (OR - 1) × 100].

In the summary of road traffic collisions involving driver data, a chi-square test was used to examine the difference in driver- and collision-related factors between heat wave days and non-heat wave days. A *p* value of ≤0.05 was considered to denote statistical significance, compared to statistically highly significant as *p* ≤ 0.001 (i.e., less than one in a thousand chance of being wrong).

This study chose four different heat wave definitions based on relative daily temperatures (i.e., HI90th, HI95th, HI98th, and HI99th) because they are the most useful and simplest metrics for the United States (Kent et al. 2014). Second, the geographic variation between different study areas (e.g., traffic facilities, culture, and policies) might cause different findings. For instance, Wu et al. (2019) found a significant and positive association (3.4 percent increase, 95 percent CI [0.9 percent, 5.9 percent]) between fatal road traffic collisions and heat waves in the continental United States. The association became negative, however

(2.6 percent decrease, 95 percent CI [-21.1 percent, 20.2 percent]), when we used the same methods and data sources but narrowed down the study area to the state of Alabama. There are two possible reasons for Alabama having low odds of traffic collision on heat wave days. First, for the United States, annual average numbers of heat wave days (defined by relative daily temperature-based HIs, such as HI90th, HI98th, HI95th, and HI99th) in 1979 to 2011 were lower in the Southeast (e.g., Alabama, Georgia, and Florida) than in the Great Plains (e.g., Texas and Oklahoma) and Northwest (e.g., Washington and Oregon; Smith, Zaitchik, and Gohlke 2013). Second, people living in a warm or hot climate zone like Alabama are more likely better prepared for living and working in the heat, such as the highpercentage using air conditioning equipment. For instance, according to the Residential Energy Consumption Survey (U.S. Energy Information Administration 2015), air conditioning is less common in a cooler climate. Therefore, the direct and indirect effects of heat waves on traffic collisions are stronger in other cooler climates than the one in Alabama.

Methodologically, geographic variations were controlled across Alabama. For instance, the climate varies geographically and thus the thresholds used for defining a heat wave day should be different from one place to another (Figure 1). To control the geographic variation in climate across the state in the analysis, we used relative heat wave definitions (HIs) instead of absolute temperatures as a proxy for heat exposure. The 90th, 95th, 98th, and 99th percentile thresholds for each 12.5 * 12.5 km grid cell statewide were determined by ranking all historical daily mean temperatures in the same grid cell from 1 May to 30 September between 2009 and 2018. Each grid cell therefore has different percentile thresholds to determine whether a specific day in it was a heat wave day or not. For instance, the daily mean temperature around 30°C (i.e., 86°F) on a specific day would be a heat wave day for a grid cell in a cold climate (which has the percentile threshold based on its historical daily mean temperatures lower than 30°C) but it would not be a heat wave day for the grid cell located in a warm climate with the percentile threshold higher than 30°C. For the geographic variation in demography, each collision-involved driver in the time-stratified case-crossover design serves as his or her own control according to fixed individual characteristics, such as age, gender, lifestyle, socioeconomic status, genetics, and physiological status. Therefore, all potential time-invariant confounders (including seasonality and overlap bias) are inherently adjusted by this study design. For the geographic variation in the spatial pattern, we noticed the spatial distribution of road collisions varied from urban to rural areas (Figure 2). This spatial pattern suggests that road collisions were more clustered on urban roads, such as in Birmingham, Montgomery, and Mobile than on rural roads, mainly because urban roads have higher traffic volumes than rural roads.

In the fatality-stratified analysis, the association between fatal road traffic collisions and heat wave days was negative, with a 4.7 percent decrease in the

odds of a collision (95 percent CI [22.7 percent, 17.5 percent]). The association between nonfatal road traffic collisions and heat wave days was negative as well, with a 1.3 percent decrease in the odds of a collision (95 percent CI [−3.1 percent, 0.5 percent]). In other words, the effects of heat waves on both fatal and nonfatal road traffic collisions were negative but not significant. By contrast, a study found a significant and positive association (3.4 percent increase, 95 percent CI [0.9 percent, 5.9 percent]) between fatal road traffic collisions and heat waves in the continental United States (Wu, Zaitchik, and Gohlke 2018). That study investigated nationwide fatal road traffic collisions using records from the Fatality Analysis Reporting System provided by the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration. This study investigated statewide road traffic collisions using the collision records from ALDOT. We applied our analysis to the collision data from the Fatality Analysis Reporting System, the source used by the previous research (Wu, Zaitchik, and Gohlke 2018) and obtained similar results: The association between fatal road traffic collisions and heat waves (defined using HI95th) in Alabama was negative (2.6 percent decrease, 95 percent CI [−21.1 percent, 20.2 percent]).

The results of stratified analyses reveal the associations between heat wave days and traffic collisions in different collision-related conditions. For instance, we found that the association between heat wave days and traffic collisions that occurred at the intersections was not significant. Similarly, Mussone, Bassani, and Masci (2017) found that increased air temperatures are related to the decrease in crash severity at urban road intersections. When the analysis was stratified by some societal factors, the results showed that the effects of heat waves on traffic collisions are indirect. For instance, for vehicle usage, the associations between heat wave days and traffic collisions with personal and nonpersonal vehicle usage were insignificantly negative. This result indicates that the effects of heat waves on traffic safety did not change significantly when the purpose of using the vehicle was different. The negative associations were found when the analysis was stratified with speed limit level (low, ≤25 mph; medium, >25 and ≤55 mph; and high, >55 mph). For instance, a significant and negative association was observed for the high speed limit (6.7 percent decrease, 95 percent CI [−11.8 percent, −1.4 percent]). Theseresults suggest that the lower odds of road traffic collisions on highways on heat wave days might be due to reduced long travels in hot weather.

We found a positive and significant association between heat wave days and road traffic collisions stratified by precipitation. Precipitation therefore becomes the most important factor among all factors we used to stratify the analysis in Figures 2 to 4 because its results show significantly positive associations with traffic collisions. The possible reasons behind the positive associations found for driving in nonprecipitation conditions include its higher traffic volume or more vehicles running on the roads than in the precipitation condition. This positive association between road traffic crashes and heat wave days without precipitation can be applied to state policies and interventions aimed at improving transportation safety. For example, the Community Traffic Safety Program supported by the Alabama Department of Economic and Community Affairs focuses on the effective execution of law enforcement duties to prevent traffic violations, collisions, and collision fatalities and injuries at locations most at risk (Alabama Department of Economic and Community Affairs 2020). This finding suggests that this safety program can concentrate law enforcement efforts on dry conditions during heat waves. For heat wave days with precipitation, the association became negative. This negative association is consistent with some findings of a negative correlation between precipitation and the number of road traffic crashes (Andrey 2010; Bergel-Hayat et al. 2013; El-Basyouny, Barua, and Islam 2014); conversely, other researchers have found a positive correlation (Brijs, Karlis, and Wets 2008; Qiu and Nixon 2008; Jung, Qin, and Noyce 2011; El-Basyouny and Kwon 2012).

For many areas—especially rural areas—the NLDAS-2 gridded data offer a closer spatial match for the HI calculation than data from the nearest weather station. We also used coordinate information from road traffic collisions to determine within which HI grids they were located. The analysis of the address-level data provided more accurate exposure estimates than the analysis of ZIP code– or county-level data, the results of which might contain errors due to the geometric shape and size of ZIP codes and counties. Furthermore, we used ten-year (2009–2018) statewide traffic collision data from ALDOT and summer month temperature data from the National Aeronautics and Space Administration

for the HI calculation. Therefore, our study had substantial power in detecting associations between heat waves and road traffic collisions.

This study served as the follow-up research for the previous findings on the associations between heat waves and fatal traffic collisions in the continental United States. Our findings suggest that the associations might need to be confirmed in different states when state policies and interventions are made to mitigate the adverse effects of heat waves on traffic safety.

This study has several limitations. First, we lacked traffic volume data to control the effects of traffic volume on collision numbers. Our time-stratified sampling design, however, which combines data by year and month, produced estimated associations consistent with those from models adjusted for traffic volume (Brijs, Karlis, and Wets 2008). Second, our study only addressed road traffic collisions in the state of Alabama. Further studies are warranted to confirm whether these findings can be applied to other states or regions with different climatic features. Third, the risk of driving on the road varies in some factors that change from day to day, such as other weather conditions (e.g., precipitation and solar radiation). Measured and unmeasured time-varying confounders therefore might create bias in the case-crossover analysis.

## Conclusions

* This study adopted a widely used method in environmental epidemiology to examine the effects of heat waves on traffic collisions in Alabama.
* We did not find an association (−1.4 percent, 95 percent CI [−3.1 percent, 0.4 percent]) between heat wave days and traffic collisions.
* Similar results were found when the analysis was stratified with different driver-, vehicle-, and collision-related factors.
* Our results suggest that previous findings of a positive association between fatal road traffic collisions and heat waves in the continental United States might not apply to certain areas of the United States, such as Alabama. This does not mean, however, that heat waves had little effect on road traffic crashes.
* We found increased odds of road traffic crashes for nonprecipitation days (23.5 percent increase [95 percent CI [7.3 percent, 42.3 percent]]) when heat waves occurred. State policies and interventions for transportation safety can focus on this condition to mitigate the adverse effects of heat waves.* Various factors can cause inconsistent findings regarding the effects of heat waves on traffic collisions, including different HI definitions and geographic variations in traffic facilities, culture, and policies.

This study demonstrates that the association between heat waves and traffic collisions does not hold throughout the United States; thus, a more nuanced look at this relationship is warranted so that we can better understand geographic variability. Methodologically, this study made contributions to (1) bridge physical and human systems by bringing in climatology with societal phenomena and policies, (2) apply the time-stratified case-crossover design in the field of traffic safety by focusing on all traffic collisions in a small area in a decade, and (3) explore why existing studies have inconsistent findings on the effects of heat waves on traffic collisions from a geographical perspective.

Future research and studies can focus on the effects of heat waves on traffic collisions in the Great Plains (e.g., Texas and Oklahoma) and Northwest (e.g., Washington and Oregon) of the United States. Because these regions had higher annual average numbers of heat wave days than the Southeast (e.g., Alabama, Georgia, and Florida; Smith, Zaitchik, and Gohlke 2013), additional research in the Great Plains or Northwest will help scholars better understand how geographic variation might cause inconsistent findings, as well as how that variation might affect localized strategies and policies aimed at improving transportation safety (e.g., the Community Traffic Safety Program). Other future studies should concentrate on the importance of heat wave definitions in traffic safety. Because existing heat wave definitions were mainly proposed and developed in epidemiology and environmental health (Smith, Zaitchik, and Gohlke 2013; Kent et al. 2014), researchers should compare approaches in these heat wave definitions to confirm which ones might be optimal for the analysis related to traffic safety. A heat wave definition designed explicitly for traffic safety can pave the way for future research on similar topics and becomes essential to facilitate the improvement of local traffic policies and interventions, such as a reliable heat wave alert system for drivers.
