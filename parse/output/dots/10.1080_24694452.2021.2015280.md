# Translocal Precarity: Labor and Social Reproduction in Cambodia

W. Nathan Green* and Jennifer Estes†

*Department of Geography, National University of Singapore, Singapore
†Department of Sociology, National University of Singapore, Singapore

Many people in the Global South have left behind rural homes in search of employment in urban and transnational labor markets often defined by precarious work. Employment is insecure, uncertain, and temporary, and for transnational migrants, there is the constant risk of deportation. Although geographers have studied such migrant precarity, there is an increasing interest in its translocal dimensions, particularly related to how precarious work travels home and affects left-behind family members. This scholarship, however, tends to assume that precarity arises primarily in the spaces of production. Drawing on twenty months of ethnographic fieldwork in rural Cambodia, we argue that precarity simultaneously emerges from the challenges of social reproduction faced by kin in rural communities. As such, we further develop the concept of translocal precarity to capture the fragility of social reproduction strategies that migrant households employ across space. Translocal precarity is the looming threat that family members' efforts to support one another might fall apart due to the instability of urban labor markets in tandem with a lack of sustaining infrastructures in rural areas. Our argument advances geographic scholarship on precarity by explaining how it is experienced across the translocal relations that connect the productive and reproductive labor of household members living in different locations. Key Words: Cambodia, labor, migration, precarity, social reproduction.

We sat with Makara under the shade of a mango tree one afternoon while living in her rural Cambodian village. Her two young sons played in the rice field behind us while Makara nursed her newborn. She did not have the money to buy fish for dinner. Instead, her sons had to make do with a papaya given to her by a neighbor. “We never have enough money for good food,” Makara told us, “so my children are always getting sick.” Her husband, Dy, was away working in the capital city’s booming construction sector. His jobs were short-term and low-paid, however, and the money he sent home was always too little.

Kunthea, a secondary school student who lived nearby, was also concerned about remittances. Like all her classmates, Kunthea took five hours of private classes per day on top of attending public school to prepare for the national twelfth-grade completion exam. She normally relied on money from her older sisters working in Thailand and in Cambodia's garment industry to pay for these classes, but one of her sisters had recently been laid off and had sent nothing home that month. Missing her private classes made Kunthea anxious. “If I don't do well on the exam, I won't get into university. Then I'll never get

a good job,” she explained. Kunthea’s mother and sisters were counting on her to support them in the future, and she did not want to fail them.

In this article, we draw on ethnographic research with Makara, Kunthea, and their families to further develop the concept of translocal precarity. Geographers and others have recently studied how precarity travels home, from the site of work to rural households, thereby harming the lives of left-behind family members (Alipio 2019; Tappe and Nguyen 2019; Yea 2019; Silvey and Parreñas 2020). Such analyses tend to locate the source of precarity at the site of production and in the conditions faced by the individual migrant worker. Jobs are often in the informal urban economy, where labor protections are minimal (Davis 2004). Other jobs are in the formal sector, but global competition, surplus populations, and union suppression make them insecure and short-term positions (Munck 2013; Bernards 2018). For those migrants who travel across national borders, citizenship regimes make work even more precarious due to the looming possibility of deportation (Strauss and McGrath 2017; Ye and Yeoh 2018).

Although we agree with these conclusions, we argue that translocal precarity simultaneously emergesfrom the challenges of social reproduction faced by kin in rural communities. In other words, rural life is also precarious, as a result of untenable agrarian livelihoods, household debt, and the privatization of social services like health care and education (Rigg et al. 2016). Translocal precarity is thus more than the export of precarity from one location to the other, from the site of labor's alienation to family members back home. Rather, the concept refers to the fragility of social reproduction strategies that migrant households employ across space. It is the looming threat that family members' efforts to support one another might fall apart due to the instability of urban labor markets in tandem with a lack of sustaining infrastructures in rural areas. Translocal precarity can best be understood by studying the spatial relations of dependency that span multiple bodies (Butler 2009), whose productive and reproductive labor underpins capitalist economies (Mitchell, Marston, and Katz 2003).

This approach advances geographic theory about both rural and migrant precarity. It builds on the notion that precarity has its most analytical and political value when seen as the relationship between forms of labor and the fragile conditions of life (Millar 2014). We do so by conceptualizing this relationship in explicitly spatial terms of translocal householding. Precarity is located neither here nor there; it is experienced across the multiple relationships that connect together different people who are physically removed from one another (Porst and Sakdapolrak 2018; Bylander 2020). Put differently, precarity might be place-based, but it is not place-bound (Massey 1994). Friction of distance, national borders, and differential mobilities variously structure the relationship between precarious life and work, as families face challenges of social reproduction that might be occurring in faraway locations (Katz 2001; Mitchell, Marston, and Katz 2003).

These multiscalar, translocal connections between work and life in times of migration can exacerbate the inequitable distribution of precarity (Oakes and Schein 2006; Brickell and Datta 2011). This is because there are varied trajectories of translocal householding (Douglass 2006). For rural households that are well-positioned economically and socially, migration as a strategy of householding is more likely to raise up living standards, provide for rising aspirations, and strengthen household resilience (Lawreniuk and Parsons 2020). In contrast, already vulnerable

households could become exposed to additional risks and uncertain futures when they depend on remittances from members working in unstable labor markets (Gidwani and Ramamurthy 2018; Yea 2019). In grappling with these inequitable challenges of translocal householding, new social and spatial divisions of labor emerge (Jacka 2018), sometimes reinforcing precarious life (Green and Estes 2019).

We begin by laying out our conceptual understanding of translocal precarity as distinct from how it has been discussed so far within geography and other fields. We then introduce our field site and describe our research methodology. In the second half of the article, we elaborate on Makara's story to show how migrant families rely on reproductive labor in the village, carried out by multiple generations of women, and how this life's work actively shapes the experience of precarity. We then delve into the experiences of Kunthea and her brother, both of whom relied on the precarious work of their mother and siblings to successfully complete secondary education. Their story suggests the possibility that translocal precarity can be negotiated through flexible arrangements within nuclear and extended families to achieve aspirational futures. We conclude the article with the implications for geographic scholarship on precarity.

## On Precarity

Precarity is most commonly used to describe the loss of security and stability in work (Millar 2017; Strauss 2018). It is linked to a wide range of factors, including low pay, uncertain working hours, a lack of worker representation, and limited access to employment rights, social benefits, and other protections. Precarity is “not the fact of consistent unemployment, but the looming threat, and perhaps frequent fact, of it” (Banki 2013, 2). The rise of precarious work has been linked to the economic transformations wrought by a post-Fordist regime of accumulation (Standing 2011), characterized by flexible production, time-space compression, and financial deregulation (Harvey 1989). As global capitalist production became more mobile in the 1970s and 1980s, labor unions lost many of their gains against capital in the European-American core countries. As a result, the traditional capitalist proletariat fragmented into a multitude of groups, marked by“anger, anomie, anxiety, and alienation” (Standing 2011, 19).

This interpretation of precarity, however, tends to ignore the majority of the world’s experiences of precarious work under colonial and postcolonial capitalism. For example, the temporal and spatial bounding of precarity as a condition of post-Fordism privileges a golden era of formal, secure employment that was often available to only a select few workers within Europe and North America, usually those who benefited from race and gender discrimination toward others (Ettlinger 2007; Neilson and Rossiter 2008). In other words, it elides how temporary, dangerous, and insecure work has long defined labor conditions of capitalist production for marginalized groups (Bernards 2018). Moreover, precarious work is highly uneven in its “characteristics, consequences, and contestations” (Lee and Kofman 2012, 405), which are dependent on labor’s spatial and social positioning within global networks of capitalist production.

When viewed from the Global South, precarity is not the result of post-Fordist deindustrialization. Rather, precarious work has long existed in the sense that there was no prior rural idyll (Rigg et al. 2016), especially in the wake of colonialism, late industrialization, and neoliberal structural adjustment (Lee and Kofman 2012). From this perspective, precarity is an outcome of a growing trend in rural dispossession that has produced footloose and surplus labor (Breman 1996; Davis 2004; Li 2010). With the prevalence of surplus labor, competition among workers for limited formal employment has weakened their position vis-à-vis mobile capital, particularly in contexts marked by authoritarianism and militarism (Campbell 2020). Whereas precarity has been experienced as a loss of security and stability in the deindustrialized European-American capitalist core, it is often characterized by informal and unfree labor conditions in the peripheral spaces where production has relocated (Natarajan, Parsons, and Brickell 2019). A reconstructed understanding of precarity, which includes the perspectives of the Global South, views it as an outcome of the restructuring of global capital to take advantage of these spaces of labor (Munck 2013).

## Precarious Life's Work

Yet even this reconstructed understanding of precarity tends to prioritize the conditions of work over the ways in which precarity is experienced as a part

of daily life. In contrast, there is a strand of literature that has focused on the precarity of life itself (Millar 2017). Inspired by the work of Butler (2004, 2009), this scholarship draws attention to the existential condition of precariousness as a shared condition of all life. According to Butler (2009), “Precariousness implies living socially, that is, the fact that one’s life is always in some sense in the hands of the other” (14). Our bodies, as social phenomena, are never only our own (Butler 2004). Life is sustained by reciprocal relations of dependency on people “we may know, barely know, or know not at all” (Butler 2009, 14). Importantly, Butler (2009) distinguished precariousness from precarity. Whereas precariousness is an existential fact of living socially, precarity “designates that politically induced condition in which certain populations suffer from failing social and economic networks of support” (Butler 2009, 25). Without support, alongside arbitrary state violence, such populations are at “heightened risk of disease, poverty, starvation, displacement, and of exposure to violence without protection” (Butler 2009, 25).

To understand precarious life, we must therefore examine the political, economic, and social relations that either sustain conditions for life or magnify exposure to bodily harm. For this reason, Millar (2014) argued that understandings of precarity are incomplete without analyzing the relationship between precarious work and life. In her ethnography of trash collectors in the garbage dumps of Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, she showed that the “everyday emergencies” of life necessitated flexibility of work in ways that made the precarious conditions of garbage collection preferable to the rigid timetables of formal employment (Millar 2014, 34). Based on this research, Millar suggested that precarity has the most analytical and political value “when approached as an open question about the relationship between forms of labor and fragile conditions of life” (Millar 2017, 7).

Geographers have since taken up this approach to understand contemporary conditions of precarity (e.g., Strauss 2018; Rosario and Rigg 2019). Following Meehan and Strauss (2015), we contend that such approaches can be strengthened by engaging with feminist theories of social reproduction. This concept helps us to interpret the relationship between precarious life and work in terms of the relations of labor, care, and dependency that constitute the precarious worker. Although worker reproduction is a foundational element of Marx’s theoryof capital, his writings on reproduction tended to emphasize “the perpetuation of modes of production and the structures of class inequality” (Bakker and Silvey 2012, 4). In contrast, Marxist feminists expanded the definition to include the work that goes into maintaining life and reproducing the next generation (Katz 2001; Federici 2014). Early work in this vein showed that labor-power, as the commodity that produces surplus value necessary for capital accumulation, is maintained by unpaid labor carried out primarily by women within the private spaces of the home (Dalla Costa and James 1975). In short, social reproduction captures the “the fleshy, messy, and indeterminate stuff of everyday life,” which make up the practices “through which social relations and the material basis of capitalism are renewed” (Katz 2001, 709–11).

The concept has since been extended beyond the gendered divisions of labor within the household to include the range of activities and institutions, such as health care and education, needed to sustain life and meet the needs of the workforce (Mitchell, Marston, and Katz 2003; Meehan and Strauss 2015). From this perspective, social reproduction has become more precarious as a result of state policies that erode such sustaining infrastructures (Bakker and Silvey 2012). In the Global South, the policies of colonial states and, later, the imposition of structural adjustment policies actively produced bodily insecurities. For example, the privatization of many social services and public amenities was a condition of sovereign debt restructuring (Davis 2004; Li 2010). Such privatization reforms have often been accompanied by authoritarian forms of governance, which actively suppress activism and political accountability. Furthermore, financial tools like microfinance have produced “debtfare states,” whereby households in the Global South take on debt to manage their own social reproduction in the absence of state services (Soederberg 2014). This financialization of social reproduction has exposed women in particular to greater hardship as they must manage the risks of overindebtedness for their families, while simultaneously negotiating precarious economic transformations (Rankin 2001).

## Translocal Households and Migrant Precarity

In the face of such conditions, migration in search of better employment has increased. As such, rural

households across Asia and other regions now pursue strategies of translocal householding (Douglass 2006; Kelly 2009; Peterson 2010). Translocality encompasses the practices, symbols, and institutions that connect particular locations across multiple scales (Oakes and Schein 2006). The concept arises out of studies on transnational migration, which have long explored “the processes by which immigrants forge and sustain multi-stranded social relations that link together their societies of origin and settlement” (Basch, Glick Schiller, and Szanton Blanc 1994, as cited in Oakes and Schein 2006, 25). Given that domestic migration is more common than transnational migration (Douglass 2006), however, translocality seeks to also encompass the local–local connections that are not necessarily bounded, or defined, by national borders (Brickell and Datta 2011). Nevertheless, issues of citizenship and precarity of place for transnational migrants remain important elements in shaping the relationship between precarious work and life for translocal households (Strauss 2018; Yeoh et al. 2020).

Translocal householding can be either a pathway toward economic betterment and aspiration or a reinforcement of precarity (Mullings 1999; Douglass 2006; Safri and Graham 2010). Such trajectories are influenced by the social and economic conditions of the sending household and migration regimes (Strauss and McGrath 2017; Lawreniuk and Parsons 2020). Much of the geographic scholarship on precarity has focused on how these trajectories are shaped by the conditions of work faced by migrants at their destinations (Waite 2009; Strauss 2018; Ye and Yeoh 2018). There is a growing interest, however, in the translocal dimensions of this precarity as well (Porst and Sakdapolrak 2018; Tappe and Nguyen 2019; Silvey and Parreñas 2020).

Specifically, studies of translocal precarity have explored how migrant precarity can travel back home and harm left-behind families and communities in places of origin (Porst and Sakdapolrak 2018; Alipio 2019; Yea 2019). Yea (2019, 553) described this as “secondary precarity.” In her research on Filipino and Cambodian migrants in the Southeast Asian ocean fishing industry, for example, she showed that these migrants’ unexpected loss of income disrupted remittance flows that families back home needed to pay for the costs of social reproduction. Alipio (2019) similarly observed that migrant precarity exacerbates precarity among left-behindchildren. Bylander (2020) importantly pointed out that translocal precarity is shaped by the necessity and risks of juggling household debt across transnational borders. Although this work has made theoretical inroads into the concept of translocal precarity, Yea (2019) noted that “explaining the precise avenues through which ‘secondary precarity’ is created remains a largely unfilled task” (553).

In what follows, we develop the concept of translocal precarity to move beyond describing how it affects left-behind households. We argue that translocal precarity is constituted by conditions found in migrant labor markets, rural spaces, and how these localities are connected to each other. The characteristics and consequences of translocal precarity are shaped by the relationship between precarious work and life. This relationship is increasingly stretched across space, often spanning national borders, as households pursue translocal strategies of social reproduction. We focus on householding strategies particularly in terms of their generational arrangements. In the context of transforming economies where large numbers of youth are migrating out of rural villages, intergenerational relations of dependency often tie together rural and urban precarity (Rigg et al. 2020).

## Method

To analyze translocal precarity, we draw on findings from broader ethnographic projects conducted by each author. Between 2013 and 2017, we each carried out twenty months of research in a rural village of 283 households in southern Cambodia. The village is in a district of approximately 100,000 people, located three hours by express bus from the capital city of Phnom Penh. For one full year between 2016 and 2017, we lived together with a Cambodian family in a hamlet of twenty-nine households within the village.¹ We chose our study village because it had social and economic characteristics commonly found in rural Cambodia. Residents were Buddhist Khmer speakers and the majority of households engaged in both subsistence and commodity rice agriculture. A minority of others were local merchants, small business owners, and civil servants. Within our hamlet, 55 percent of households had at least one member working outside of the province. Within these translocal households, 81 percent of migrants worked in a different province and 19 percent worked abroad in Thailand or South Korea.²

While living in the village, we carried out participant observation of daily activities and community events, such as religious ceremonies, weddings, and funerals. This method enabled us to study the processes, meanings, and place-making activities central to the transformation of Cambodia's rural economy and society (Herbert 2000). Our research was conducted in the Khmer language, which both authors speak at an advanced level. We informed people in the community about the purpose of our research to gain their consent.

In addition to the participant observation that we conducted together in the village, our analysis draws on research that we conducted separately to serve our broader, individual projects. The first author's study focused on household debt and agrarian transformation. This author engaged in farming activities, did participant observation with a local bank, and interviewed farmers, state authorities, and community leaders, among others. The first author also carried out an economic survey of the households in the study hamlet, including Makara's, to gather descriptive details.³ The second author's project examined the political economy of changing youth life trajectories. This author carried out participant observation in the two secondary schools that serviced the district and conducted semistructured interviews with forty-five youth about their life histories and future aspirations, including with Kunthea and her brother.⁴

In the second half of this article, we analyze the experiences of Makara and Kunthea in terms of translocal precarity. Their stories come from our separate ethnographic projects, although we both had opportunities to interact with each person and their families. The use of intimate case studies is a strength of ethnography as a method (Herbert 2000). It allows us to uncover the processes and meanings that form the relationship between precarious work and life (Millar 2014). Moreover, ethnography helps us to situate families' daily negotiations with precarity in a broader conjuncture of economic and social transformation in rural Cambodia. In doing so, our ethnography advances the geographic theory of precarity by explaining how it is experienced across varied spatial and social relations of translocal householding.

## Reproducing a Translocal Household

We begin with the story of Makara and her husband Dy struggling to raise a family across space.They faced many challenges negotiating intergenerational care work from a distance. These challenges included helping Dy's mother raise their children in the village while she struggled with terminal illness and then, later, coping with rising indebtedness to the country's predatory financial sector. Their family's story demonstrates how precarious life back home can shape migration trajectories by reinforcing precarious work.

Makara and Dy first met in 2006 when they were working in Trat Province in Thailand. At the time, Dy had a job in construction and Makara was cooking in a restaurant that catered to Western tourists. They were among the hundreds of thousands of Cambodians who have migrated to Thailand in recent decades (Derks 2011). Dy was from our study village, where he grew up in a poor family. He first migrated before finishing secondary school. Makara was from northwestern Cambodia, where she was one of ten siblings, most of whom had also migrated to Thailand at a young age. Like many others, Makara and Dy had left their homes in the countryside because they felt that making a living in rural Cambodia was no longer tenable. “There are no good jobs here in the district,” Dy told us when we first met him in his village. “They are unreliable and only part-time. The pay is better in Thailand, and there are a lot of different places to work.”

The challenges of rural living can be traced back to Cambodia's violent history in the last decades of the twentieth century. Civil war wracked the country in the early 1970s, sparked by the wider conflagration of the second Indochina War and culminating in the rise to power of the Khmer Rouge regime in 1975. Led by Pol Pot, the Khmer Rouge launched a Maoist-inspired plan to industrialize the nation through forced, collectivized labor in the countryside. During this time, nearly 2 million people, uprooted from their homes, died from starvation, illness, and execution (Chandler 2008). Following Vietnamese-backed liberation in early 1979, most Cambodians returned to their natal villages to rebuild their livelihoods in the 1980s. In the central provinces of the country, people pursued wet rice farming in small villages, often disconnected from international markets. By the early to mid-1980s, the state began redistributing formerly collectivized land to individual households. This land distribution, however, set the stage for later social and economic inequalities (Gottesman 2003). Dy's parents, for example, had been Khmer Rouge cadre

leaders and thus had fled the village after the regime's fall to escape retribution. They only returned in the early 1990s, at which point the best agricultural land had already been claimed by their former neighbors.

The Cambodian state, led by the Cambodian People's Party and backed by Western aid, has long promoted commodified forms of intensive agriculture for economic development. In the past decade, it has implemented a national export policy to formally integrate the country's rice sector into regional and international markets. Yet the state has provided minimal support to farmers to compete with neighboring Thailand and Vietnam, two of the largest rice exporters in the world (Cramb 2020). Similar to agrarian crises found elsewhere (Akram-Lodhi and Kay 2009), small farmers in Cambodia now struggle with rising costs of inputs, inequitable access to water, and terms of trade dictated by local traders and an oligopoly of rice millers and exporters (Green 2021). Cambodians like Makara and Dy have thus found it increasingly difficult to reproduce their households through farming alone.

With agriculture untenable and rural employment scarce, many people feel little choice but to leave behind their home villages (Natarajan, Parsons, and Brickell 2019). In fact, following regional examples (Douglass 2006), the Cambodian government has made the export of labor part of its official policy to improve people's livelihoods. It has signed memoranda of understanding with Thailand, Malaysia, and South Korea to facilitate the flow of workers to transnational labor markets (Derks 2011). As such, Cambodian migrants are now “doing family” across national borders like other precarious workers in the region (Yeoh et al. 2020).

Makara and Dy had their first two sons while in Thailand, but the pressures of work made it difficult to raise children there. Few Cambodian workers in Thailand have secure employment contracts, and they can be cheated out of wages or have their pay temporarily withheld (Bylander 2019). Jobs in factories and industrial farms also frequently involve health hazards (Derks 2011). Moreover, although Makara and Dy had both originally migrated with proper paperwork, they eventually overstayed their visas. The possibility of deportation—or even jail time—always hung over them. Makara told us, “Our neighbor here in the village went to jail for three months for overstaying his visa. We worried all the time that could happen to one of us.” For Makaraand Dy, their neighbor's experience was a cautionary tale of the "precarity of place" familiar to many migrants (Banki 2013).

At first, Makara and Dy were willing to take on such risks because they did not want to give up the relatively high wages they could earn in Thailand. Eventually, however, they decided that it would be best for their children if they sent them home to live with Dy's mother in the village. Although grandparents have long assisted with intergenerational care in Cambodia, the rise of translocal householding has made families more dependent on these arrangements. The rising costs of social reproduction, alongside growing debt levels, have made intergenerational care more compulsory and based on monetary relations (Green and Estes 2019).

Having Dy's mother look after their children worked for a while. By the time we met the family in 2016, however, the precarity of life in the village had compelled Makara and Dy to return home from Thailand. Dy's mother had passed away from cancer earlier in the year. Her illness had gone largely untreated because she lacked access to affordable, quality health care near the village. Cambodia's state-run medical facilities are chronically underfunded, particularly in rural areas (Ovesen and Trankell 2017). The Cambodian state has invested little in its health care infrastructure in part because it adopted fiscal austerity policies as part of structural adjustment conditionalities in the 1990s, and more recently, to cultivate a financial landscape attractive to foreign investors (Springer 2010). These neoliberal policies have been inflected by authoritarian tendencies of the ruling government (Lawreniuk 2020). The Cambodian People's Party has actively undermined social services by channeling resources from local communities upward to the party elite as part of its patrimonial form of governance (Eng and Ear 2016). Given the low quality of state services, rural households often feel compelled to use private medical facilities in urban areas (Ovesen and Trankell 2017).

In some ways, Dy's mother suffered from secondary precarity (Yea 2019). The precarious work of her children in Thailand made it difficult for her to pay for appropriate health care when she was sick, because their remittances were too meager and irregular. In this sense, precarity traveled home. This interpretation only gets at one dimension of her precarity, though. Her family's translocal strategy of

intergenerational reproduction, in which she was responsible for caring for her two grandchildren by herself, meant that she did not have the means or the time to visit a hospital in Phnom Penh. Moreover, unlike wealthier neighbors in her village, many of whom were also dependent on household members working in Thailand, she was unable to cope with losses in remittance income. These other households had accumulated land since the 1980s and thus had other assets to mortgage or sell to pay for medical emergencies (Green and Estes 2019). In contrast, Dy told us, "My family was always the poorest in the village. My siblings and I grew up with very little." More generally, precarious life for Dy's mother in the village was compounded by the "absence of sustaining infrastructures" of Cambodia's political economy (Butler 2015, 119). The privatization of state services for health care, driven by fiscal austerity and patrimonial governance, meant that social safety nets were either absent, or insufficient, for poorer households. For Dy's mother, this absence of support for social reproduction contributed to the conditions of letting die found throughout rural areas in the region (Li 2010).

After Dy's mother had passed, Makara and Dy had to figure out new strategies to sustain their family. They could not go to Makara's home in northwestern Cambodia, because there was no place for them to live and her siblings had no resources to help. Makara told us, "I've already borrowed money from my sisters, but they can't help me anymore. They have their own problems with debt now." Returning to Thailand was not an option, either. In 2014, there was a mass exodus of Cambodians from Thailand following a police crackdown on irregular migrants (Lawreniuk and Parsons 2017). Dy and Makara felt that the official paperwork to reenter Thailand was too expensive and the risks of going without that paperwork were too great. The issue of employment was a pressing one, however. Dy wanted to grow vegetables in the village, but he did not have the money to build a well for irrigation. "Maybe after I save up some money working in the city, I can come back and plant some sugarcane and lettuce," Dy explained. He and Makara decided that he should find work in Phnom Penh, even though the pay was lower than in Thailand.

Makara and Dy felt this precarity acutely, which
affected both their life and work. When Dy's mother
was alive and their sons had first been sent back
from Thailand to live with her, she had taken out a
US$3,000 loan from a microfinance institution with
Dy as a guarantor on the contract. At the time, his
mother's house lacked a bathroom, and the kitchen
was located outside under a thatched roof. The fam-
ily had wanted safer living conditions for the chil-
dren, so they put the money toward constructing a
new home. After Dy's mother passed away, the full
responsibility for repaying the loan fell onto Dy.
Each month, while Dy was away, microfinance credit
officers would show up at the house asking Makara
to hand over her husband's loan repayments. Makara
almost never had enough money to give them. She
and Dy wanted to negotiate for lower loan repay-
ment rates, but Makara could not do this by herself
because her name was not on the loan. As such, Dy
had to return home periodically to try to resolve his
bad debt. Although his employment was in jeopardy
each time he left the city, the stakes were too high
for him to stay away. Dy and Makara had witnessed
Dy's uncle, who lived next door, being coerced into
selling his land to make loan repayments. They
feared this could happen to them as well. "If we sell
the land, we will have nothing to give our children
when they are older," Makara lamented. "We cannot
sell our land. Where will they build their houses?"

In their efforts to feed their children and hold
onto their land, Makara and Dy had adopted spatialstrategies of reproduction that exposed them to greater translocal precarity. They had to negotiate everyday emergencies across space, destabilizing the already precarious working conditions Dy faced in the construction industry. The friction of distance separating Dy from his family made journeys home expensive and time consuming. It was not just the unregulated and dangerous conditions of Dy's work that caused precarity. It was also the challenge of resolving family problems from a distance. Although translocal householding has been beneficial for some in Cambodia (Lawreniuk and Parsons 2020), for Makara and Dy, negotiating reproduction across sites of work and life contributed to their precarity. Indeed, such translocal precarity is self-reinforcing. As an increasing number of rural households sell their land in distress, they have few options left but to migrate out of the village to enter into precarious labor markets (Cambodian Alliance of Trade Unions, Center for Alliance of Labor and Human Rights, and Cambodian League for the Promotion and Defense of Human Rights 2020).

## Education and Precarious Life

We now turn to the story of Kunthea to examine how translocal precarity can be negotiated to achieve a more stable future. Kunthea and her brother, Seng, were the first in their family to successfully complete secondary school in 2017 in large part because of the support provided by their mother and siblings, who had migrated to work in precarious labor markets. In pursuing this household arrangement, each family member experienced translocal precarity differently. Yet their story also shows that the outcome of translocal householding is an open question, in which migrant forms of labor might offer the possibility of alleviating the fragile conditions of rural life.

Kunthea had three older sisters and a brother, Seng, who was born just ten months prior to her. When Kunthea was young, her parents struggled to grow enough rice on their small plot of poor farmland. They often fought with each other as they tried to make ends meet, and they divorced soon after Kunthea was born. Kunthea's mother, Sampoah, was left to care for all the children, but her husband claimed most of their property as his own. This was not an uncommon outcome for women divorcees, who continue to face social stigma

in Cambodia and are disproportionately harmed by the precarity of divorced home life. It is rare for ex-husbands in Cambodia to provide monetary support for their children. Instead, the responsibility generally lands on the woman (Brickell 2020). Sampoah continued to farm what little land she had left, but she also needed to take on odd jobs to cover the family's expenses. She worked on other people's farms during the rice harvesting and transplanting periods and sold foraged berries at the market. Kunthea remembers her childhood as a struggle. “We always had some food and a place to sleep, but things were hard,” she told us. “We were constantly short of rice, clothes, and proper shelter. When it would rain, water would enter through our broken roof and walls.”

Like most rural people of her generation, Kunthea's mother, Sampoah, had gone to school only through the fifth grade. She wanted her children, however, to attend university. Sampoah believed in both the intrinsic value of education and its strategic value to help the family achieve some level of financial stability in the long run. She aspired for her children to obtain white-collar work with the government. In Cambodia, both parents and youth covet state jobs because of the reliable pay and low likelihood of being fired. “If we work for the state, we are not worried when we get sick,” Kunthea explained. “If we work for a private company, we need to ask for sick leave, and even if we get it they will still deduct our pay.” Sampoah was hardly alone in wanting her children to acquire a higher education. Families in many contexts are investing heightened levels of time, money, and attention into the process of raising and educating children (Katz 2018). In her studies of generational social reproduction, Katz (2018) suggested that treating children as “accumulation strategies” is a way that families attempt to shore up their futures in a time of deep economic insecurity across the globe.

For many Cambodians, however, the education needed to obtain a stable government job is financially out of reach. Only 24 percent of rural youth continue schooling past the ninth grade (National Institute of Statistics 2018). Although state school is nominally free, there are many associated expenses. In particular, paid private classes have been woven into the fabric of the Cambodian public education system since the early 1990s (Brehm 2017). State schooling alone is insufficient for students to meetbasic educational goals due to high student-to-
teacher ratios, limited contact hours, and a lack of
teaching materials. If students want to pass the high-
stakes twelfth-grade completion exam, which deter-
mines admission into universities, it is particularly
imperative that they attend private classes. The
Cambodian state has failed to regulate these private
classes, let alone improve the quality of the public
education system to help reduce their necessity. This
failure is an outcome of both the state's larger neo-
liberal commitment to the privatization of public
services necessary for social reproduction (Brehm
2017) and a political strategy of the Cambodian
People's Party. Specifically, the party has long pre-
ferred to build new school buildings, rather than sup-
port preexisting schools, because such construction
projects allow the party to visibly display their power
and garner votes in the countryside (Hughes 2006).

The Cambodian state has thus shifted the financial
burden for education onto families who pay for their
children's private classes. Families must also cover the
costs of other necessities not provided by schools, like
transportation, uniforms, and learning materials. In
2014, Cambodian households spent approximately
US$324—or nearly one third of average national
incomes (World Bank 2014)—on educational expen-
ditures for each child in their family who was attend-
ing upper secondary school. By contrast, the
Cambodian Ministry of Education, Youth, and Sport
spent only US$95 per student (Brehm 2017).

Given these costs, as well as the duration of
schooling, many Cambodian families must juggle
education and precarious work. They simply cannot
afford the investment required to send all of their
children through secondary school, let alone univer-
sity (Eng and Hughes 2017). Instead, some parents
encourage, or sometimes force, children to quit
school and migrate for work to help contribute
money to the education of their younger siblings,
who ideally will one day provide for the whole fam-
ily (Derks 2008). In other words, families balance
the short-term economic benefits of additional wages
from some children with long-term financial invest-
ments in other children's education. Such was the
case in Kunthea's family. From a young age,
Kunthea and her brother Seng were quick learners
and loved to study. Their mother and sisters recog-
nized their academic potential. As Seng put it, "All
my older siblings decided to quit school and look for
jobs so they could support the family. They stopped

studying and started working to earn money to sup-
port my education.” The family’s goal was for
Kunthea and Seng to finish secondary school and go
onto university, so that they could eventually get
stable jobs with the government. At that point, they
would share a portion of their salaries with their
mother and sisters. “They would rather not have
anything for themselves so they can support me, so I
will support them back as much as I can,”
Seng explained.

To find work, Kunthea's oldest sister dropped out
of school after the eighth grade and migrated to
Thailand, where she worked on industrial shrimp
farms. Soon thereafter, Kunthea's other two sisters
left for jobs in garment factories in Phnom Penh and
in the coastal province of Kampong Som. They had
followed in the footsteps of many other young
women. Cambodia's garment sector has taken off
since the turn of the millennium due to preferential
trade deals with the United States and the European
Union, as well as foreign direct investment, particu-
larly by Chinese companies. It is now one of the
largest sources of wage labor in the country, employ-
ing nearly 700,000 workers, 90 percent of whom are
women (Lawreniuk 2020). On average, each of these
workers supports an estimated four family members
who live back home (Cambodian Alliance of Trade
Unions, Center for Alliance of Labor and Human
Rights, and Cambodian League for the Promotion
and Defense of Human Rights 2020), including both
their elderly parents and their young children
(Green and Estes 2019).

With their sisters laboring to help pay for their
education, Kunthea and Seng felt a great deal of
pressure to succeed in school. To do so, they took
an average of five hours of private classes a day on
top of attending state school. Paying for these clas-
ses, however, was a constant challenge. “It costs a
lot of money to buy study materials and pay for
classes,” Kunthea lamented. “Fees increase every
year, too. It is difficult for us, children of farmers, to
pursue what we love.” With unreliable work con-
tracts, Kunthea’s older sisters often had to switch
jobs. Garment factories in Cambodia are notorious
for mass layoffs and shuttering their doors, often
with no notice, when they face bankruptcy. Despite
such labor abuses, independent labor union activity
is actively suppressed by the Cambodian People’s
Party (Lawreniuk 2020), making it difficult for fac-
tory workers to seek redress. Under these precariouswork conditions, there were periods of time that Kunthea and Seng did not receive remittances from their sisters.

Eventually, Kunthea's mother, Sampoah, decided to enter into wage labor herself to help pay for their private classes. She left her home for Kampong Som, where she found work as a cleaner in a hotel. Kunthea and Seng constantly worried about her. Sampoah's health was already poor, and this new work put her under additional strain. Moreover, with their mother gone, Kunthea and Seng had to take on house and farm work on top of their studies. Kunthea cooked meals and Seng cared for the family's two cows, which often meant arriving at school late in the mornings. That year, the family did not grow any rice, because Kunthea and Seng could not manage this labor-intensive task alone and still have sufficient time to study. Without their own crop, however, they had to rely even more on their mother's and sisters' remittances to purchase food.

Life got even more precarious for them when one of Kunthea's sisters married and gave birth to her first child. The family decided that Sampoah would quit her job as a cleaner to look after the baby so that Kunthea's sister could continue working in the garment sector, which paid slightly more. The need for child care disrupted Sampoah's work, which in turn hindered Kunthea's and Seng's education. Once they no longer received remittances from their mother, they found that they barely had enough money to cover food, let alone pay for their private classes. The two started taking on whatever odd jobs they could find locally.

Kunthea and Seng were anxious about how all this extra labor affected their studies. Seng, who often stayed up well after midnight catering for local weddings, would come to school exhausted the next day and sometimes fall asleep during his classes. He and Kunthea were at a distinct disadvantage compared with their better-off peers, whose family members had stable state jobs or worked as merchants. These other students did not have to worry about making payments for private classes or coming to school overly tired. Seng was frustrated that the school did not do more to help students who came from poor families. He explained that if he were the principal, "I would create a working group to support poor students such as providing them with uniforms and study materials. I would try to support them as much as possible, so that they would not feel so

discouraged at school." In the absence of such institutional support, however, many students like Kunthea and Seng struggled to stay in school.

Kunthea and Seng were deeply concerned that given their circumstances, they would be unprepared for the twelfth-grade completion exam. Nevertheless, as they neared the end of secondary school, they needed to make plans for what they would do if they passed and were accepted into a university in Phnom Penh. Even if their siblings kept sending remittances, they knew that they could never afford tuition plus the costs of living in the city. Kunthea and Seng had a few older cousins who had migrated to Phnom Penh several years before. "Though they are not rich, they are very helpful," Kunthea told us, and so she reached out to them for assistance, casting her "net of obligations" beyond the nuclear family (Lawreniuk and Parsons 2020, 12). One of their cousins, who worked at a new mall in the city, agreed that Kunthea could come live with her in her small apartment and that she would help Kunthea find a part-time job. Another cousin, who worked at a private primary school, offered to house Seng and arrange for him to teach a few hours a week at the school.

By the end of 2017, Kunthea and Seng successfully completed secondary school, passed the exam, and continued on to university. In the absence of sufficient state support for education, however, they were only able to achieve this goal because of the productive and reproductive labor of multiple members of their household. To provide Kunthea and Seng with an education, their family strained under the "looming threat" of unemployment and illness (Banki 2013). Their siblings had to migrate to secure their economic future by entering into insecure work in Thailand and in Cambodia's garment sector. Their mother risked her health to work as a cleaner, even as she also cared for her children and grandchildren and sent home remittances. In other words, the family had pursued fragile strategies of social reproduction, thereby experiencing translocal precarity, so that one day Kunthea and Seng might achieve white-collar jobs with the state. Their future was still precarious, however. As Kunthea explained, "Some [students] finish their studies but can only find a job in a factory. They tried to study for years, but after they finished, they could not find good jobs." She worried that despite their best efforts, and those of their sisters and mother, that could all too easily be what happened to them.## Conclusion

Precarity today cannot be fully understood without examining the reproductive work that maintains and constitutes labor on a daily and generational basis. Much of the precarious work across the world today is done by migrants, however, and so the spaces of social reproduction far from labor's alienation are often overlooked. By analyzing ethno-graphic examples from our fieldwork in rural Cambodia, we have further developed the concept of translocal precarity to capture how the spatially extended strategies of social reproduction that sustain migrant workers and their families are made fragile by both the instability of urban labor markets and the costs of social services in rural areas. This conceptualization shows that precarity does not just arise from insecure and dangerous conditions of labor, which then travel home. Rather, precarity is experienced across the translocal relations of dependency that connect the productive and reproductive labor of multiple people living in different locations.

This argument has several implications for studies on precarity. Our research supports the findings of others who criticize essentialized theories of precarity as a general condition of neoliberal, post-Fordist capitalism (Bernards 2018; Strauss 2018). Precarity is diverse in its characteristics and consequences, determined in part by labor's social and economic position within global capitalism. Even this definition, however, does not capture the constitutive links between precarious work and life (Millar 2014). As Butler (2004, 2009) argued, precarious life is a politically induced condition whereby inadequate institutions of support, combined with state violence, disproportionately expose some populations to heightened risks and bodily harm. In the context of Cambodia, the supporting institutions of life are unavailable to people who cannot afford them as a result of the privatization of health care and education, rural indebtedness, and state patrimonialism.

We took this relationship between precarious work and life and turned to its spatial constitution across translocal sites of household reproduction. Although the connections that tie together these places have the potential to bolster household resilience, they can also reinforce precarity. Such outcomes depend on the specific arrangement of migration routes, conditions of work, and household forms of dependency. For instance, Makara and Dy experienced a deepening of translocal precarity,

whereas Kunthea and Seng achieved an aspirational future of attending university. Their different stories further showed that the composition and norms of family structure are important dimensions of translocal precarity. In navigating translocal livelihoods, families in Cambodia are both relying on normative divisions of labor within the household and forming new arrangements. The burdens of precarious life are often disproportionately borne by women, especially divorcees like Kunthea's mother, because they continue to be primary caregivers in accordance with Cambodian gender norms. In contrast, the generational divisions of labor are changing. Older generations increasingly find themselves engaged in the care work of raising grandchildren. At the same time, younger generations must sometimes navigate the challenges of reproduction on their own, due to the absence of parents.

In sum, future geographic research on precarity would benefit from studying translocal strategies of household reproduction. For labor geographers, it means a continued need to theorize conditions of work with an eye toward the life's work that constitutes labor. Incorporation into labor markets is conditioned not only by the classed, raced, and gendered contestations over the terms of work (Strauss 2019). It is also constituted by the social spaces of dependency that bring the laborer into the market every day. With rural to urban migration an ongoing feature of capitalist trajectories (Rosario and Rigg 2019), this means that labor geographers must take seriously the conditions of rural life. For rural geographers, the concept of translocal precarity can help to advance studies on agrarian transformation. Indeed, social reproduction has been largely overlooked in critical agrarian studies (Gidwani and Ramamurthy 2018). By locating precarity in the social spaces of both household production and reproduction, such geographers will be better positioned to analyze questions pertaining to agrarian change.
