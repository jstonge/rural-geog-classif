# “They Will Destroy Themselves Wanting Purely American”: Labor and Carceral Immigration Enforcement in the U.S. Pacific Northwest

Leah Montange

Department of American Studies, University of Toronto, Canada

This article examines the upheaval associated with the extension of carceral immigration enforcement into a particular rural county in Washington State. Migrant workers in shellfish, cranberry, and tourism industries began to leave the county, either through the forced mobility of deportation or quasi-voluntarily, to rejoin deported family or to avoid deportation. This process simultaneously constrained the agency of undocumented workers and presented employers with a destabilized race and labor regime that they represented as a labor shortage. In this situation, the local race and labor regime was destabilized, to the detriment of local capitals. This article extends the understanding of the regulation of labor via carceral immigration enforcement, arguing for an understanding of the place-specific and conjunctural nature of the articulation of immigration control and labor regimes. Such an approach reveals how immigration enforcement's many functions, including the sovereignty-producing and political capital-producing functions, can work in contradiction with the labor regulating, social control functions. Key Words: *carceral geography, deportation, immigration enforcement, migrant labor, race and labor*.

Port of the Peninsula sits on the slim Long Beach peninsula on Willapa Bay in southwestern Washington. The port consists of a small dock lined with crabbing skiffs and oyster dredgers; just to the north, you can see where long wooden poles mark out shellfish beds. These beds were blocked out and allocated to settler shellfish growers long ago, before Washington became a state. Locals claim that this bay grows 25 percent of the commercial oyster crop in the United States. Between 2016 and 2018, people working in the port frequently observed U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agents here. They surveilled the parking lot, presumably to identify potential targets among the Mexican and Latinx shellfish workers.

Frances,¹ who owns a shellfish farm, met me in this same port parking lot. She stepped down from her truck to greet me when I pulled up—she planned to show me what shellfish production looked like and to talk with me about how ICE enforcement affected her business. Before we could really start talking, though, she received a phone call—an employee calling her back. He had not come to work that day, but it was not because he was picked up by ICE. This was a U.S. citizen, just out of high school. Evidently, he had missed work

because he had partied too hard the night before. After our tour of the port, Frances brought me back to her office where she told me that not showing up to work was not something that they would have tolerated in the past, but right now, she explained, there is a labor shortage, and they must be more flexible.

In this article, I examine the relationship between immigration enforcement and a local labor regime, including its attendant racial discourses and geographies of social reproduction, in the U.S. interior. In Pacific County, a rural county in southwestern Washington State, a particular race and labor regime had developed, contingent on the presence of a community of undocumented workers of Mexican origin.² As the region I studied became a site for intensified carceral immigration enforcement, however, the presence of this working population, and the labor regime that congealed around that presence, became destabilized. I argue that this case gives us evidence that the relationship between labor control and immigration control is conjunctural.

I use the term *carceral immigration enforcement* to indicate the various ways that confinement, surveillance, and mobility regulation are used in the enforcement of immigration law. These includeconfinement in detention centers; surveillance and policing in the community; and immobilizing and then forcing mobility through arrest, transport, and deportation. In the United States, carceral immigration enforcement has expanded through a series of techniques, which include the blurring of the boundary between immigration and criminal law (Stumpf 2006); the devolution of immigration law enforcement to local jurisdictions through programs like Secure Communities and 287 g (Varsanyi et al. 2012; Armenta 2017); the expansion of digital surveillance and e-carceration techniques into immigration enforcement (Gonzalez 2019); and the literal and figurative criminalization of immigration (Macías-Rojas 2016).

Together these techniques have greatly extended the reach of immigration enforcement capacity in the U.S. interior (Coleman 2007). Scholars in particular have highlighted how migration regimes characterized by precarious, temporary, or illegalized sociolegal statuses contribute to vulnerability of non-citizen populations to labor exploitation (Harrison and Lloyd 2012), and to unfree conditions like reduced mobility and confinement in the workplace (Mares 2019; Guzmán and Medeiros 2020). In particular, the unfreedoms introduced by carceral immigration enforcement are thought to qualitatively and quantitatively regulate the migrant labor force in a manner favorable to capital (De Genova 2010; Golash-Boza 2015).

In this article, I explore carceral immigration enforcement as a relational force that (dis)regulates a local labor regime. I do so through a case study that emphasizes the articulation (S. Hall 1980) of capital accumulation, national immigration policy shifts, and racial ideology in place. My research extends scholarship on the intersection of labor control and immigration control by focusing attention on the conjunctural nature of the articulation of social relations and forces in a local labor regime.

To frame my study, I first situate it within a broader literature on the role of carceral immigration enforcement in the regulation of labor and social reproduction, and literature on spatial articulations of place and labor regimes. The analysis then unfolds over several sections that outline the conjuncture of various relations and forces in Pacific County. I describe the race and labor regime that was in place in Pacific County, which relied on a disciplined, flexible Latinx workforce. I then describe the

destabilizing impact of increased carceral immigration enforcement on this labor regime, as an already tight labor market became increasingly strained due to ICE targeting of workers at their homes, workplaces, and in the community. In this case, carceral immigration enforcement introduced forced mobilities and constrained agencies that destabilized the local labor regime, producing what many locals described as a “labor shortage.” As workers left the region, either because they were deported or to avoid deportation, employers and other community members dealt with the destabilization. In the concluding section, I discuss how a particular articulation of relations and forces came together in this case, to argue that the way that carceral immigration enforcement shapes unfreedom within labor relations and marginalization in the geography of social reproduction is conjunctural. Such an approach reveals how immigration enforcement’s many functions, including the sovereignty-producing and political capital-producing functions, can work in contradiction with the labor regulating, social control functions, resulting in this situation where the local labor regime was destabilized, to the detriment of local capitals.

# Migrant Labor Regimes and Unfreedom

## Immigration Control and Labor Regulation

Scholarship on precarious immigration status and migrant labor has tended to emphasize how vulnerability to immigration enforcement marginalizes migrant workers, rendering them exploitable. Qualitatively, the threats of arrest, detention, and deportation have been particularly important levers of power of employers over noncitizen workers. People with precarious or no legal status can lack access to labor protections. They are then susceptible to abusive workplace relations, and to employer retaliation for workplace self-advocacy or organizing via reporting workers’ unauthorized presence to the state (Cho and Smith 2013). Carceral immigration enforcement’s regulation of labor can also work less directly, though. Immigration enforcement is thought to complement the needs of employers by forging a distinction between citizen and noncitizen workers (Bauder 2006; Sharma 2012) and thereby regulating the labor supply. De Genova (2002) coined the term deportability to conceptualize thevulnerability that comes with sociolegal status and conditions noncitizen, especially undocumented, workers as racially devalued laborers. For De Genova (2010), the spectacles of bordering, detaining, and deporting cover over social relations in which ille- galized migrant labor is made both ubiquitous and subordinate. It simultaneously racializes workers and rationalizes the hierarchical distribution of wages and protections. In other words, the deportation of some shores up the vulnerability and exploitation of the many. Geographers Coleman and Kocher (2011) conceptualized this vulnerability as “migrant incapa- citation,” or vulnerability that cuts across not only the work life, but the everyday lives and mobilities of those who are deportable.

Golash-Boza (2015) also described a relationship between deportation and the regulation of the size of the working population, arguing that “mass deporta- tion of men of color ... [is] a U.S. policy response designed to relocate surplus labor to the periphery and to keep labor in the United States compliant” (5). That is, deportation can sop up and remove sur- plus labor from society, thus quantitatively regulating the number of workers. In her rigorous history of the U.S. Border Patrol, Hernández (2010) charted how enforcement ebbed and flowed with the economy and labor relations in the U.S. Southwest, becoming more lax when unemployment levels were low, and tightening when economic recession, depression, and high unemployment set in.

These literatures bring into focus a picture of detention and deportation's role in regulating the working population both qualitatively and quantita- tively, by shaping a disciplined, socially marginalized, and flexible workforce, and modulating the size of the working population according to labor market needs to the advantage of capital. Nevertheless, the relationship between immigration control and labor control is quite complex, and carceral immigration enforcement has multiple functions beyond labor control. Scholars have especially attended to how carceral immigration enforcement and bordering function to reinscribe racial hierarchies, expendabil- ity, and exclusions that are constitutive of national narratives (Walia 2010; Márquez 2012; Sharma 2012). Carceral immigration enforcement is also “configured as a preeminent means of defending, enacting, and thus verifying state sovereignty, by defining who is disposable and who is not” and thus it has the effect of producing illegalized subjects and

illicit mobilities (Zilberg 2011, 4; see also Ngai 2004; A. Hall 2010). Moreover, the production of sovereignty, state power, and noncitizens can be, and often are, politicized within party politics and right-left struggles within federal and local govern- ments (Nevins 2002). Further, carceral immigration enforcement has multiple effects beyond relations in the workplace, many of which are spatial. Scholars have widely acknowledged the impact of carceral immigration enforcement on the geography of social reproduction for families with precarious legal status in the U.S. interior, by affecting mobility and trans- portation patterns (Stuesse and Coleman 2014; Maldonado, Licona, and Hendricks 2016), access to institutions of social reproduction like schools and health care (Xu and Brabeck 2012), access to healthy foods (Mares 2019), and by stretching family units transnationally across borders (Berger Cardoso et al. 2016; Ybarra and Peña 2017).

## Place, Articulation, and Labor Regimes

My article extends scholarship on the intersection of labor control and immigration control by theoriz- ing this complexity through a conceptualization of place, articulation, and conjuncture in local labor regimes. Jonas (1996) coined the term *local labor control regime*, which he defined as “an historically contingent and territorially embedded set of mecha- nisms which co-ordinate the time-space reciprocities between production, work, consumption and labour reproduction within a local labour market” (325). This concept encompasses how local labor markets are regulated not only at the point of production, but also in the spaces of consumption and reproduc- tion. That is, labor control extends through spatial configurations that encompass workers’ home, recre- ation, schooling, and consumption sites. Numerous ethnographers studying undocumented labor in rural areas of the United States in the twenty-first century have highlighted how undocumented agricultural workers manage their vulnerability to immigration enforcement by limiting their mobility, remaining close to their homes and workplaces, and maintain- ing their invisibility in landscapes of consumption (Nelson, Trautman, and Nelson 2015; Maldonado, Licona, and Hendricks 2016; Mares 2019). Hypervisibility to immigration enforcement officers and invisibility in the community (Licona and Maldonado 2014) marginalizes their presence in therural landscape, affecting food access, health care access, everyday mobilities, and the creation of social networks (Mares 2019; see also Guzmán and Medeiros 2020). This marginalization in turn affects workers' power in the workplace, making workers dependent on their employer for access to key resources like food, dampening the ability to advocate for better conditions of employment, shaping a tolerance for challenging work schedules or lax safety measures, and even perpetuating confinement to the workplace and homeplace (Harrison and Lloyd 2012; Mares 2019; Guzmán and Medeiros 2020).

Deportability and the racialization of migrant workers (along with other forces) have shaped local labor regimes with precarious or undocumented soci-olegal statuses of immigrants (cf. Torres et al. 2013; Nelson, Trautman, and Nelson 2015). In their study of local labor regimes in rural areas undergoing gen-trification, Nelson, Trautman, and Nelson (2015) traced the recruitment and employment of “a grow-ing Latino presence” that is “predicated on powerful processes of exclusion and invisibility for low-wage immigrant newcomers subordinated by hierarchies of class, race, and illegality” (843). In the labor regime that Nelson, Trautman, and Nelson (2015) found, vulnerability and marginalization were structured by intersecting processes like deportability, racialized assumptions about workers’ right to belong and to a home life, as well poverty. This article responds to these scholars’ call for further scholarly attention to labor regimes in rural areas and the invisibilities of migrant labor in these landscapes.

In this article, I complicate this picture through a case study where the unfreedoms associated with car-ceral immigration enforcement have destabilized a local race and labor regime, rather than shoring it up. In doing so, it adds to work by scholars of bor-ders and migrant labor who have documented spe-cific moments and contexts where immigration controls' excesses have contradicted the labor force needs of local capitals (cf. Hernández 2010; Sifuentez 2016). To understand this, I find it useful to think of local labor regimes spatially, as relatively stabilized moments in the ever-shifting articulation (S. Hall 1980, 1996) of multiple, power-laden socio-spatial relations (Massey 2013).

For Massey (2013), places are constituted through the intersection of “economic, political and cultural social relations, each full of power and with internal structures of domination and subordination” (154).

In addition to being power-laden and uneven, these relations are “stretched out over the planet at every different level, from the household to the local area to in the international” and constitute places at “articulated moments” (154) where these social rela-tions intersect in complex, asymmetrical relation-ships with one another. That is, particular places are shaped through political projects, economic shifts, and discourses that exceed the boundaries of the locale but connect with one another in sometimes unpredictable or contradictory ways, linking places with broader processes.

Local labor regimes are similarly shaped relation-ally, through the intersection of multiple processes and forces that link local geographies of production and reproduction across space. Global production networks and consumer demands (Smith et al. 2018); global trade, investment, and patterns of eco-nomic restructuring (Buckley 2012); and national and regional regulations for both work and immigra-tion (Sassen 1996) all exert force to shape labor regimes in specific locales. So, too, smaller scale pro-cesses and forces such as local labor laws and regula-tions (Torres et al. 2013), the capacity of local regulatory bodies to intervene (Gleeson 2012), and proximity to nodes of immigration enforcement (Guzmán and Medeiros 2020). These scholars have offered us the conceptual tools for understanding the complex, spatial relationship between local labor regimes, unfreedom, and migration. Together these shape the visibility and invisibility, marginalization, and conditions of work and home life for undocu-mented workers in various local labor regimes.

The concept of articulation is a specific and fruitful way of understanding the way that spatial forces and relations intersect not just additively, but through one another to shape a local labor regime. S. Hall (1980, 1996) drew on Althusser, Laclau, and Gramsci to con-ceptualize the intersections of social forces as articula-tions, which both bring together and express multiple determining structures of dominance. In particular, Hall attends to how race and racist ideologies articu-late with and through class relations in complex ways. Class relations and race relations (among other social relations) are structuring forces that work through each other in a social formation, shaping power rela-tions, cultural discourses, and also the production of space and place. As Lawson, Jarosz, and Bonds (2010) explained, “Articulation captures the dialectical rela-tions between places, particular forms of capitalaccumulation and their inseparability from social and cultural meanings and practices of race difference (and indeed other axes of difference)” (658). That is, in invoking Hall’s conceptualization of articulation, we invoke in particular an emphasis on how race or racism and other social relations of difference and power shape a social formation. In this article, I write of a specific race and labor regime to underline this point.

Articulations of social relations in space-time are best grasped concretely—that is, through the examination of real situations, and the untangling of the various relations that interconnect, producing a particular *conjuncture*, or moment in social reality where multiple forces and relations come together. Hart (2007) and other geographers have used Hall’s concept of articulation as a framework for analyzing historical and geographical conjunctures (see Lawson, Jarosz, and Bonds 2010; Chari 2017; Ekers, Kipfer, and Loftus 2020). A conjunctural analysis allows for insight into not only the relations and logics that shape a situation, but also the tensions, instabilities, and possibilities that arise concretely. S. Hall (1980) emphasized that the conjunctures that emerge through the articulation of multiple relations are “complex structure[s],” “in which things are related, as much through their differences as through their similarities” (325). An important implication of this is that conjunctures “are the product of an articulation of contradictions, not directly reduced to one another” (S. Hall 1980, 326). Massey (2013), too, identified that places, insofar as “they are constructed out of the juxtaposition, the intersection, the articulation, of multiple social relations,” “are frequently riven with internal tensions and conflicts” (137). This is important to understand because it reminds us that although the social and spatial relations (e.g., class, race, gender) that intersect in place are relations of power and dominance, we cannot know a priori how they will combine, align, or conflict with one another as they express themselves in place. This article attends especially to tensions and contradictions that emerge geographically in a local race and labor regime in the U.S. interior to argue that the relationship between labor control and immigration control is conjunctural.

## Methods

For this study, I conducted a rich case study of a particular place undergoing a shift in the intensity of immigration enforcement. To trace the multiscalar

complex of forces that together constitute a (changing) place, I juxtapose and weave together a broad set of sources. I draw on twenty-one key informant interviews, ethnographic field work, and archival analysis conducted in Autumn 2017, Winter 2018, Summer 2018, and Winter 2019. This is a subset of the research I conducted for my dissertation that examines detention and carceral immigration enforcement in Washington State, with extensive research conducted in and around the Northwest Detention Center (NWDC) in Tacoma.

I selected key informants who could provide expert information on the process of ICE enforcement in Pacific County and its impact from a wide variety of subject positions. I conducted interviews with county residents including three ICE targets, five family members of ICE targets, four employers, four agricultural industry experts, one local journalist, four school district representatives, four advocates, and two social service providers. In some cases, people held overlapping roles; for example, as an employer of migrant workers and a school district employee, or the family member of a target and a social service provider. I met my first informants from Pacific County outside the detention center, where they attended a rally in August 2017. These were advocates who were organizing material support for people who ICE had apprehended in Pacific County, and who were seeking ways to stem ICE activity, which they perceived to be harmful, in their county. They invited me to come out to their community to learn more. I met some of my informants through cascading introductions once I had entered the community. I approached others who I selected based on the kind of expertise they had, contacting them through phone calls and e-mails.

I conducted interviews in English and Spanish, according to the preference of the informant. Interviews lasted from thirty minutes to two hours and were recorded unless the participant declined, in which case I took extensive notes during the interview. All names were changed into pseudonyms, recordings were erased after transcription, and original names and contact information were destroyed in my records to protect the anonymity of the participants.

The ethnographic field work involved frequent visits to and stays in Pacific County, observation in an advocacy group’s community events, trainings and meetings regarding ICE activity in the county, touring a shellfish farm and the port where migrant workerswork, visiting with someone from Pacific County inside the immigration detention center, and attending court at the detention center. Through these experiences, I also had informal conversations with county residents, immigrants, labor and civil rights attorneys, county economic development experts, employers, local elected officials, and journalists, always informing them that I was conducting research.

I also gathered extensive information about ICE activity in Pacific County through a set of locally produced sources. A grassroots advocacy network provided me with minutes from a public meeting with ICE in the county and access to a public record request they made with the county sheriff's office on their communication with ICE. A local journalist has published two series of articles on ICE activity in the county, featuring interviews with people who were targeted, their family members, neighbors, friends, and employers. My own public request with ICE on its activity in the county has not been filled. I gathered population statistics from the Pacific County Economic Development Council, U.S. Census, and Washington Superintendent's office.

For additional context, I attended public meetings and events where immigrant workers discussed their labor struggles throughout Washington (including dairy workers in eastern Washington, berry pickers in northwestern Washington, and day laborers and construction workers in the Seattle region), and interviewed state-level labor, grower, and immigration advocates. Over the course of my field work, I also collected, catalogued, and read journalist, gray literature, activist, and government sources on changing immigration enforcement patterns and immigration policies under the Obama and Trump administrations.

I brought these sources together in an iterative process that unfolded over many months. Following an interview or field visit, I would transcribe as quickly as possible, rereading my interviews and field notes to identify political, economic, and social processes at work. I looked for other sources of information to triangulate information and would set goals for new sources and experts to approach. Once I reached saturation, I ended the research process.

## Migrant Workers in Pacific County

In Pacific County, shellfish and cranberry farming, shellfish processing, and tourism together make up the vast majority of nongovernment jobs. Many

Latinx workers in Pacific County work in the shell-fish industry, planting, harvesting and processing oysters, mussels, crabs, and clams. They also work on cranberry farms and in the restaurant kitchens and hotels. According to the 2010 U.S. Census, the overall population of the county hovers around 21,000; in 2010, 87.4 percent of residents were White and 8 percent were "Hispanic" or Latinx.³ Latinx adults, almost all from Mexico, began coming to the region about thirty years ago, and more and more began to settle, some coming up directly from Mexico and others after first living in California or in eastern Washington. Additionally, there are Latinx seasonal workers who regularly circulate through the county during peak shellfish processing season after the berry and tree fruit seasons in other areas of Washington end. The region was understood to be a relatively safe and quiet place to live and work without permits or official documentation—shellfish jobs especially paid a living wage, and Latinx residents encouraged their family members and friends to come join them in Pacific County. Informants shared with me how settlement patterns shifted over the years. In the 1990s and into the 2000s, Mexican-origin men circulated between Pacific County and various communities in Mexico, working for several years up north and then returning south to build homes for themselves and family members while rotating their position on a shellfish farm to a relative or friend. As border enforcement in the United States tightened, it became riskier and more expensive to circulate between the countries; people began to settle more permanently in the county with their spouses and began raising children in the United States in mixed-status families.

In this rural county, the year-round Latinx workforce and the cyclically returning seasonal migrant workforce are regarded as skilled and valuable laborers. The work that migrant workers do in the county is either the most physically demanding (shellfish cultivation and processing, cranberry cultivation), or the least well paid (hotel cleaning and restaurant kitchens).

Shellfish farming in Pacific County is a year-round occupation, mostly done by hand. Farmers work in a mixture of oyster, clam, and crab, producing both oyster-on-the-shell product for city restaurants and oyster bars, and shelled bulk product for canning. Farms range from family businesses running on tight margins with one or two employees tolarger shellfish businesses that have as many as 100 employees. Workers on the farms take jobs from driving boats and making shell bags to planting and harvesting the shellfish. Planting and harvesting shellfish happen with the tides, and thus the work-day can start early in the morning or late at night: The tides do not wait for the workers. Harvesters are generally men and are paid a piece rate. I was told that a fast worker can make $19 to $20 per hour, and that the rate was calculated to secure workers who will stick with shellfish as their primary or sole employment—employers do not want workers to miss a tide because they are prioritizing another job. Nevertheless, workers do pick up extra income through activities like informal clamming in the bay and doing odd jobs. Processors can be either men or women and are paid a piece rate, too, for shucking in facilities placed right at the port.

There are several pressures on the industry, including ocean acidification that has led to changes in the seeding process, the intrusion of eelgrass on the shellfish beds, and the intrusion of ghost shrimp (*Neotrypaea californiensis*), an invasive species. Food safety and ecological regulations shape the handling of the shellfish and add layers of administrative tasks. At the same time, demand for shellfish is high in the restaurant industry, leading to the expansion of the kinds of shellfish grown and the markets in which they can be sold.

Although the shellfish industry employs more of the Latinx workers in Pacific County, cranberry farming is another important source of agricultural employment for this population. This industry is less labor-intensive, the margins are narrower, and the farm owners are aging (in contrast, shellfish farm owners tend to be younger, raising families). Often the cranberry farmer will work another job and rely on a head worker, often an experienced Latinx worker, to run the farm full-time, with seasonal hands recruited for the harvest. These head workers are relied on over many years, and I was told can be considered “part of the family” (see Reid-Musson 2017 on paternal and familial discourses as applied to migrant farm workers and the restriction of their mobilities).

People who I spoke with in Pacific County pointed out how the Latinx workers were the backbone of the shellfish, cranberry, and tourism industries. A discourse circulates through the county about race and work: White workers would not do

these jobs, as they are lazy, hedonistic, and prefer to live off benefits like disability. Latinx workers are good, reliable, and hard-working employees. One shellfish farmer told me about a shellfish processing plant he sells to, and their experience trying to hire White locals:

> They advertised for workers, and they got quite a few people who signed up but they work a day or two and then turn in something to L&I [Labor & Industry, the state office for workers' compensation]. ... They claim they injured their back or got carpal tunnel, and then get a check. The money is just too easy now for all these people, so they just don't have to work. But the Mexican guys, they are not afraid of hard work and they love it.

The local agricultural extension officer (a university employee who works as a communicator and intermediary between agricultural researchers and farmers) had this to say about Latinx workers:

> I would say they are salt of the earth individuals and you are not going to find better workers or more valued employees or more honest and with integrity and they are just a joy and they do what no one else does and they do it with a song in their heart—in the middle of winter and they are out there singing and digging clams and I think, that is an amazing individual.

As S. Hall (1980) explained, race and racism do not have a single, universal, transhistorical character; yet, “racisms also dehistoricize—translating historically-specific structures into the timeless language of nature” (342). That is, “race” and racism operate as an interpellation of political and economic subjects for the capitalist market, naturalizing the hierarchical placement of certain social groups. Within a local race and labor regime, “essential” or “natural” characteristics of a certain, differential social group are used to explain their place, in relation to other social groups, in a segmented, racialized labor regime. In the case of Pacific County, the racial discourses comparing White and Latinx workers make sense of the value of these workers in the county’s economy and the dependence of employers on the Latinx workers in terms of the characteristics of these workers as a racial or ethnic group. The assessment of Latinx workers is positive (hard-working, honest, salt of the earth), but this does not indicate an antiracist stance or solidarity. The discourse about the good Latinx worker reflects a relation of belonging that Mezzadra and Neilson (2013) called “differentialinclusion”—the inclusion of migrant workers in a
subordinated labor status. Latinx workers’ inclusion

"It's heavy work, in the evening, at night. For example, 10, 11, 12, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 in the night. ... But at night, you are really tired, and many people who are American don't like to work. They say, "Let's go to work!" but they [the American workers] don't want to. So, if I go out there, they won't go, it's just that we [Latino workers] *have to do the work, the heaviest work*. It's just that we have to do the work. Because the money—nobody else will pay our bills, will pay for everything here. (Interview, Ronaldo, ICE target, italics added)

The Latinx workers in Pacific County work hard at the heaviest work and with discipline because their other option is poverty. Their labor is conditioned by the lack of a social safety net, the need to provide for family members near and far, and their vulnerability to immigration enforcement.

The positive narratives about Latinx workers are complicated by their marginal geography of social reproduction: their invisibility in the landscape and the inhabitance of temporary mobile homes. As is the case in other rural places in the United States (Licona and Maldonado 2014; Mares 2019), most undocumented Latinx farm workers in Pacific County live and have lived under the radar, invisible to many in the county despite the important work that they perform in the county's major industries. The marginal status is symbolically and materially linked with the kinds of housing that many Mexican and Latinx workers occupy: mobile homes or trailers. One trailer park out on the peninsula is colloquially known as Little Tijuana. One of my interview participants, Isabel, phoned in to her interview with me from her trailer, which was parked far from Pacific County. She and her husband, who is undocumented, had been living in Little Tijuana when ICE came to arrest him. Isabel, a White woman with deep roots in the county, had successfully made the argument to the ICE agents that her undocumented

husband needed to stay with her one more day to take her to a doctor appointment. When the officers backed off, Isabel and her husband did not wait to see if ICE would change their minds—they hooked up the trailer and left town. A school employee who had built close relationships among Spanish-speaking and Latinx parents in his district connected trailer living to the unbelonging of these community members: “I’ve heard from parents and community members that they prefer living in trailers out here because it’s temporary housing and they might get deported.” The race labor regime within the county, as structured by vulnerability to immigration enforcement and differential inclusion, is materially expressed in this marginalized geography of social reproduction, the mobile home.

In Pacific County, carceral immigration enforcement began to have an impact beyond the marginalization symbolized through their residence in mobile homes. This had consequences beyond the qualitative and quantitative regulation of labor in the district—it began to destabilize the labor regime.

## Carceral Immigration Enforcement in Pacific County

In mid-2016, ICE officers began to come to Pacific County with increasing regularity, and people began disappearing. Community members observed ICE agents in unmarked vehicles parked at the Port of the Peninsula and outside Okies, a supermarket. ICE began picking up community members and taking them to the detention center in Tacoma. Fear, confusion, and concern began to take hold. Rumors circulated that ICE was paying people to make reports, that rogue Department of Licensing officers called ICE after people came in to renew driver's licenses, and that they were not ICE agents at all but rather bounty hunters picking people up (interview, Isabel, family member of target; interview, Joel, school employee; interview, Cesar, family member of ICE target; interview, Marina, social service provider). An ICE officer explained to community members in a public meeting in May 2017 that “ICE is being told that no one who is without status is exempt from removals. But ICE does prioritize people who have committed crimes. ICE has targeted operations and usually know who they are looking for” (excerpt from meeting minutes, 4 May 2017). Community members understood from this that ICEwas informing them that they would focus on arresting those with a criminal background (interview, Elizabeth, advocate; interview, Betsy, school employee and employer). In one incident that community members identify as a turning point, though, ICE arrested a woman who was selling handmade piñatas over the Internet at the rendezvous with her customer. The implication was that an ICE officer had set up a sting by falsely soliciting the purchase to draw her out of her home and make the arrest (interview, Ruby, advocate; interview, Ronaldo, target). If people had believed up until this point that ICE was acting in good faith and targeting problematic or criminal elements, now it appeared as though ICE was predatory.

It later became clear that ICE officers assembled lists of targets through a process of database sleuthing and ground-truthing before eventually approaching their targets for arrest (Funk 2019). ICE officers would run the license plate numbers or names of people they identified through the Department of Licensing's Driver and Plate Search, a Web-based search tool. This helped identify the name (if it was unknown), address, and country of origin. From there, they had access to other databases, such as the Thomas Reuter's database CLEAR and a license plate recognition database from Vigilant Solutions, to gain more information about their targets. They did not use the county sheriff or local police departments as resources in finding targets, although there is ample evidence that the sheriff's office was informed at least some of the time when ICE came to town (Funk 2019).

The reasons this was happening in Pacific County remained unknown. The best answer that community members who I spent time with could come up with was that the Latinx workers in the community are low-hanging fruit for filling the detention beds in Tacoma (field notes, 2018). Then-Sheriff Scott Johnson affirmed this view when he told BBC reporters covering the story that he thought that Pacific County was being picked on because they were “easy targets” (Strasser 2018).

Interior immigration enforcement had expanded dramatically through the Bush and Obama presidencies, with the creation of U.S. ICE, an agency that is responsible for migrant detention and interior enforcement, in 2003 with a $3.3 billion budget. ICE's budget had expanded to $6.1 billion by 2016 (American Immigration Council 2020). Under

Obama, the expansion of interior enforcement relied heavily on increasing collaboration between local police forces and ICE under the Secure Communities and 287 g programs. In the Secure Communities program, when local police forces run a person's fingerprints through the FBI database, the information is automatically shared with the Department of Homeland Security, which then checks if the person is a noncitizen and has immigration status concerns. ICE can then issue a detainer, which is a request for the local law enforcement agency to hold the person in custody on ICE's behalf until an ICE officer can come take them into custody. In the 287 g program, local law enforcement officers are deputized as immigration enforcement officers and can perform some duties in enforcing federal immigration laws. These two programs represent a trend toward both interiorization and devolution of immigration enforcement to local jurisdictions, resulting in a highly uneven geography of immigration enforcement across the U.S. interior (Coleman and Kocher 2011; Varsanyi et al. 2012). The Pacific County sheriff's office had never made immigration enforcement a priority. In a context where interior immigration enforcement was largely happening through collaboration between ICE and local law enforcement, this made Pacific County a relatively safe place to live for undocumented workers.

The election of Donald Trump to the U.S. presidency in 2016, however, brought expanded resources for the existing interior enforcement infrastructure and changed enforcement priorities. Trump famously campaigned with a rhetoric of nativism and xenophobia, promising stronger border enforcement, and the expansion of interior immigration enforcement to rally support. In a campaign speech on 31 August 2016, Trump listed incidents where undocumented residents had murdered U.S. citizens, called for the protection of citizens, and then laid out his immigration plan. The speech specifically called for a tripling of the number of “ICE deportation officers,” declaring that “no one will be immune or exempt from enforcement. ... Anyone who has entered the United States illegally is subject to deportation” (Politico Staff 2016).

In addition to motivating a base of supporters through xenophobic and nationalist rhetoric, once elected, Trump relied on White nationalist political actors, such as Attorney General Jeff Sessions and advisor Steven Miller to shape policies and rules that would advance the project of racializing andexcluding noncitizens. After inauguration, the Trump administration rapidly took action to broaden enforcement priorities in the interior and to expand detention infrastructure. In the first week of his presidency, Trump issued Executive Order No. 13768, Enhancing Public Safety in the Interior of the United States. This order mandated an expansion of immigration arrests, the mass hiring of immigration officers, and a broadening of enforcement priorities. These new priorities would include those who were charged with a criminal offense or had committed acts that could be charged with a criminal offense (in the United States, crossing the border without authorization is a criminal offense), and those that an immigration officer judges to be a threat to public or national security. In the context of Executive Order 13768, which declared all undocumented people a threat to public safety and national security, this latter category included everyone without legal status.

ICE officers were freed up to pursue migrant workers, who the Obama administration had deemphasized in its enforcement priorities. Elizabeth Godfrey, who was Acting Director out of the Portland office at the time (under the Seattle ICE Area of Responsibility), remarked to a reporter that her office made targeting decisions based on who was findable, saying, “We still have a mandate. So where are we going to locate these people? At a place of employment or at their home or anywhere else we can find them” (quoted in Funk 2019). Meanwhile, ICE’s detention system had a surplus of detention capacity close by. The NWDC, a private medium-security prison in Tacoma, is ICE’s anchor facility in the region; its contract required that ICE pay for a guaranteed minimum of 1,181 beds filled per night, which is above the number of beds that interior arrests in the region can account for.⁴ This case is indicative of the relationality of detention space (see Ybarra 2021), and in particular, the spatial relationship between the detention center and communities of migrant workers who might fill these beds (see also Gilmore 2007).

Arrests in Pacific County typically happened in a parking lot—outside the supermarket, bank, or post office—but people were also followed and pulled over by federal agents making arrests on the road, outside their workplaces, and at their homes. The arrest process typically involved an approach by a pair of agents who called their target by their name, sometimes even a nickname. The agents would be

calm, and make the arrest, but reportedly sometimes made personal remarks, inquiring about the target’s children or other family members by name, which was unsettling (interview, Isabel, family of target; interview, Betsy, school employee and employer). One interview participant described to me how he requested that he be able to move his car prior to arrest, and this request was granted, and another was allowed to retrieve his medication (interview, Ronaldo, target; interview, Luis, target). The ICE officers would drive the arrested individual to Portland, Oregon, where they would fingerprint and process them; arrested individuals would get to make a brief phone call. From there, private guards would bring them to the NWDC in Tacoma, although some first had a brief stay at the NORCOR jail in The Dalles, Oregon. From there, family members worked to hire an attorney, assess if there was a way to get relief and fight out the case, or if it was a better idea to accept deportation right away. Those who felt their case had a chance of success sought bail so they could return to Pacific County and work while their case ran its course. People arrested in Pacific County tended to remain in detention from one to three months. A support network for ICE targets and their families facilitated legal assistance and bond assistance, which helped those in detention to get released or deported relatively quickly.

By April 2019, those who were keeping track had identified nearly 100 ICE arrests, just under 6 percent of the official census count for Latinx population in Pacific County (interview, Elizabeth, advocate).⁵ These arrests had a deep impact on the population, bringing into focus the relationality between the detention space in Tacoma and the spaces of production and social reproduction in Pacific County.

## Upheaval

Ruby, a White retiree who had become close with a Latinx family in her neighborhood, told me about the impact of carceral immigration enforcement. Her neighbor, Manuel, had been arrested at his trailer after twenty years of living and working in shellfish in the county. He was the crew chief at work, had three U.S. citizen children, drummed in a local band, and hosted volleyball games for a local Spanish-speaking league in his backyard. After ICE detained Manuel, his wife Marisol tried to make do, as this interview with Ruby makes clear:After he left, after he was picked up, she was out there every day [digging clams in the bay]. Because he wasn't earning any money, they had to survive somehow, so it didn't matter how windy it was, how rainy it was, how cold it was, I could see her out there. This poor woman, I mean she is about a size four if she is anything. And she would stand out there until the water was collecting around her knees, kneeling in the water in the mud to get those last clams out during the day. And it was just so hard. ... So, finally, probably two months after Manuel actually was flown down to the border and returned to Mexico, maybe two and a half months, Marisol decided it was time to pack up the kids and go.

Ruby helped Marisol to organize a sale of their household belongings and said goodbye to her neighbors.

Many among the noncitizen Latinx population of Pacific County began to leave due to deportation, and because family members followed those who ICE arrested and deported. People also left to avoid detention and deportation. Isabel, mentioned earlier, and her husband left the county for this reason. Isabel has ongoing major medical concerns, and she did not want to be separated from her husband while going through these, or to move to Mexico during her treatment. Cesar, another Latinx worker I interviewed, had gone into hiding within the county: When ICE arrested his brother, he called Cesar and warned him that ICE had inquired about him. Cesar quit his job with a shellfish producer and moved in with his mother-in-law. He and his wife did not want to leave the county as they had a newborn, and family in the area, but he dropped out of the labor force, at least for the time being.

Not everyone disappeared, though. Some, like Ronaldo, stayed to work after release from the detention center. Ronaldo's partner was already deported when ICE arrested and detained him. After release from detention, he received temporary work authorization, allowing him to keep working while his case makes its way slowly through nondetained immigration court. Although Ronaldo, a shellfish worker and father, might not win his case, his plan was to stay and earn money in Pacific County for as long as possible to be able to support his partner and children now in Mexico. He told me that Ocean Park, where he lived, was “a ghost town”:

> Before, all the time when you saw another Mexican person—and Americans, too, the same, but for example, a Mexican, you greet each other when you see each other in the store and all that. And now, it's rare. Now immigration is watching almost all the time,

parked all the time at Okies [the supermarket], they are parked there all the time in the morning. (Interview, Ronaldo, ICE target)

While carceral immigration enforcement reverberated through the homes and lives of Latinx workers, it also upset workplaces and labor relations in the county, destabilizing the county's race and labor regime. Ronaldo succinctly articulated this destabilization: “They will destroy themselves, wanting purely Americans.” The xenophobia expressed through expanded carceral immigration enforcement undermined the local race and labor regime, which was secured in part through a threat of such enforcement.

Despite a local average unemployment rate of 7.3 percent in the region during the period under study, employers seemed to associate the pressure from ICE on their employees with a labor shortage. As Anderson and Ruhs (2010) wrote, employer claims of “labor and skill shortages’ and migrants’ superior ‘work ethic’ must be critically interrogated” (23). Claims of a shortage are often made by employers seeking to recruit labor with a different set of expectations for wages, work conditions, and so on, than that of the employer (Anderson and Ruhs 2010, 29). In Pacific County, shellfish work requires unsocial work hours and laboring in the saltwater that citizen workers are less likely to be willing to do, insofar as they have other expectations and other options. Shellfish and cranberry growers had come to depend on the presence of migrant Latinx workers whose willingness to meet employer expectations is essentialized as a national or racial characteristic—even though it is conditioned by responsibility to family afar, poverty, and lack of social safety nets, as well as the threats of immigration enforcement; meanwhile, the actuality of deportation threatens that presence.

The local paper printed an anonymous statement from a farmer on 1 August 2017:

> Five. They've taken five of my guys. They took my foreman. He'd been working for me for 18 years. He came over the border three different times without documentation, but he had no crimes on his record. Even so ... he took his chances and so did I. ... Something's got to happen for the good of the agricultural community. I don't mean just here. I mean all over our country. We depend on our Mexican labor force. It they are all sent back, our agricultural community will collapse. (Stevens 2017)

One agricultural expert in the county said that it was a problem in his sector second only to the ghost shrimp pest problem, which he had made his life'swork to solve. The same agent commented to me, "There is no alternative to these workers. So, we need to figure out how to fix this."

This “labor shortage” is an extension of an already tightening labor market that growers have attributed to border control and that had already shifted how labor is recruited and maintained in Pacific County. Grower Frances described it like this:

> We had several people who came and went back and forth to Mexico over a ten-year period. And then that just stopped. I mean they just could not come and go like they had. And that was something that we really noticed ... and then in general I mean, there was always too just new people that would just come and ask for jobs and you don't—and, now you don't see that.

The group of people who came to work cyclically to support their families in Mexico stopped cycling as border controls hardened and its crossing became more expensive and dangerous. This pattern not only has affected shellfish and cranberry growers in Pacific County, but has created what one growers' association representative told me is a “ picker's market” in agricultural communities throughout Washington, where half of all farm workers are undocumented (interview, Matt, growers' advocate). With a slowed influx of undocumented agricultural workers from Mexico has come a more settled and aging undocumented workforce with changed expectations (Martin 2019). In Washington, this happened while agricultural jobs underwent an expansion. This labor market is also tightened in relation to higher paying jobs in construction in Washington's booming Seattle-Tacoma metropolitan area, which creates an alternative employment opportunity that absorbs undocumented workers (interview, Mike, growers' advocate).

Employers, agricultural industry experts, and economic development experts I interviewed reported a variety of responses to the ICE arrests and the labor shortage that it has exacerbated. Employers have contracted their businesses by reducing the amounts of shellfish that they seed, harvest, or process, or the number of days their restaurant is open. Daniel from the local Shellfish Growers' Association gave the example of an employer reducing their capacity by 50 percent, when they could only hire thirty workers instead of sixty. One crab producer closed their business after ICE arrested some undocumented workers and the rest stopped coming to work. A cranberry

farmer I spoke with was not able to bring in their harvest. Employers also loosened workplace discipline and standards to attempt to retain locals, as was the case for Frances, who was discussed at the beginning of this article. Daniel from the local Shellfish Growers' Association explained to me that in Willapa Bay, employers will attempt to “steal” shuckers from each other by offering bonuses for employees who bring more workers in (interview, Daniel, growers' advocate). Others attempted to recruit not only locals, but workers from California and Arizona. One of the long-term solutions that employers in Pacific County have tried out is the H2A program, a visa program for hiring temporary foreign workers that expanded dramatically in Washington under the Trump administration, but that has also seen heavy criticism for the way that the visa produces vulnerability to exploitation and unfreedom (Montange 2022).

## Discussion and Conclusion

In this article, I analyze the impact of immigration enforcement on labor, race, and social reproduction, in relation to intersecting social, political, and economic processes that shape place. The various political projects that are implemented through expanding interior immigration enforcement shape relations of labor and social reproduction in place, although in complex ways. The articulation of class relations with related racial discourses, shellfish and cranberry production, and the local environment in Pacific County formed a local race and labor regime that was then destabilized by a force from “outside”—increased carceral immigration enforcement. In this study, I analyze how class–race relations in a particular place, which had congealed into a particular labor regime, have articulated with the politicization and expansion of interior immigration enforcement.

In summary, after decades of relative security, undocumented workers in Pacific County began to come under the direct pressure of the U.S. interior immigration policing agency, ICE. As the community of marginalized undocumented workers became unsettled and pushed further into the margins, various people found themselves in deportation proceedings and others chose to leave to avoid detention and deportation. Scholarship on migration and labor has emphasized how immigration enforcementcomplements the needs of employers in disciplining and regulating the labor supply (De Genova 2002; Harrison and Lloyd 2012; Golash Boza 2016). Local race and labor regimes that regulate labor through noncitizenship, social marginality, immobilization, and the nonavailability of social safety nets can con- geal around the presence of racialized undocumented workers. Jonas (1996) pointed out, though, how local labor regimes can embody what he called “a contradiction between space and place” (325). That is, the political, economic, and social relations that stretch across and produce space can sometimes come into contradiction with the local sociospatial arrangements in a particular place, as these develop unevenly. This means that local labor regimes might become stabilized or destabilized through larger scale capital restructuring, shifts in global markets, or new federal or regional level (de)regulations related to partisan antagonism, class projects, geopolitics, and more. This can even happen through restructuring not directly related to labor or production, but instead to other elements of the sociospatial configu- ration. Within the particular articulation of relations and forces that made up the conjuncture that this article addresses, immigration enforcement began to destabilize the local race and labor regime, rather than stabilizing and regulating it.

The labor shortage and destabilized race and labor regime in Pacific County following ICE enforcement emerged through the articulation (S. Hall 1980; Hart 2007) of multiple place-specific material condi- tions and social relations: the handwork and difficult hours involved in shellfish cultivation and processing that makes this work unappealing; the raced and classed discourses about workers in Pacific County; narrow profit margins and environmental constraints within the shellfish and cranberry industries; employer reliance on skilled, experienced workers in these two industries; the domination of the market by small to medium-sized businesses that have relied on migrant workers; and the relative seclusion and protection of a rural community in which local law enforcement does not involve itself in immigration enforcement. These, in turn, articulate with regional and national trends in employment and immigration enforcement: the escalation of ICE activity and everyday interior bordering under the Trump admin- istration; the surplus in detention beds nearby in Tacoma, Washington; the availability of work for undocumented families elsewhere in the region; and

bordering practices that make circulatory migration less possible. These factors together constitute the effect that carceral immigration enforcement in Pacific County would have on labor and employ- ment in the county. Immigration enforcement that makes workers absent came into conflict with a race and labor regime premised on the presence of work- ers who are disciplined, marginal, and structur- ally vulnerable.

In other words, carceral immigration enforcement is a force within but exceeding labor relations, a vec- tor of unfreedom that stretches across space to artic- ulate with other sets of social and spatial relations in place. I echo McGrath and Strauss's (2016) call for an understanding of unfreedom in labor relations (as opposed to unfree labor relations). Instead of under- standing the relationship between unfreedom and work to reside in the characteristics of a workplace or inside an individual employer–worker relation, McGrath and Strauss called for an understanding of how forms of unfreedom, including those that exist elsewhere in the political economic context, inform the distributions of power between employer and worker within capitalist labor relations, constraining mobility and agency.

Here, I underline how the relationality between unfreedoms, mobilities, and power differentials within labor relations play out conjuncturally, within local contexts. That is, unfreedom in labor relations takes different shapes within specific historical and geographical conjunctures. This can include surpris- ing or contradictory configurations in specific local race and labor regimes, in specific places. Immigration enforcement in particular is a vector of unfreedom within labor relations that has multiple functions, and when these are spatialized they can come into contradiction with one another, as they articulate with other economic and social forces. For example, the expansion of carceral immigration enforcement plays an ideological and political func- tion in mobilizing a political base and advancing a conservative political agenda. At the same time, the confinement and constrained mobilities that carceral immigration enforcement introduces into the lives of migrant workers affect both labor relations and workers’ geographies of social reproduction.

In Pacific County, carceral immigration enforce- ment destabilized a local race labor regime that hinged on the presence of a disciplined, marginalized supply of migrant workers whose vulnerability andsusceptibility to exploitation is contingent on their immigration status. The ways that immigration enforcement articulates with labor regulation in this case is geographically and historically specific and complex. This analysis, however, does not amount to an empirical description of a unique case. Rather, in this article, I am working from a conceptualization of places and their local labor regimes as produced through multiple, power-laden, and intersecting processes. This is not a conception of place as something unique, but rather as a product of what Hart (2018) called “internal relations in which differentiation and particularity arise from *inter-dependencies*” (79). That is, the particularity of a place flows from the uneven spatial and social forces that intersect and articulate with one another in particular moments in time, and that also connect a locality to the production of space at a larger scale. The case analyzed in this article exemplifies something about the relationship between labor control and the unfreedoms associated with immigration control: It pulls apart the two so that we can see that carceral immigration enforcement does not have a necessary or unidirectional relationship with labor control, but rather a conjunctural one.
