# From Labor to Life in Vegetal Geography:
Exploring Ontological Marginalization and Critical
Externalities in the Plantationscape of a Global
South Country

Suranjan Majumder


*Department of Geography and Applied Geography, University of North Bengal, India*

In the ever-expanding era of urbanization where the dominance of the “urban human” increasingly defines global society, marginalized subaltern groups in remote rural spaces remain stark reminders of systemic inequities. Nowhere is this more evident than in India, a rising superpower whose rapid growth paradoxically coexists with deep-rooted societal divides. Within its vegetal geographies, such as the tea plantations of the Global South, landscapes of cultivation are not merely sites of production; they are living terrains where plants, labor, and social hierarchies intertwine, reinforcing the very inequities that urban progress claims to transcend. This study explores the ontological marginalization within the tea plantationscape of the Dooars region in India. Ontological marginalization is conceptualized as the “systematic exclusion of marginalized ways of being,” rooted in colonial histories and perpetuated by modern socioeconomic systems. The findings reveal a paradox of livelihood duality. On one hand, subsistence practices rooted in collective labor, seasonal variability in livelihoods, and reciprocal exchange continue to sustain households. On the other, these practices are increasingly enmeshed in exploitative wage economies that define the vegetal geography of tea gardens. In this landscape, the very plants that anchor livelihoods also structure social relations, tying human survival to cycles of cultivation that reproduce dependency and marginality. The research reveals how historical disruptions, contemporary structural inequalities, and gendered dimensions of exclusion have perpetuated intergenerational poverty and systemic vulnerabilities. It argues that addressing ontological marginalization demands rejecting colonial development logics and embracing justice-based frameworks that value diverse “ways of being, knowing, and surviving.” Key Words: developmental paradox, existential exclusion, indigenous livelihoods, ontological marginalization, vegetal geography.

The concept of ontological marginalization, where certain lives are rendered existentially subordinate and excluded from legitimate political and human recognition, finds its most influential articulation within biopolitical thought, which locates governance as inherently producing zones of neglect and exclusion. Foucault’s *Society Must Be Defended* lecture in 1976 instituted the “shift” from sovereign power to biopower: a form of governance that distinguishes between lives worth fostering and those that may be left to die, excluding certain groups at the level of their very being (Foucault 2003). This racialized administration of life constructs a “biopolitical hierarchy” in which marginalized populations, such as Indigenous and tribal

communities, are governed as risks and anomalies rather than full political subjects. For Foucault, such marginalization occurs when groups are defined as naturally dangerous, inferior, or internal enemies, rendered less fully human within societal logics (Foucault 2003; Murray 2003; C. Taylor 2011). Extending this, Agamben (1998) theorized *homo sacer* as “bare life,” stripped of political value yet subject to sovereign force, crystallizing the condition of ontological marginalization. Whereas Foucault emphasized historical shifts in power regimes, Agamben universalized the logic of exception, producing a more metaphysical and juridical theory of exclusion. Esposito ([2002] 2011), through his theory of *Immunitas*, reframed marginalization as a protective mechanism of the political community, negating “othered” life to preserve social coherence and enforcing exclusion through purification. Building on such critical engagements with the politics of life, Lemke (2011) provided the first systematic overview of biopolitics, offering a framework to understand how political power targets “life itself” as its explicit concern. Influenced by Foucault, Lemke examined how, in the modern era, politics increasingly aligns itself with biological laws, giving rise to forms of governance that regulate “bare life” and construct “biological citizenship.” He critiqued and expanded on the work of later theorists such as Giorgio Agamben, Hardt, and Negri, situating biopolitical mechanisms within the context of neoliberalism and bioscience. This orientation, however, carries problematic historical resonances, including eugenics and racism, underscoring the ambivalent and often exclusionary nature of biopolitical governance.

Positioned within the broader framework of ethnic marginality, biopolitical mechanisms take concrete form in the erasure of tribal and Indigenous ontologies in the Global South. Urbanization, as both demographic process and epistemic project, privileges the urban, mobile, rational citizen while casting remote-dwelling tribal communities into zones of invisibility and ontological liminality. In the current era of relentless urban expansion and the global ascendancy of the “urban human” paradigm, subaltern Indigenous and tribal groups remain confined to remote rural geographies, especially in the Global South. This urban-centric trajectory fosters a global sociospatial condition in which modernity, development, and citizenship are increasingly defined through urban logics (Parnell and Robinson 2012; Randolph and Storper 2023). India, as a rapidly ascending global power, exemplifies this paradox: Whereas its cities reflect global capital flows and technological growth, its tribal communities, such as the Oraon,¹ continue to face deep structural inequalities and exclusion from dominant sociopolitical frameworks (Nagendra et al. 2018). Within this developmental matrix, ontological

marginalization serves as a critical lens for examining how certain ways of being, knowing, and relating to place are systematically rendered illegible within hegemonic systems of knowledge and governance (Shah 2007; Soguk 2007; Duarte and Belarde-Lewis 2015).

Rooted in critical theory, existential philosophy, and decolonial thought, the concept underscores how dominant paradigms of modernity construct hierarchies of being that exclude tribal and Indigenous ontologies from what is deemed real, legitimate, or human. As Fanon (1952) argued, colonial systems imposed an ontological rupture that reduced the colonized to objects devoid of subjectivity. Building on this, Wynter (2003) and Maldonado-Torres (2007) showed how coloniality continues to structure the postcolonial world through a racialized “genre of Man” that situates Black, Indigenous, and other racialized communities outside full humanity. This resonates with Peterson’s (2015) insight on emplacement, wherein marginalized groups, despite economic deprivation, remain rooted in relational geographies, defying erasure through cultural endurance and place-based identity.

Recent scholarship shows that emplacement and relational personhood endure as acts of resistance and cultural survival. Far from vestiges of a premodern past, these alternative ontologies contest the extractive and homogenizing logics of capitalist urbanism. Addressing ontological marginalization thus demands a radical epistemic shift, redefining who and what is recognized as fully human within the frameworks of development, modernity, and the state.

India’s sociocultural fabric has been shaped by successive waves of coerced displacement, ecological adaptation, and political conquest. As Kosambi ([1956] 2008) noted, India’s history is not merely dynastic but a layered social history grounded in material conditions and the movement of peoples across diverse ecological zones. Colonial rule brought extensive displacement and dispossession to tribal communities of central India, particularly those from the Chotanagpur Plateau (Guha 1988; Damodaran 2006). To frame such uprooting as “migration” sanitizes historical violence and erases the lived experience of ontological marginalization, obscuring the coercive nature of displacement and the exploitative systems of indentured and corvée labor imposed on these communities (Guha 1988).The marginalization of tribal communities, including the Oraon, is illuminated when situated within the evolution of geographical thought on human–environment relations and sociospatial stratification. Classical determinists like Ratzel and Semple emphasized environmental factors in shaping societies, positing that ecological advantages facilitated sociopolitical dominance. Although critiqued for determinism, this framework informed later theories such as G. Taylor's (1936) *zones and strata model*, which argued that successive waves of settlement pushed earlier racial or ethnic groups into environmentally marginal regions. In India, this produced concentric sociocultural zones in which newer arrivals occupied resource-rich cores, while Indigenous populations were relegated to peripheral spaces. For instance, in the colonial tea plantations of North Bengal, settlement policies reserved resource-rich cores for elites, such as planters, while relegating migrated indentured labor groups to the peripheries, thereby creating enduring socioeconomic dependency. The continued vulnerability of tribal communities thus reflects a persistent spatial logic in which access to ecological resources and proximity to political power determine ontological positioning within the Indian sociospatial hierarchy (Guha 1988; Chattopadhyay 2014).

Building on this, Soja's (2010) concept of *spatial justice* enriches the analysis by revealing how power operates spatially to create “center” and “periphery,” both geographically and in social consciousness. This spatial displacement was not uniform or absolute, however. In this light, Bendapudi's (1958) notion of *negative areas*, remote, resource-rich landscapes often avoided by mainstream settlement yet inhabited by tribal groups, shows that ecological richness does not equate to political and social advantage. Thus, while Indigenous communities might initially retain control over resource-rich areas but remain confined to isolated and less productive zones, they continue to be marginalized in terms of political power and socioeconomic inclusion. For example, tribal communities in the resource-abundant forests of the Chotanagpur Plateau remained marginalized politically and economically despite controlling these *negative areas*. The Oraon tribe's historical habitat in the Chotanagpur Plateau and surrounding uplands exemplifies this dual marginalization: Ecologically resource-rich yet distant from political centers and socially displaced through centuries of agrarian expansion, colonial plantation economies, and postcolonial development-induced displacements perpetuated ontological marginalization.

Harvey's critique of spatial inequalities, rooted in capitalist relations, frames spatial inequality not as mere physical distance but as a structural product of uneven development and capitalist exploitation, continually reshaping the socioeconomic landscapes of communities like the Oraon. First displaced under colonial extractive regimes and later marginalized by postindependence development dilemmas, the Oraon experienced the colonial imposition of administrative grids, economic logics, and ideological chronologies, which fractured their ontological unity, what was called the “homogeneous, empty time of modernity” imposed on “plural, lived temporalities.” Thus, their marginalization is not only spatial, social, or economic, but also a biopolitical production of “life-as-risk,” “life-as-surplus,” and “life-as-outside.”

This article begins by contextualizing the research setting and outlining the ethnographic research design used to gather data from Oraon households. Subsequently, I² examine the concept of ontological marginalization across conceptual, narrative, and quantitative dimensions within the contexts of livelihood stress and diversification. Employing an ethnographic livelihood perspective, I analyze intersecting relationships beyond the immediate confines of village boundaries, focusing on contemporary dynamics rather than longitudinal trajectories (Clendenning 2024). Finally, I discuss how adopting a livelihood deprivation lens enhances data triangulation while presenting inherent challenges in portraying the complexities of marginalization.

## Dualism in Geography of Backwardness: Equity or (In) Equality

> When the government comes with promises of development, we are the ones who always lose our forests, land, and homes. Who is this development really for? (#R49, M, Grassmore TG)

The preceding quote captures the irony of pseudo-development, a phenomenon I observed during my doctoral field work in a rural tea garden in the Dooars region, at the foothills of the Darjeeling Himalaya. This apprehension, “progress” for some and “disposability” for others, emerged intensely through interviews and focus group discussions, which revealed among Oraon inhabitants a sense of exclusion not limited to poverty and neglect but extending to invisibility within developmentaldiscourse: a basic absence from contexts defining modern progress (Paradies 2020). What surfaced was not only the irony of pseudo-development, where “growth” narratives mask deepening precarity, but also the existential “weight of being” relegated to the periphery of normative imaginaries of citizenship, value, and development.

In India, where blunt wealth divides reinforce social stratification and economic expansion coexists with entrenched hierarchies, development programs claiming to foster inclusivity often reproduce structural inequalities. They displace and dismantle Indigenous forms of life, disrupt relational economies, and impose extractive logics on already vulnerable communities (Byrd et al. 2018; Paradies 2020; Clendenning 2024). This is absolute in the plantationscape³ of Dooars, where the Oraon, historically recruited into colonial tea plantations, continue to earn subsistence wages under labor regimes replicating colonial succession of command. This article examines how the Oraon persist within and respond to systemic marginalization. Following Peterson's (2015) framing of emplacement as ontological resilience, their continued inhabitation of tea gardens is recited not as inertia but as a relational survival strategy. They do not merely reside in these geographies; they exist through them, resisting displacement despite persistent hardship.

This study further contributes to debates on rural marginality and the geography of backwardness by foregrounding multiple deprivations, the simultaneous experience of economic, infrastructural, and symbolic exclusion. Geographical isolation compounds these vulnerabilities, producing chronic underfunding of public infrastructure and restricted access to state services (Woods 2009; Peterson 2015; Majumder 2025a). Yet, despite these constraints, communities remain anchored to place, reflecting not passivity but an ontological commitment to land, memory, and identity.

## Historical Foundations of Marginalization and Disruption of Traditional Equity Systems

Historically, Oraon social structures centered on collective labor and reciprocal exchange, fostering solidarity and equitable resource sharing. These systems were disrupted by colonial interventions and later neoliberal state policies, which formalized land

ownership and replaced shared governance with private landholding (Majumder 2025a). Colonial land revenue systems (Guha 1968, 1980) prioritized extraction and surplus production, displacing the Oraon from ancestral lands and weakening mutual labor exchange, thereby introducing new stratifications. Land alienation became widespread, pushing many to forest fringes, a legacy that continues today, intersecting with postcolonial development agendas, industrial expansion, and displacement.

As one elder from Abhihill Tea Garden lamented:

> The land that once sustained our ancestors now operates by others. Four generations ago, we were compelled to leave our ancestral homeland and were resettled here ... laboring in the tea garden. We have spent our entire lives working relentlessly, hoping for a better livelihood, decent living conditions, and enough food for our families. Nothing has truly changed. (#R56, M, Abhihill TG)

The preceding testimonial underscores the persistent dissonance between externally imposed development models and the community's intrinsic rights to livelihood, identity, and territorial belonging. Experiences of dispossession reveal the compounded effects of state-led interventions, capitalist market forces, and urban expansion, which not only reproduce intergroup inequalities but also erode intracommunity solidarities and traditional mechanisms of equity. Like many rural working-class groups, the Oraon remain distanced from political engagement; economic marginalization extends beyond material deprivation to political exclusion (Majumder 2025a, 2025b). Dependence on low-wage labor and landlessness limits their capacity for collective action, as survival needs override political participation. As Spivak (1988) noted, subaltern groups struggle to access platforms for expression, leaving their concerns absent from mainstream discourse. This aligns with Marx's assessment of “class domination,” where the ruling class wields the state to perpetuate economic and social inequities (Marx and Engels 2023), further restricting the Oraons' visibility in governance and policymaking (Guha 1980).

## Fragments of Ontological Marginalization

Modern governance in the Global South selectively supports certain populations while consigning others to systemic neglect and the slow violence of abandonment. For the Oraon community and other marginalized groups inhabiting forested landscapes, tea plantation belts, and tourism circuits, livelihoods are neither static nor merely adaptive; they arise at volatile intersections of traditional subsistence economies, informal labor regimes, and violent restructuring of land and resource access under late-capitalist expansion. Everyday survival becomes an act of navigating spaces of dispossession, contested territorialities, and abandonment.

Unlike many tribal communities in eastern and central India engaged in marginal farming or forest economies, the Oraon in this study are intrinsically pushed into the tea plantation economy, possessing little or no agricultural land and lacking viable livelihood diversification. Dependence on shadow plantation economies, combined with state neglect, perilous leasehold settlements, and landlessness, produces a form of entrapment where shifting capabilities is unattainable. This establishes marginality across generations, reproducing not only economic deprivation but also ontological exclusion, shaped by illicit arrangements,⁴ seasonality, and chronic unemployment. Ethnographic and quantitative mapping reveal multifunctional socioeconomic dynamism within this marginality, arising from processes of simultaneous “inclusive exclusion” and “exclusive inclusion,” where state-led development and market forces seize land, water, and labor while eroding social and cultural capital. In this paradox, inclusion within development frameworks becomes another form of exclusion (Figure 3).

Based on observations and narrative accounts integrated into a geographical perspective, four interconnected ontological dimensions emerge, shaped by historical processes of systemic exclusion, entrenched contemporary backwardness, and intersecting identities. First, the Oraon experience marginalization at the level of “being and recognition” (Deb 2022; Majumder 2025a), as their Scheduled Tribe identity is filtered through exclusionary discourses, erosion of indigenous knowledge systems, and enduring stereotypes that restrict mobility and erase cultural presence.

Second, their inability to secure food, shelter, and clean water reflects structural abandonment rather than mere economic failure; reliance on precarious, low-wage, seasonal labor positions them in chronic existential insecurity (Majumder 2025b).

Third, education remains largely inaccessible, constrained by poverty, poor infrastructure, and structural demands for child labor, representing not just an unfulfilled right but exclusion from systems that could expand futures (Banerjee 2015; Sarkar 2019).

## Epistemic Exclusion: Living as the “Peripheral”

In Mal subdivision, the Oraon tribe endures entrenched deprivation and prejudice, rooted in the convergence of historical marginalization and present socioeconomic challenges. Despite being among the most populous tribal communities in the region, the Oraon remain systematically excluded from mainstream development, perpetuating material and existential vulnerabilities. This exclusion undermines their capacity to sustain cultural heritage, safeguard traditional practices, and envision futures beyond poverty and discrimination (Majumder 2025a).

Colonial land alienation and state-led development projects have produced landlessness, displacement, and the erosion of subsistence livelihoods, forcing a

shift toward low-paying, insecure wage labor. This dependence on precarious employment limits their socioeconomic agency. The nexus of deprivation and prejudice transcends economic hardship, manifesting in persistent social discrimination and stigmatization. Such prejudice operates through restricted access to resources, ethnicized hierarchies that cast them as “inferior,” and the constraining of aspirations through deep-rooted stereotypes about the community’s worth.

During the field survey, it became evident that the Oraons’ deprivation and lack of opportunities are rooted in systemic neglect, perpetuating a cycle of poverty. The lack of infrastructure and effective government support compounds their marginalized status, leaving them trapped in poverty with limited opportunities for advancement. ## Denied Essentials: Living at the “Limits of Basic Needs”

The Oraon tribe in the Mal plantationscape endures precarious living conditions, reflected in the dilapidated state of housing (Figures 4 and 5). The lack of stable shelter reinforces structural vulnerability and perpetuates marginalization. Among tea garden workers, employer-provided houses are typically semipermanent, yet many fall into kaccha⁶ or semi-kaccha categories, and even semi-pucca⁷ dwellings often lack durability and basic amenities. Kaccha homes, built with mud and bamboo walls and tin, thatch, or clay-tiled roofs, are highly vulnerable to rain, leakage, and flooding.

Housing conditions reveal more than material scarcity; they embody ontological marginalization. One respondent (#R25, F, Sukhanibasti) described “decades of overlooking” that left “roofs leaking and walls collapsing” during rains. Another respondent (#R28, F, Grassmore TG) recalled a roof “partially repaired more than a decade ago” but never since, due to financial constraints. Rain disrupts sleep, rooms fill with water, and containers are placed under leaks to manage intrusion. Such dwellings, lacking permanence and formal ownership of homestead land, are not simply “homes in disrepair”; they materialize a history of being overlooked, rendering Oraon habitation politically and existentially invisible to the state as “permanent or worthy.”

The struggles of the Oraon community go beyond the visible decay of housing, permeating the very core of their daily existence through aggregate downgrading. Here, marginalization also saturates basic utilities, particularly water and sanitation (Figure 4). Nearly two-thirds of households rely on unprotected wells for drinking water, with some using untreated river water. Seasonal extremes intensify risks: Summers bring shortages as the water table declines; “monsoon rains contaminate open wells, leaving water dirty and dark with a foul stench” (#R3, M, Dakshin Khalpara). Such dependence is inherited—“What my parents did and what most people in my village do” (#R11, M, Jogesh Chandra TG)—and persists despite recurring health problems. Safer, protected sources installed by a few garden authorities remain inaccessible to those “residing in far-off areas” (#R3), reflecting how spatial exclusion deepens basic and everyday infrastructural neglect. Sanitation indicators are equally stark: Only one-fifth of households have enclosed facilities for exclusive family use, whereas over a quarter of respondents practice open defecation due to absent infrastructure. The normalization of such practices stems not from cultural preference but from infrastructural absence, an emblem of deeper ontological backwardness.

workers] as full citizens, entitled to dignity and care.” In his words, their suffering has become “routine, absorbed into the bureaucratic and social order,” marking material deprivation from the symbolic and institutional life of the state.

The ontological marginalization of the Oraon community becomes starkly evident when material deprivation intersects with deeper epistemic and existential exclusions. This goes beyond deficits in wealth and infrastructure, revealing how the community’s way of life, daily choices, and survival strategies are rendered invisible, devalued, and dismissed. A forty-six-year-old household head described his socioeconomic vulnerabilities as, “I felt trapped in a relentless cycle of economic and social anxiety. I was exhausted from trying to meet the immediate demands of my family, let alone imagining a better future for my children ... desperately wished to escape this trap and help lift my family members out of the darkness that had descended upon us” (#R17, M, Ranicherea TG).

## Devaluation of Livelihoods: Insecure Work, Wage, and Uncertainties

From a Marxist perspective, “labor is the fundamental source of value in society”; yet under capitalism, workers are alienated from their products; their contributions are undervalued and poorly compensated (Marx 2023). Among the Oraon, this is evident in informal labor such as tea garden work and sand quarrying. Their labor sustains industries and enriches employers, but they remain trapped in poverty, reflecting Marx’s theory of surplus value, where workers receive only a fraction of what they produce. Over half of Oraon households rely solely on tea garden wages, heightening vulnerability to poverty, food insecurity, and malnutrition. Most households require two or more members in garden work, yet wages, 232 Indian rupees per day (approximately US$2.62¹⁰) during the survey, later rising marginally to 250 Indian rupees per day (approximately US$2.85), remain far below a dignified standard of living. Formal workers average 180 to 210 workdays a year, and casual workers face irregular and seasonal employment.

In small gardens, women engage in informal leaf plucking for five to six Indian rupees per kilogram (approximately US$0.07), typically collecting forty-five to fifty kilograms per day, highlighting the precarious, informalized nature of female labor. A respondent's (#R36, M, Chalsa TG) claim, “It is just not possible to support a family on 200 to 230 Indian rupees a day (approximately US$2.28–2.62)”conveys a lived reality of undervalued work. His statement that “our livelihoods have become increasingly uncertain” points to the erosion of futurity, where even next-day planning is uncertain. Repeated but ignored appeals to the government and owners reveal a paradox of hypervisibility and era- sure: visible as workers, voters, and “poor tribal peo- ple” in political rhetoric, yet excluded from decision- making and development priorities (Golay and Hannan 2025). They exist as “bare life”—used but not valued, visible as labor yet invisible as persons.

The study found a clear generational divide in employment. Casual workers, often first-generation laborers from nonplantation backgrounds, lack expe- rience and access to long-term benefits, whereas per- manent workers are typically third-generation laborers in the formal plantation system, reflecting tea garden labor as intergenerational entrapment. Despite working over eight hours a day, including unpaid tasks beyond hazira hours, wages remain rigid and inadequate, tied strictly to fixed quotas. Plantation management justifies low pay through statutory provisions under the PLA, housing, health care, subsidized rations, and basic education, avail- able only to permanent workers and their depend- ents. Casual laborers, a significant share of the Oraon workforce, are excluded and receive only basic wages. During lean seasons or closures, many migrate for short-term informal work, returning through contractual intermediaries, further eroding job security.

This dispossession is economic, relational, politi- cal, and historical (Majumder 2025b). Oraon mar- ginalization emerges at the intersection of caste, ethnicity, and capitalist reorganization. Without land rights or subsistence farming, they remain trapped in exploitative labor regimes, stripped of autonomy and treated as disposable. The degradation of plantation work stems not from the labor itself but from the systemic devaluation of workers’ social worth; their labor sustains industries, yet their lives remain expendable. Historically, a rigid colonial hierarchy, managers, assistant managers, babus, mun- shis, dafadars, and at the lowest rung, the so-called coolies, structured plantation life (Banerjee 2015; Deb 2021). As one official noted, tea gardens run on a “shadow economy” that publicly projects order and productivity while concealing exploitative hierar- chies of class and labor. Even so, resistance has emerged: With the “collapse of managerial

oversight,” worker-led cooperatives have disrupted colonial disciplinary structures, emerging as counter- points to structural domination, asserting a fragile but potent reclamation of agency. The establishment of worker-led cooperatives has emerged as a form of economic and political resistance (Deb 2021). These cooperatives are typically initiated through collabo- rations between local worker collectives, and occa- sionally external advocacy groups, rather than being directly driven by management. Local trade unions sometimes participate in mobilizing members or pro- viding support during the formation stage, but their involvement is often inconsistent due to internal political divisions. Representation within these spaces is far from equitable (Bhowmik 2011). These acts, although often small in scale, represent attempts to reclaim dignity, assert collective agency, and challenge the very conditions that render work- ers simultaneously indispensable and disposable (Bhowmik 2011; Bhaduri 2018; Deb 2021; Wolf 2022). Regarding Oraon representation, although they form the backbone of the plantation workforce, their leadership within both trade unions and coop- eratives remains limited. They are often participants rather than agenda-setters, which constrains the transformative potential of these spaces of struggle. Yet these moments of resistance, whether in the form of wage petitions, strikes, or the establishment of cooperatives, unfold under the lasting shadow of deprivation, where the disgrace lies not in the work itself but in the devaluation of those whose labor sustains the industry while they themselves remain food insecure (Bhaduri 2018; Deb 2021).

The economic sustainability of tea gardens in North Bengal is in jeopardy. Once framed as a path to stability, plantation labor has become emblematic of distress. Although the government has periodi- cally introduced interim wage hikes, including the latest in June 2023 (Bag 2025), these remain far below subsistence levels and fail to account for infla- tion, rising living costs, and the historic undervalua- tion of plantation labor (Majumder 2025b). Low wages trap workers in cyclical poverty, a condition worsened by recent garden closures. As a community head noted, this is “pushing many toward starvation, precarious migration, or debt-bondage in distant urban peripheries.”

Ontological marginalization is thus being alive without time, where social and economic relations obstruct the secure construction of selfhood, future planning, and sustained well-being.

## Conclusion

To deepen understanding of recurring everyday noncompliance in achieving sustainable equity, this article presents a case study of the resettled Oraon community in northern West Bengal, India. Drawingon key findings, I foreground the concept of ontological marginalization to capture informal dynamics that perpetuate de facto inequalities, uneven wealth distribution, and gender contrasts. Ethnographic and mixed-methods research reveals the complex nature of this marginalization and the acute livelihood vulnerabilities faced by marginalized tribal populations. The findings highlight a profound disconnection between developmental ideologies and the lived experiences of a marginalized group.

Systemic exclusions faced by the Oraon are not incidental by-products of progress but are rooted in colonial legacies and contemporary neoliberal frameworks that sustain “hierarchies of being, existence, and value.” Their survival has been bound to the tea economy for over a century, spanning three to four generations, with the present households continuing this inherited dependence for at least the past two- and-a-half decades. The divergence between development as a state-imposed agenda and livelihood as a lived reality constitutes epistemic injustice, whereby dominant systems of wisdom and governance erase indigenous ontologies, reduce communities to passive subjects of intervention, and sever relational ties to land, labor, and culture. This study emphasizes the intersection of historical displacement, structural inequality, and present-day socioeconomic challenges. The findings reveal a paradox in which externally framed progressive interventions often intensify inequalities and disrupt traditional systems of equity, leaving communities like the Oraon further marginalized.

The Oraon community's livelihood duality, between traditional subsistence practices and wage labor in tea gardens, construction, and quarrying, reflects not mere economic diversification but ontological downgrading. Their ways of knowing, being, and living are devalued within a development paradigm that views them solely as labor, not as skill bearers or ecological stewards. As Spivak (1988) observed, the subaltern “cannot speak” not for lack of voice, but because dominant structures refuse to listen in their language. This marginalization is manifest in occupational instability and volatile livelihoods. Wage labor in tea gardens or Teesta River quarrying remains precarious, poorly paid, and without social protection, and migrant work, often a forced strategy for men, leaves women managing households through insecure, feminized labor. Viewing poor housing, inadequate health care,

deficient education, and unsafe drinking water as “normal” underdevelopment obscures their reality as material expressions of dual marginalization, both as a constitutionally recognized Scheduled Tribe and as a population bound for generations to an uncertain plantationscape.

The community's opportunities and inequities reveal systemic failures in which external interventions and political neglect deepen vulnerability. From an existential geographical perspective, development must move beyond reductive materialism toward ontological justice, recognition of historically denied ways of being, knowing, and living. Education, touted as a pathway to mobility, becomes another site of “inclusive exclusion,” as state quotas ignore linguistic and cultural disconnects. The absence of mother-tongue instruction accelerates intergenerational cultural loss, threatening language, memory, and identity.

Addressing the systemic inequities sustaining ontological marginalization requires a transformative policy agenda grounded in philosophical reflection and pragmatic justice. Key directions include (1) embracing ontological pluralism by recognizing indigenous epistemologies, practices, and systems of equity, prioritizing relational, community-led processes over extractive, top-down interventions; (2) implementing land redistribution with legal protections against displacement, focusing on marginalized, especially female-headed households; (3) facilitating livelihood diversification beyond monoculture economies through skill development, market access, and infrastructure for sustainable agriculture, forest-based livelihoods, and microenterprises that enhance security without eroding cultural ties; and (4) ensuring inclusive education by removing economic and spatial barriers through free, quality schooling, stipends, transportation, and culturally relevant curricula.

Although this study draws on diverse empirical data, its scope and representativeness are limited. The findings provide valuable localized insights but cannot fully capture the structural complexity of ontological marginalization and systemic exploitation. Time and logistical constraints limited field work depth, particularly on seasonal livelihood shifts and evolving vulnerabilities. These constraints point to the need for longitudinal, comparative ethno-graphic and socioeconomic research involving other tribal communities in the plantationscape facing similar livelihood disruptions and cultural dislocations.Ultimately, this article advocates for development as a virtuous praxis, recognizing marginalized lives as equally “real, vocal, and valuable” within broader sociopolitical and economic structures. Centering the Oraon community’s voices, it calls for policy frameworks that address both material deprivation and the deeper structural roots of exclusion, challenge entrenched hierarchies, and ensure genuine participation in decision-making. Only through such transformation can development move beyond tokenism toward justice, dignity, and meaningful inclusion for historically marginalized communities.

Enforcement remains inconsistent, however, with many plantations failing to meet even the basic statutory requirements.
9. In local Sadri language, residential quarters refers to *Girmik Lines* historically built in North Bengal's tea plantations to control and discipline workers. These cramped, poorly maintained clusters endure as visible legacies of plantation capitalism. Intergenerational dependence on plantation jobs, tied to residence in these lines, perpetuates economic vulnerability and entrenches structural marginalization within the tea economy.10. Equivalent in U.S. dollars is based on the midmarket exchange rate of Indian rupees (as of August 2025).
11. The Joint Forum, an apex body of about thirty tea trade unions in North Bengal, was formed to oppose the prolonged delay in fixing a minimum wage for tea workers (Bag 2025). Political divisions between ruling-party-aligned and opposition-affiliated unions have fragmented decision-making. Consequently, the decade-long wait for wage reform has demoralized workers, many of whom continued working during protests, reflecting eroded collective motivation and trust (Bag 2025; Sinha 2025).
12. The Minimum Wage Committee, comprising industry, union, and state representatives, was tasked with determining fair wages within six months (Bag 2025). Yet, after nearly a decade and around twenty meetings, no consensus emerged (Sinha 2025). Tea planters argue that fringe benefits, such as housing, health care, and rations should count as wages, justifying low cash payments, whereas unions demand higher wages based on inflation and economic indicators. The issue was later referred to the State Productivity Council on the labor minister's advice (Sinha 2025). These fringe benefits are often nominal, masking systemic exploitation under legal pretenses, while mandated welfare under the Plantation Labor Act remains widely neglected.
