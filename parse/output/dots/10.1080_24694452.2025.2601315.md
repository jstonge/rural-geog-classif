# Community-Based Participatory Geographic Information Systems: Informing a Long-Term Drinking Water Research Collaboration

Yolanda J. McDonald,<sup>a ID</sup> Marie A. Caiola,<sup>b ID</sup> Samantha Wyman,<sup>c ID</sup> Eleanor Crone,<sup>d ID</sup> Natalie N. Robbins,<sup>e ID</sup> Mariah D. Caballero,<sup>f ID</sup> and Shamayeta Bhattacharya<sup>g ID</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Human and Organizational Development Department, Vanderbilt University, USA; <sup>b</sup>Department of Environmental Health Sciences, Mailman School of Public Health, Columbia University, USA; <sup>c</sup>Department of Sociology, Harvard University, USA; <sup>d</sup>Program in Climate and Environmental Studies and Program in Public Policy Studies, College of Arts and Sciences, Vanderbilt University, USA; <sup>e</sup>Vanderbilt Institute for Spatial Research in the College of Arts and Sciences, Vanderbilt University, USA; <sup>f</sup>Department of Environmental Studies, Wellesley College, USA; <sup>g</sup>Department of Community Engagement and Leadership, Point Park University, USA

This study introduces the concept of community-based participatory geographic information systems (CBPGIS), a novel integration of community-based participatory research (CBPR) and participatory geographic information systems (PGIS) research methodologies. The CBPGIS approach is unique from CBPR and PGIS applications because it integrates human and organizational theory to build trust and sustain team dynamics. It also leverages local spatial knowledge from community stakeholders at every stage of the research process, emphasizing the need for coproduction of geospatial knowledge to inform products and deliverables that support long-term research collaboration. CBPGIS can foster equitable town-gown research collaborations by intentionally involving academic and community stakeholders to coproduce community-informed research priorities and solutions that could inform interventions and policy changes. To facilitate immersive student town-gown research experience, learning outcomes, and a sense of community belonging, we adapted the geospatial technology model by integrating community engagement and academic research competencies to develop the CBPGIS research competency model. Furthermore, using a statewide case study, we identified the critical need to coproduce a geospatial community water systems (CWS) service area boundary geodatabase to support drinking-water-related infrastructure and public health needs. We demonstrate how the CBPGIS approach was used to obtain spatial data, which are often scant or missing in a study area (e.g., rural areas). The CBPGIS approach ensured trust was established with local spatial knowledge stakeholders to obtain the necessary information to coproduce 97 percent of the CWS service area boundary data for populations of 3,300 or less; only nine U.S. states have comparable data at this level. Key Words: community-based participatory research, geographic information systems, immersive learning, participatory GIS, town-and-gown relations.

As public health concerns about drinking water quality in the United States have increased (Allaire, Wu, and Lall 2018; McDonald and Jones 2018), particularly in rural contexts (Marcillo and Krometis 2019; Mueller and Gasteyer 2021; McDonald 2023), a critical need for spatial representations of the public drinking water supply attributes (e.g., distribution area digitized as a polygon) has become glaringly evident. Conceptual Framework

Community-Based Participatory Research Methods

Community-engaged research methods originated
in the health sciences discipline and have effectively
addressed preexisting health inequities (Wallerstein
and Duran 2006, 2010; Sánchez et al. 2021).Community-engaged research methods integrate community perspectives at every stage of the research process, starting from initial consultations through testing and eventual implementation, thereby enhancing community outcomes (Sánchez et al. 2021). With community-engaged research methods, the level of engagement and leadership experienced by community partners exists on a nonlinear continuum, varying at each stage (i.e., inform, consult, involve, collaborate, and share leadership). Researchers argue that the community-based participatory research (CBPR) approach has the potential to be the most impactful and effective form of engaged research (Wallerstein et al. 2019; Sánchez et al. 2021). Moreover, Collins et al. (2018) posited that “CBPR provides a framework to equitably involve community members, researchers, and other stakeholders in the research process, recognizing and maximizing the importance of their diverse contributions” (885).

CBPR methods have increased in use as more organizations have recognized their importance. For instance, the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences has recognized the utility of CPBR methods as a framework to inform environmental health research (London et al. 2020). Although the CBPR approach is not a novel research method, it requires time to build trust and establish long-term reciprocity benefits for all stakeholders; it is best suited for long-term collaboration (Friedman Ross et al. 2010). As scholarship on CBPR praxis continues to grow, there is increasing recognition of the need to integrate and actively engage public and governmental entities in long-term research collaborations as a source of continuity and symbol of trust within communities (Wallerstein 2020). Although universities and academic institutions play a critical role in coproducing evidence-based scholarship and applied research, public and governmental entities are uniquely positioned to translate these findings, allowing for the development and implementation of policies and programs on behalf of their constituents with a goal of improving public health and population health (Wallerstein 2020).

The CBPR approach embraces a framework of shared and equitable leadership between academic and community partners, where all partners have the agency to drive change within the research process, as highlighted by a collaborative research coalition between the University of California, Berkeley and the Community Water Center, a nonprofit organization

dedicated to ensuring Californians have access to affordable potable water services (Balazs and Morello-Frosch 2013; Sánchez et al. 2021). This coalition identified a critical piece of missing data for their collaborative San Joaquin Valley Drinking Water Study: systems service area boundary data (Balazs and Morello-Frosch 2013). Although this coalition did employ stakeholder participation across the CBPR continuum to address both parties’ data needs and to jointly secure funding for the creation of geospatial systems service area boundary data set in California, the geospatial component of this study was not coproduced by the coalition participants (National Institutes of Health, Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Recovery, and Centers for Disease Control 2011; Balazs and Morello-Frosch 2013; Home Visiting Applied Research Collaborative 2018). Although town-gown (i.e., community and university) research can be one-sided, time-intensive, and might not result in a published study (Bruning, McGrew, and Cooper 2006), many universities and communities continue to work toward long-term collaborations (Harasta 2022). Opportunities for faculty and students to use a CBPR approach can transcend the classroom from a physical space on campus to the community by establishing community engagement goals and learning outcomes (Mohan 1995; Bednarz et al. 2008). These opportunities provide students with immersive learning experiences that deepen service-based learning (Bonsall, Harris, and Marczak 2002). Moreover, when coupled with GIS as an analytical and visualization tool to leverage local spatial knowledge, these immersive learning experiences can strengthen town-gown research (Kim 2018).

## Participatory Geographic Information Systems

Participatory mapping is a collaborative process that leads to the cocreation of maps by local communities with the involvement of supporting organizations, including governments, nongovernmental organizations (NGOs), universities, and other engaged stakeholders (Corbett 2009). Through a collaborative mapmaking approach, participants share spatial knowledge and experiences to realize shared aspirations, such as research outcomes that facilitate knowledge coproduction or a path toward more equitable policy change (Denwood, Huck, and Lindley 2022). Participatory mapping evolved from the reflexive efforts of GIS researchers and interested lay audiences to incorporate spatial knowledge to improve democracy in decision-making and include those whose voices were often excluded (Elwood 2006; Radil and Jiao 2016). Participatory mapping methods vary and can be broadly categorized into paper-based approaches like sketch and mental mapping, and digital methods, such as participatory GIS (PGIS) and public participation GIS (PPGIS).

PPGIS, as defined by Tulloch (2008), represents a field within GIScience that investigates how the general public uses GIS tools to participate in processes such as mapping and planning. A recent review of the use of PPGIS found that it is an increasingly relevant method, with 699 articles published on the application and dissemination between 1997 and 2023, and predominantly applied by researchers in the United States, Finland, and Australia (Mohamed 2025). PPGIS is often applied in urban settings within developed countries to inform land-use decisions, whereby participation is open to the general public, instead of being restricted to specific groups of stakeholders or decision makers (Brown and Kyttä 2014; Kahila-Tani, Kytta, and Geertman 2019; Bąkowska-Waldmann and Kaczmarek 2021). PGIS synthesizes participatory learning and action and geographic information technologies to leverage local spatial knowledge and democratize mapmaking (Corbett et al. 2006). It is an approach that integrates community knowledge into a GIS platform and is increasingly being applied to involve stakeholders in delineating local boundaries and prominent landmarks (Kwaku Kyem 2004; Dunn 2007). PGIS increases granular data collection applications and means for community engagement, thereby decreasing known errors in coarse-scale data and diversifying data contributors. PGIS emerged as a vital tool to empower communities in predominantly low-income countries to make more informed local decisions to support community advocacy (Corbett et al. 2006; Verplanke et al. 2016). PGIS's use of locals' spatial knowledge to create maps, process GIS data, visualize data, and interpret maps actively involves communities in making informed place-based decisions (Harris et al. 1995; Craig, Harris, and Weiner 2002; Jankowski 2009; Denwood, Huck, and Lindley 2022). Although PGIS emerged in low-income countries (Corbett et al. 2006), it can inform environmental decision-making, irrespective of a country's low- to high-income status. Importantly, PGIS emphasizes public participation in marginalized and rural communities, making it applicable in any country (Jankowski 2009).

PGIS can be a crucial tool to bridge the rural GIS gap by integrating communities into environmental decision-making, such as water resource management and conservation (Jankowski 2009). This approach is particularly well-suited for rural communities in high-income countries, as they often lack the financial resources and capacity to invest in GIS resources and staff. For example, despite the initial mistrust of state-provided data by community members in north-central Idaho, Jankowski (2009) found a community-involved PGIS approach to be effectively used by rural municipalities and local authorities. The local authorities worked with farmers, ranchers, and tribal communities to reform their drinking water practices and develop policies to establish mutually beneficial measures for safeguarding local drinking water, including designated drinking water protection zones (Jankowski 2009). By centering local knowledge and participation, PPGIS methods offer a powerful tool in advancing community-centered environmental planning. In Grand County, Colorado, Brown, Montag, and Lyon (2012) engaged local experts through PPGIS methods to identify and assess ecosystem services valued by the community. Similarly, Brown, Kelly, and Whitall (2014) combined input from randomly selected residents and National Forest volunteers in California's Sierra, Sequoia, and Inyo National Forests, using PPGIS methods to balance local knowledge and ecological expertise in guiding future forest management strategies. Although PGIS can foster the democratization of local maps and inform community-involved decision-making, it does not ensure better decisions (Blaschke 2004), nor does PPGIS.

In many instances, PPGIS and PGIS are conflated or referred to collectively as P/PGIS (Kar et al. 2016) or PPGIS/PGIS (Brown and Fagerholm 2015). Historically, PGIS relied on nondigital, culturally appropriate mapping methods; however, advances in mobile and Web-based technologies have increasingly blurred the technological boundaries between PGIS and PPGIS (Brown and Kyttä 2014). As PGIS becomes increasingly digital, there is a growing risk that the focus on spatial technologies will eclipse the participatory foundations that define the approach (Mukherjee 2015; Radil and Anderson 2019). Additionally, P/PGIS is predominantly implemented as a singular data collection event (i.e., project-based) rather than a sustained participatory process that supports continuous reflection, refinement, and decision-making across stages (Brown and Kyttä 2014). P/PGIShas not been fully utilized in research settings as a tool for stakeholder engagement across the full planning cycle (e.g., inform, consult, involve, collaborate, and share leadership), or for long-term research collaboration (see Kwaku Kyem 2004; McCall and Dunn 2012; Grady, Cáceres Cabana, and Malone 2024 for exceptions). Similarly, initiatives led by NGOs tend to be shaped by short-term project objectives and donor expectations, which limit deeper and sustained participatory practices (Sieber 2006). More broadly, government funding to support research studies that poststudy solely evaluate participatory processes, with or without GIS components, has historically been minimal (Sewell and Phillips 1979).

Brown and Kyttä (2018) argued that meaningful social change requires technological components that ultimately serve the participatory process rather than dominate. This necessitates a shift toward more community-based participatory GIS (CBPGIS) approaches. The relative ease of creating maps and the short-term, project-based nature of PGIS might lead to an emphasis on the visualization of a *space*, highlighting a physical location defined by geographic and/or administrative boundaries, rather than a *place*, which refers to a geographical location imbued with meaning or value by an individual or community (Rezeg, Roche, and Eveno 2025). In other words, without sustained and meaningful participation throughout the entire CBPR continuum, the human significance and relational context embedded in the geospatial deliverables risk being overlooked or lost. Together, these concerns underscore the rationale for deepening both PGIS practices by introducing the complementary framework of CBPR, supporting more sustained, community-driven engagement, and more rigorous evaluation of participatory outcomes. The integration of CBPR and PGIS offers the potential to strengthen long-term research collaborations and enhance decision-making grounded in spatial data.

## Community-Based Participatory Geographic Information Systems

This article describes the contextual need, design, and implementation of community-based participatory GIS research (CBPGIS), an integration of CBPR and PGIS. CBPGIS addresses some of the limitations of using GIS as a technical spatial tool in PGIS applications, which might not integrate geospatial methodology to inform decision-making. The overreliance on

using GIS as a software tool rather than a cohesive set of geospatial capabilities to understand the nuances of place and space (Ndzabandzaba 2020) and the use of GIS techniques, such as georeferencing and digitization, can be hindered by insufficient or nonexistent publicly available data (e.g., PFAS-producing facility locations and safety net services, such as uninsured medical care services, community food pantries, and low-cost, quality adult care centers) compounded by the lack of a relationship with community members to obtain data and understand local spatial knowledge (Brown and Kyttä 2014, 2018; Denwood, Huck, and Lindley 2022). When geospatial software tools are used without a critical understanding of their limitations, power imbalances could be inadvertently produced, undermining the emancipatory potential of participatory mapping (Elwood 2006). The coproduction of outcomes and deliverables is weakened through emphasizing the “GIS” over the “P,” potentially impeding the intended implementation or change. Without intentional participatory processes centered on reciprocity (e.g., gives and gets throughout the collaboration), focused on coproduction, GIS technology risks overshadowing the very perspectives and knowledge essential to a successful and meaningful intervention. Concomitantly, when these data are constructed through artificial intelligence, such as machine learning methods, mistrust can arise if the community was not involved in the process (King et al. 2025). On the other hand, participatory mapping approaches, such as PGIS and PPGIS, are predominantly limited to the coproduction of needed spatial data for a specific research project often neglecting the long-term collaborative effort between the community and the research team (Dunn 2007; Brown and Kyttä 2014; Radil and Anderson 2019). As demonstrated by prior research, PGIS methods have proven particularly valuable in rural contexts, bridging the utility of GIS technology with the local spatial knowledge and needs of a community (Jankowski 2009; Rambaldi 2010; Brown et al. 2012, 2014; Brown and Kyttä 2018, 2014). Although PGIS is highly useful in these contexts, it is often limited to discrete projects and participation is confined to one stage (e.g., data collection, sketch mapping, or plotting on a digital map) rather than integrated throughout the entire continuum of the CBPR approach (Brown and Kyttä 2014). The conceptual nested pyramid model of public engagement integrates qualitative and quantitative methods and best practices fromcitizen science, digital and spatial humanities, and communications to offer a sustainable framework for advancing P/PGIS beyond entry-level public participation toward contributing and leading roles, through spatial narratives and leveraging the ability of deep maps to reveal temporal and spatial relationships and generate multiperspective, multistakeholder questions (Ridge, Lafreniere, and Nesbit 2013; Juip et al. 2025). Although the nested pyramid model of public engagement is nascent and has been demonstrated through the lens of a study, it has the potential to counter critiques that PGIS is ill-suited for participatory applications due to its technical nature and reliance on experts to process publicly sourced data (Dunn 2007; Radil and Anderson 2019). Given the potential for GIS to supersede or overshadow participatory processes, the often short-term, project-based nature of PGIS, and the added value of involving governmental entities across the CBPR continuum, we propose a CBPGIS approach. We posit that CBPGIS is most apt for long-term collaboration, whereby needed spatial data are surprisingly scant or missing for parts of a study area (e.g., public health data in a rural context) and require local spatial knowledge expert involvement at every stage of the spatial community-based participatory research continuum. We emphasize that the CBPGIS approach benefits from the praxis of human and organizational development theory. To build trust and establish a shared vision to guide our long-term research collaboration, we integrated Tuckman's (1965) model of team development to guide ongoing team development and Bolman and Deal's (1991) four-frame model to inform the structural, human resources, political, and symbolic needs of stakeholders throughout the CBPR continuum. CBPGIS is a method to sustain long-term collaboration rooted in the principle of reciprocity and serves as the foundation to ensure community-engaged efforts remain focused on achieving intended outcomes. To frame CBPGIS's utility to facilitate an immersive student instruction experience, learning outcomes, and a sense of community belonging, we adapted the geospatial technology model by integrating community engagement and academic research competencies to develop the CBPGIS research competency model.

We use a case study to demonstrate the utility of CBPGIS to establish a long-term research collaboration centered on ensuring a safe drinking water supply

in a high-income country within a rural context. We aimed to demonstrate the need to integrate a key outcome of PGIS, which is the democratization of maps (Pánek 2016), while addressing methodological gaps in participatory mapping identified by Denwood, Huck, and Lindley (2022). For example, Denwood, Huck, and Lindley (2022) emphasized the need for increased transparency regarding the research's intentions and purpose, as well as rigorous documentation of the methodology, replicability, and transferability of the approach. An essential component of ensuring safe drinking water is spatial data (i.e., community water system boundary polygons), which are often missing in rural contexts, irrespective of low-, middle-, or high-income country settings. Often, statewide mapping projects omit systems serving populations of fewer than 3,300. Moreover, modeling systems serving fewer than 100 persons is even more challenging (U.S. EPA 2024c) because creating an accurate service area boundary of this size is highly unlikely without local spatial knowledge, which cannot be obtained due to a lack of relationships with these systems (Gonsenhauser et al. 2020). Omitting small and very small systems is problematic because they are disproportionately vulnerable to drinking water violations (McDonald and Jones 2018; Statman-Weil, Nanus, and Wilkinson 2020). The lack of system service area boundary data limits a system's ability to develop comprehensive assessments of needs and challenges, create protective policies to improve drinking water quality, and apply for local to federal funding programs (Acquah and Allaire 2023). Beyond this case study, missing data in rural areas are common, which hinders public health decision-making and interventions that could contribute to improved population health outcomes (Schuurman, Leight, and Berube 2008; Bekemeier et al. 2019; Bekemeier et al. 2023).

## Study Area

Tennessee receives their drinking water supply from a system, 9 percent from private wells, and 1 percent from springs and other sources (Tennessee Department of Health [TDH] 2024). A large percentage of systems in Tennessee are very small ($n = 43$) and small ($n = 129$), comprising 39 percent of all systems (see Supplemental Material, Figure S.1); these systems comprise 44 percent of systems in rural communities serving a total area of 2,828.70 square miles, as compared to 32 percent in urban communities, serving a total area of 669.96 square miles (see Figure 1; VU DWJL 2025b).

To demonstrate how CBPGIS (see Figures 2 and 3) can facilitate long-term collaboration, democratize coproduction of maps, and support meaningful community-engaged and GIS instruction and student-centered learning outcomes, we use a drinking water case study. Our study represents a six-year (2018–2023) collaboration, including 100 percent virtual research during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic. Specifically, we use CBPGIS to guide the creation of the Tennessee Public Water System Service Areas data viewer. The mapping application is a publicly available spatial data viewer that enables Tennesseans to search for system-level drinking water quality information, such as water sampling results and violations and enforcement actions, and system-level characteristics, such as the spatial extent and source water (TDEC 2024). This study required the creation of a systems service area boundary data set, similar to Balazs and Ray's (2014) study of drinking water disparities in the San Joaquin Valley, California, and Marcillo and Krometis's (2019) study of disproportionate drinking water compliance in rural Virginia. A prior effort to create shapefiles of

Tennessee systems service area boundary data set yielded approximately 15 percent of system boundaries of varying quality over ten years (TDH, “A Prior Effort to Create Shapefiles of Tennessee CWSs SAB,” 19 January 2018).

We integrated local spatial knowledge and coproduction and dissemination of spatial knowledge throughout the spatial CBPR continuum (see Figure 2). Because our stakeholder group formed on the premise of a long-term research collaboration on statewide drinking water, rather than a study, we embedded Bolman and Deal's (1991) four-frame model (i.e., structural, human resources, political, and symbolic) into the inform and consult components of the spatial CBPR continuum. Given the reality that organizational change would occur during our collaboration, the use of the four frames guided our shared decision-making and enabled us to collectively lean into empathy, better understanding the challenges and perspectives of stakeholder organizations to guide and sustain our collaboration. Bolman and Deal's (1991) structural frame, which emphasizes organizational structure and its associated systems and procedures, guided us in contextualizing our shared roles and research goals. The human resources frame, which focuses on the relationships between organizations and people, informed our coproduction of an immersive town and gown learning

experience with students. The political frame helped us reflect on the power and conflict within and between our organizations, enabling us to align our resources and interests most effectively to sustain our long-term collaboration. The symbolic frame provided us with a lens to discuss and value the cultural norms and interdisciplinary theories and methodologies within and among stakeholder groups (Bolman and Deal 1991).

## Spatial Community-Based Participatory Research

*Security.* This study used a six-stage nonlinear GIS project workflow (i.e., security, data collection, digitization, standardization, verification, and database management) CBPGIS approach (see Figure 2). A data use agreement was executed to store study files on a secure server environment (Advanced Computing Center for Research and Education 2024). In collaboration with the state agency with oversight of these data, we agreed on which stakeholders had authorized access to study data on the secure server and, of those who had access, who was authorized to read, write, or execute data files. Security was essential because the maps were also used for other studies; therefore, they contained a range of attribute data. Some maps containedextensive critical drinking water infrastructure, such as locations of storage tanks, water pumps, and water mains. In contrast, other maps were sketched by hand; the only distinguished feature was a boundary on a base map.

**Data Collection.** Several methods were used to obtain data (i.e., maps), such as field data collection, electronic transfer of data to a secure server, e-mail and phone outreach to systems, and weekly stand-up meetings. Throughout the data collection process, we used a chain of custody protocol to safeguard all sensitive geospatial and attribute information collected and to ensure data returned to the originating source. Data collection required extensive study status and quality assurance reporting, which evolved during the study to meet stakeholder needs. All stakeholders participated in the field collection of maps. The initial effort consisted of us leveraging scheduled statewide TDEC field office (N = 8) visits from May through July 2018 to pilot the Tennessee Public Water Operator Survey. Throughout the study, TDEC, TDH, and TAUD stakeholders collected maps from systems throughout the state and forwarded them to VU DWJL's primary physical location. Maps in an electronic format (PDF, TIFF, shapefiles, geodatabase) were forwarded to stakeholders, usually resulting from an e-mail and phone outreach to systems, or after the annual TAUD conference, when we provided an update to systems on the status of the study. For most of 2020 through the summer of 2022, we struggled to obtain the remaining 10 to 12 percent of systems maps in any format. We implemented an e-mail and phone outreach campaign to systems in the summer of 2022 to expedite the remaining data collection and determine if a new map was required based on system boundary verification and refinement. In the fall of 2023, we and TAUD representatives with deep local spatial knowledge of systems service area boundary data started a weekly meeting based on the daily stand-up meeting (DSM) theory (Stray, Sjøberg, and Dybå 2016). The weekly meeting, dubbed the “Monday morning stand-up virtual meeting” supported our final map collection efforts. DSMs are a common practice in the technology industry whereby coworkers consistently (e.g., frequency is based on study status) meet briefly (i.e., typically fifteen minutes or less) to share study progress and discuss and solve problems; meetings are often standing, and communication is facilitated in round-robin format to avoid

top-down communication (Stray, Sjøberg, and Dybå 2016). The weekly interval provided TAUD time to directly contact TDEC field offices and system personnel, often in person, for maps required to complete the study. Throughout the study, all stakeholders contributed to efforts to create maps (N = 23) from scratch using system personnel's local spatial knowledge. The methods varied; for example, meeting with a system representative via Zoom and talking through system service area boundary via Google Maps, sending system personnel operators large-format paper maps of the county to sketch out a service area boundary, and for very small systems, sketching out blocks of the street name and landmarks on a sheet of paper or whiteboard Zoom option.

**Digitization.** We established standard operating procedures for digitizing and georeferencing maps (see Supplemental Material; VU DWJL 2025a). Because there was enormous variation in map content (e.g., pipes or no pipes, legend or no legend), we decided to digitize all map content. In the last year of the study, however, stakeholders prioritized systems service area boundary and focused solely on digitizing boundary details. GIS training, such as theory on geographic coordinate systems and projections and the use of geospatial software, was developed by stakeholder subject matter experts for our research assistants. During the study, geospatial training materials were revised based on maps received with a formalized annual update. By the study's second year, research assistants refined geospatial training materials with oversight and review by stakeholders.

**Database and Management.** The stakeholder group moved through the spatial CBPR approach (see Figure 2) and agreed that two file geodatabases were required based on varying stakeholder needs: a state plane geodatabase and a Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) geodatabase. The need to adapt map design and database requirements as part of collaborative study dynamics aligns with previous findings because maps can serve different purposes among stakeholders (Bartling et al. 2022; Grady, Cáceres Cabana, and Malone 2024). TDEC predominantly uses a state plane coordinate system (i.e., Tennessee FIPS 4100); therefore, a state plane geodatabase would facilitate integration into the TDEC Division of Water Resources GIS Open Data Hub (TDEC 2024). A limitation of state plane, however, is the inability to conduct regional distance andarea-preserving spatial analysis using ArcGIS. Given this limitation, we proposed to create a UTM Zone 16 N geodatabase, which all stakeholders agreed to. We now have more flexibility given other studies that are part of the overall long-term research collaboration.

**Map and Attribute Standardization.** During the study's second year, the research assistants, in a collaborative effort, audited common map features to inform the stakeholder standardization workshop. As previously noted, an audit of the maps revealed inconsistent map features. Variation in attribute table fields and symbology was also substantial. Stakeholders discussed what was required in the attribute table given the different anticipated uses of these data, such as linking tabular drinking water violation data to feature class data to inform TDEC and TDH personnel of the extent of the system service area boundary in the event of a chemical spill or other hazard that could affect drinking water quality.

**Verification.** A robust nonlinear verification stage was required to ensure data quality; stakeholders agreed to allocate resources (i.e., time and funding) to ensure the study's verification process was a cornerstone. In particular, we found the “involve” and “collaborate” stages of the spatial CBPR continuum (see Figure 3) revealed CBPGIS outcomes we anticipated, such as uneven response to data requests across eight different TDEC field offices, lack of consistency in format or quality of data, or lack thereof, and the time constraints of personnel with local

spatial knowledge required to assist with creation and digitization of maps. These realities were likely to manifest as data quality issues, such as overlapping systems service area boundary. The stakeholders created an overlap analysis protocol organized by the TDEC field office to document overlaps, assess potential reasons for the overlap, and develop steps to resolve them.

To ensure a structured immersive student learning experience, we adapted the geospatial technology competency model (U.S. Department of Labor, Employment and Training Administration 2025, n.d.) to inform skills, knowledge, and abilities essential to educational, research, and professional learning outcomes associated with the CBPGIS research approach, a tailored CBPGIS research competency model (see Figure 3). The adapted model focuses on competencies needed to prepare individuals for geospatial industry jobs, whereas the CBPGIS research competency model was designed to emphasize geospatial thinking and competencies needed to train the next generation of applied geospatial researchers. We integrated community engagement and emphasized team-based science and academic research competencies that support the learning process and development and refinement of skills, knowledge, and abilities for students who pursue interdisciplinary careers that use geospatial approaches.

The model is operationalized as a pyramid with five tiers, whereby the foundational tiers represent soft skills (e.g., personal effectiveness and team-based science) and academic competencies (i.e.,

The personal effectiveness competencies (i.e., base of the model) reflect the foundational skills and values required to be successful in myriad occupations and industries. As a user moves up the model, the competencies become research-specific. In both models, Tier 1 is the personal effectiveness competency tier, which is the base of the pyramid because it lays the fundamental groundwork for students to be successful in academic research, as well as industry and nonprofit environments. Tier 2 emphasizes the importance of students learning to work as part of a team, a foundational component of team-based science, which draws on collaborative research and problem-solving across disciplines. Tier 3 represents the foundational spatial thinking competencies, emphasizing the critical role of geospatial theory to complement geospatial methodologies. In this study, the theory supporting the use of geographic coordinate systems and projections was crucial in ensuring the accuracy of georeferencing and digitization, which are essential components of a research assistant's toolbox for geospatial projects.

Next, Tier 4 encompasses the community engagement competencies that provide students with immersive learning opportunities to actively participate and lead meaningful collaborations with stakeholders and collectively advance the codeveloped drinking water long-term collaboration in Tennessee. Students are trained in the principles of reciprocity and CBPR to experience firsthand the value of building trusted and reciprocal relationships with stakeholders to obtain local spatial knowledge that can be used to coproduce spatial data of interest to all parties. Finally, Tier 5, the top of the pyramid, represents skills that are critical to disseminating research, including presenting and copresenting findings at industry, community-based, and academic conferences, as well as academic writing in the form of capstone project theses, dissertations, and peer-reviewed articles.

The development of the model was an organic student-led process that provided structure to instruction and relevant student-centered learning outcomes. The model reflects the skills, knowledge, and abilities from the student's point of view and immersive learning experience, exemplifying the contributions and learning outcomes of students motivated to reimagine a preexisting framework to include team-based science and community engagement competencies. The utility and integrity of the

new model created structure, served as a framework for GIS instruction, and built spatial research capacity among stakeholders.

## Results

Ninety-eight percent (n=438) of the systems in Tennessee have a spatialized service area boundary (see Figure 4). The CBPGIS research approach (see Figure 2) enabled stakeholders to remain engaged throughout this study. We collected paper-form maps (n=178) and established relationships with TDEC field office personnel and system operators throughout the state to support the broader Tennessee drinking water long-term research collaboration. During 2018, we received 111 maps, which were transferred electronically; sixty-seven were from earlier TDH mapping efforts, which required review to ensure system service area boundary data were current. During 2019, TDEC and TDH collected eighty-six maps from systems during their regularly scheduled statewide visits and brought them back to us for scanning.

## Discussion

Previous research has shown that long-term research collaboration requires time to develop the group norms that facilitate the necessary trust to establish an environment that ensures equitable involvement and comfort in sharing knowledge and constructive feedback within and between myriad stakeholders to support collaborative research focused on the well-being of a community (Wallerstein 2020). Although thesecharacteristics of collaboration (i.e., short-term or long-term) are known, team dynamics and communication norms are typically discussed during a project launch workshop, and the structural, human resources, political, and symbolic frames of stakeholders are often engaged at a surface level or not at all beyond a give-get group exercise. The CBPGIS approach integrates Tuckman's (1965) model of team development and Bolman and Deal's (1991) four-frame model because long-term research collaborations require a well-functioning team and shared understanding of potential constraints and how and why stakeholders view challenges differently. Additionally, stakeholder representatives will change; therefore, teams will be in different stages of forming, storming, norming, and performing, which is manageable when theory informs team dynamics. Applying Bolman and Deal's four-frame model is intended to ensure stakeholders understand the team's positionality and provide a theoretical framework to leverage when structural, human resources, political, or symbolic conditions change. The CBPGIS approach aligns with Wallerstein's (2020) finding that there is an increasing need for universities to collaborate with public and state-level governmental entities through applied research to obtain data for evidence-based scholarship and translate findings to inform the development and implementation of policies and programs on behalf of all to improve community well-being in rural and urban areas. Additionally, providing students with immersive real-world research opportunities that enable them to put into practice theory and methods learned in the classroom to address community needs is a critical component of lifelong community engagement that educators strive to instill as part of the university experience. The CBPGIS approach (see Figure 2) and research competency model (see Figure 3) are holistic approaches to long-term research collaboration and pedagogy that have facilitated inter- and intragovernment agencies, non-profits, and university research efforts (see Figure 8).

Although we used a case study to illustrate the utility of the CBPGIS approach and research competency model, it is one of many studies predicated on coproducing a community-water systems service area boundary data set, particularly for rural communities. In Tennessee, very small and small systems comprised 39 percent of all active systems and incurred 54 percent of all drinking water violations (VU DWJL 2025b). The significance of having these data for very

small and small systems has numerous benefits, such as (1) democratizing mapmaking for all system sizes; (2) supplying geospatial data for systems, which can be an important aspect of applying for funding to improve technical, managerial, and financial capacity; and (3) enabling system-level assessment of drinking water quality based on the racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic status of consumers served by these systems, given their disproportionate exposure to drinking water violations (McDonald and Jones 2018; Statman-Weil, Nanus, and Wilkinson 2020). The integration of CBPR and Bolman and Deal's four-frame model into PGIS enabled stakeholders to be more attuned to meeting system personnel where they were, regarding map status (i.e., from a geodatabase to no map), and to address overlapping and divergent stakeholder needs. For example, we were better able to appreciate the different types of local spatial knowledge and variance in GIS knowledge and resources, as well as understand drinking water needs well beyond the creation of system boundaries, such as concerns about recruitment and retention of water-sector workforce (Paxton, Anderson, and McDonald 2022).

Another compelling outcome was the opportunity for GIS instruction beyond the classroom, which was focused on engagement and collaboration with stakeholders and local spatial knowledge experts to democra- tize geospatial map creation for very small and small systems. To build CBPGIS research competency, we used a student-centered approach. Students emphasized the interdependence of spatial thinking and commu- nity engagement because local spatial knowledge, which was primarily needed in rural areas, required building trust among water sector employees who had local spatial knowledge. Additionally, many universi- ties, including those of the authors, lack a traditional geography department or degree (Dangermond and Goodchild 2020; Hess and Weihe 2020). Therefore, applying a CBPGIS approach can provide a meaningful applied research approach for students to acquire GIS theory and skills through nontraditional classroom instruction. Several of the students enrolled in the sin- gle GIS course offered by Vanderbilt University com- plemented their learning in applied settings, such as attending numerous workshops held by the university, interacting with university subject matter experts, and consulting with stakeholders and local spatial knowl- edge experts. Another important finding was the appli- cability of foundational geospatial techniques (e.g., georeferencing and digitization) in an applied research setting; we experienced how using all of the research competency tiers of CBPGIS (see Figure 3) improved the quality and quantity of data.

Working alongside stakeholders, research assistants contributed to GIS project management and data visualization (e.g., cartography and WebGIS applica- tions) in a real-world setting. For example, a colead research assistant shared that she “gained confidence with geospatial technology, communicating with gov- ernment stakeholders, and creatively problem-solving within an interdisciplinary team.” The study also piqued research assistants’ interest in learning spatial analysis to support the broader long-term lab research collaboration and strengthen academic development by incorporating spatial components into capstone projects, undergraduate honors theses, graduate theses and dissertations, internships with federal agencies and nonprofit organizations, academic and water- sector industry conferences, a water-sector magazine, and peer-reviewed journals. For example, a study

research assistant described her experience on the study as “learning so much about the importance of GIS in informing drinking water policy as well as the immense statewide collaboration that this kind of community-based research requires,” lessons she applied to her policy intern position with the Center for Climate and Energy Solutions. One unexpected finding was the extent to which student involvement gave students a sense of community belonging. The lead research assistant stated, “As an out-of-state stu- dent, I am sincerely thankful for the opportunity to collaborate with individuals at both state and water system levels throughout this study, as it has truly made Tennessee feel like home.”

## Applicability of Foundational Geospatial Techniques in Applied Research

While we demonstrate the use of the CBPGIS approach through the coproduction of the community systems service area boundary study, it is notable that during the six-year period, we applied the CBPGIS approach to several drinking water research studies that leveraged the foundational boundary data and sup- ported our long-term collaborative approach to contrib- ute to water justice for all Tennesseans (see Figure 8).

A key takeaway was the utility of systems service area boundary data before the entire state boundaries were constructed (i.e., partial state data were useful). For example, TDEC led a study to evaluate water availability and interconnection in the Old Hickory Watershed. TDH is assessing Legionnaires’ disease associated with community water systems and group housing conditions. The boundaries supported a student-driven research study to evaluate the accessi- bility of nonprofit dental services in communities based on therapeutic and nontherapeutic fluoride levels in the public drinking water supply.

To assess the risk of per- and polyfluoroalkyl chemi- cals (i.e., specifically those regulated by the Final PFAS National Primary Drinking Water Regulation on 10 April 2024) in the state’s public drinking water supply, all stakeholders are collaborating on a federally funded grant to develop a machine learning model to predict the presence and concentration of PFAS chemicals associated with anthropogenic, physical environment, and social variables (see Figure 9).
