SELLERS AND NONSELLERS OF LAND FOR NEW RURAL HOUSING*

Lizbeth A. Pyle

West Virginia University

Landowners can choose either to sell or to hold their land in areas with active rural land markets. Sellers and nonsellers are both important because their decisions shape patterns of land use. Analysis of mail survey data from 286 landowners in the Rochester, MN SMSA isolates four characteristics that distinguish sellers from nonsellers: occupation, size of landholding, recent acquisition of land, and interest in selling land. Nonsellers are often farmers with a large landholding and no interest in selling. More information is needed about their non-economic motives for holding land. Key Words: rural landownership, rural land markets, land use change.

During the 1970s many urban workers moved beyond the suburbs to gain the amenities of country living. Rural areas within SMSAs and adjacent corridors of growth provided a focus for much of the recent population redistribution [6, 12]. Numerous studies have documented the land use changes associated with this intra-regional migration [e.g., 7, 20]. Ownership changes precede nearly all land use changes, but landownership studies have been less common. Popper has suggested that landownership research often is avoided because of the difficulty in assembling landownership data from uncompiled sources, sidestepped because it can be politically sensitive, or simply underestimated [14]. The entire development process centers on individual landowners whose decisions are crucial because the development process would be halted without them.

Landowners in a market characterized by high demand for residential homesites sort themselves into two groups: those who offer land for sale and those who withhold their property from the market. For simplicity's sake, these two groups are referred to as sellers and nonsellers, respectively. My purpose is to isolate distinguishing characteristics of the two groups of landowners, using a case study in the Rochester, MN SMSA. Socioeconomic and landholding comparisons of sellers and nonsellers are made to more fully understand how these two important groups of landowners differ. The strength of case studies lies in their documentation of the observable phenomena which theories try to explain. Case studies serve equally well both in validating existing models and in identifying relevant variables for use in new models.

## Land Markets and Landowners

The dramatic increase in rural residential construction during the 1970s meant that the market for rural homesites increased its share of all market transactions in rural areas. Three trends have emerged that characterize today's rural land markets: break-up of larger holdings into smaller ownership units, an abundance of nontraditional rural landowners, and escalated land prices [9].

Rural homeowners do not require the large landholdings that farmers need, so many landholders have split their property into smaller parcels for sale to individuals seeking a home in the country. While farmers may still claim title to the largest landholdings, their number has dwindled in comparison to individual property owners who simply live on the land or use it for recreation. Higher prices are typically associated with smaller, more intensively used land. The cost of rural homesites is no exception; the prices of these parcels exceed those for most other rural land uses.

The demand exerted by people seeking homesites seems irresistible to many landowners holding property on the rural margins of metropolitan areas. They willingly supply land to the market at a price agreeable to both buyers and sellers. Other landowners opt to withhold their land from the market, weathering the pressure to sell.

* I wish to acknowledge the assistance of the Center for Urban and Regional Affairs, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, which supported this research.The landowners who do not sell exert a demand of their own, termed their "reservation demand," a measure of the landowner's desire to continue to hold the property [8, 16]. The reservation price for the land is its imputed value when continued in its current condition of ownership and use. If an offer from a prospective buyer exceeds this price, the landowner might consider selling the land. Many landowners are strongly inclined to keep their land despite the high demand exerted by potential buyers; these landowners have high reservation prices. The supply of land to the market is said to be "sluggish" because of the reluctance of many landowners to sell [3, 9].

Ottensmann's study relating urban sprawl and land values emphasized the importance of landowners' expectations on their decision-making, which in turn affects the pattern of development [13]. Popular wisdom holds that individuals who do not sell their land at present are waiting for a higher price in the future. Bahl's theoretical account of this decision process demonstrated that landowners refuse to sell their property as long as its rate of return exceeds its opportunity cost [1].

Neither Ottensmann nor Bahl incorporated empirical data on individual landowners in their studies. Both concluded that we need more information about individual landowners and the basis for their decisions to sell or hold land despite the fact that they excused themselves from such an investigation. Some economists have been reluctant to collect primary data from individuals because such survey data may include subjective variables and the results may not fit neatly into the mathematical equations of economic models [1].

Few studies have systematically collected data at the level of the individual landowner. A 1978 federal government survey, the only comprehensive study of its kind since 1947, provided general information about the socioeconomic and property characteristics of rural landowners [2, 10]. The survey's broad scope emphasized the interests of its sponsoring agency, the U.S. Department of Agriculture, so it was not particularly useful to policy makers in areas of rural residential development.

Two more recent studies have contributed to this lean body of literature, but each emphasized a particular category of landowners rather than comparing sellers and nonsellers in a systematic way. Healy and Short studied land market participants in several areas experiencing different types of rural development [9]. Their work emphasized characteristics of new, nontraditional owners or buyers, rather than sellers. The work by Brown et al. emphasized current landowners in urban fringe land markets [3]. They interviewed a much smaller sample of sellers but did not directly compare sellers and current landowners. This paper systematically compares personal and property characteristics of sellers and nonsellers in one Minnesota county.

## The Rochester Study

Changes in population and rural land use within the Rochester, MN SMSA are representative of nationwide trends in the continuing process of urban decentralization [12]. Rochester serves as a regional center for southeastern Minnesota and has a population of 58,000 [18]. It is centrally located in Olmsted County, which is the single county in this SMSA. The Mayo Clinic medical facility and a branch plant of IBM employ most urban workers. Agricultural activities in the surrounding rural areas of Olmsted County emphasize feed grains and dairying. During the 1970s the movement of Rochester's urban workers into the surrounding agricultural areas escalated. The market for land for rural homesites has been active in recent decades so the area provided a good location for detailed study.

Uncompiled records maintained by state and local government offices provide useful information about landowners and land use. Locally-filed copies of Minnesota Department of Revenue forms identified the sellers. The state requires that the value of real estate be certified when property is transferred; the document also identifies the buyer and seller and specifies the buyer's intended use of the land.

Rural residential landholdings may be one of a number of uniformly-sized parcels in a residential subdivision; these landholdings are called platted lots. Residential parcels of land that are not part of a platted residential subdivision are referred to as unplatted. Nonresidential parcels of rural land are also considered unplatted land holdings. No sales of platted lots in rural subdivisions were included in this study because the conversion of that land to residential use essentially takes place when it is platted, not when it is sold to an individual. Farmenlargement sales were also excluded because no major land use shifts accompanied these ownership changes. The sales used to identify sellers involved either (1) unplatted land (i.e., not part of a rural subdivision) sold for residential use or (2) land reportedly in agricultural use that had not been purchased to enlarge an existing farm. The latter type of sale signaled the possibility for hobby farms, country estates, or speculative ventures because few people today purchase land to begin full-time, commercial farming operations. Between January 1975 and December 1979, 634 sales in rural areas of the SMSA met the criteria established for study.

Some real estate records contained the address of the seller; other addresses were obtained using telephone or commercial rural directories. Three hundred seventy-one sellers (59 percent of the 634 sales) whose current mailing addresses could be located were contacted. Some of these sellers accounted for more than one of the sales, but individuals were surveyed only once. Employees of the local government office where the records were housed generally proved unable to provide information that could be used to locate the remaining sellers.

Nonsellers' names and addresses came from the current tax roll of rural landowners in the SMSA; a 20-percent stratified random sample was taken. The sample was stratified by townships to parallel the distribution of sellers. The total of 225 nonsellers was selected randomly within townships. A comparison of names contained in the two groups eliminated the possibility that any landowner would be contacted more than once.

The mail survey instrument followed the methodology developed by Dillman [5]. It included questions about the current landholdings, land market activity, and socioeconomic characteristics of sellers and nonsellers. Landholding and socioeconomic variables such as size of landholdings or owner's age and occupation traditionally have been emphasized in landownership studies [3, 10]. Questions about land market activity provided important information because the sale of land during the five-year period served as the dependent variable in the data analysis.

After as many as three contacts, returns from the landowners yielded a 60-percent response rate. The rate reflects adjustments made for such problems as recent deaths and undeliverable letters. Usable responses from 150 sellers and 136 nonsellers were included. The land market study by Brown et al. yielded a similar response rate [3]. The difficulty of tracing sellers and getting those who have been traced to respond usually results in the lower response rates. These survey data nevertheless provide a useful beginning point despite their nonrandom nature.

Contingency table analysis offered an appropriate method to test for significant differences between sellers and nonsellers because most of the variables were nominally scaled. Chi-square served as the test statistic.

## Results and Discussion

If landowners can choose to supply land to or withhold it from the market, what distinguishes sellers from holders? Are nonsellers simply landowners waiting for the right time to sell? Relatively few characteristics distinguish the two groups from one another (Tables 1 and 2). The few differences found suggested that sellers and nonsellers hold their land for very different reasons.

### Personal Characteristics

Little variation existed between sellers and nonsellers when comparing age, employment status, and education (Table 1). Few young landowners hold property in rural areas. Less than one-third of the sellers and an equally small proportion of nonsellers were younger than 50. A slightly higher proportion of sellers older than 65 matched the edge that this group also held over nonsellers who had retired. Well over one-half of both groups still worked full- or part-time. A majority of sellers (52 percent) and nonsellers (63 percent) had not pursued their education formally beyond high school. Equal proportions of the two groups had completed college (8 percent), but a few more sellers (12 percent) than nonsellers (8 percent) had been to graduate school. The landowners chose distinctively different careers despite their generally similar educational backgrounds.

Farmers occasionally do sell land, although their reasons for selling frequently depart from the notion that high taxes drive them off their land [3, 15]. Yet sellers worked in a "white collar" capacity almost equally as often as they did on the farm. They found employment in professional, technical, or managerial positions nearly twice as often as nonsellers. The medical facilities associated with Rochester's Mayo Clinic and a branch plant of IBM employed many in this group. Many sellers fit the stereotype of "speculators" [11]. They held property for reasons other than its current economic use.

## Landholding Characteristics

Twenty-nine percent of the landowners who sold rural property between 1975 and 1979 no longer owned any land in rural areas of the Rochester SMSA; many had moved into the city. Sellers who still owned rural land generally had more modestly-sized landholdings than nonsellers (Table 2). Over one-half of the sellers who still owned land in rural areas owned less than 40 acres, and they accounted for 90 percent of all landowners in that size class.

Most sellers did not farm their land and a decline in parcel size commonly has accompanied shifts from agricultural to nonproductive uses of land. Healy and Short recognized parcelization, or the splitting of larger holdings into smaller tracts, as one of the major trends in rural land markets [9]. Conversely, the largest proportion of nonsellers owned more than 160 acres. More landowners in this group farmed and their productive uses of land required larger holdings than those of sellers.

The national survey of landownership found that 88 percent of all rural landowners held fewer than 50 acres [10]. Both groups of landowners in the study area owned more land than rural landowners nationwide, because farming continues to be a mainstay in the area's economy. The conversion of rural land to residential use near Rochester escalated during the 1970s in the county, but much of its outlying rural area remains in larger agricultural parcels.

Despite differences in the sizes of their landholdings, other characteristics of sellers' and nonsellers' landholdings were similar. Both groups had owned rural land for an average of 20 years, but more sellers than nonsellers acquired their rural land during the 1970s. Over half of both the sellers (58 percent) and the nonsellers (63 percent) held their property in joint ownership; most other landowners owned their land individually. Sellers, by definition, have been active in the local land market. They sold at least once between 1975 and 1979, and 28 percent of them had sold two or more parcels. Sellers did not limit their activity to land disposal as nearly 25 percent also had purchased additional rural property during that time period. More than one-third (37 percent) who "recently acquired land" during this period had acquired two or more parcels.

Most sellers bought the additional acreage for rural residences, other future development, or farm enlargement purposes. Individuals who have bought or sold land recently are familiar with the local market and more likely to know about additional land for sale. They often take advantage of "inside" information and speculate in the land market, i.e., they buy land beyond their immediate needs with the intention of selling it at a higher price.

Some sellers who farmed may have sold part of their property with good frontage for a housing site and replaced it with another parcel equally suited to agricultural use. The additional parcel might not be adjacent to the farmer's principal landholding, but an increasingly smaller proportion of farmers today enjoy the benefits of operating a farm composed of completely contiguous landholdings [17].

Fewer nonsellers (15 percent) added to their landholding and most who did acquire a single parcel. Nearly half of them (48 percent) bought land to expand their farming operation. Farm enlargement sales traditionally dominate rural land market transactions until the wave of development rolls over urban fringe areas.

A smaller group (19 percent) of nonsellers whose landholdings increased in size during the study period received the property as a gift or through inheritance. Intergenerational transfers of land have always been important in the farming community. Fewer sellers farmed and none had inherited land.

The active role of sellers in the local land market apparently continued beyond the five-year period discussed here. Many said that they "may sell land" again during the next five-year period; 42 percent saw such plans on their horizons. Individuals simply investing in land, rather than making current economic use of it, typically hold the property for a shorter period of time.

Sellers' plans provided a sharp contrast to those of the nonsellers. Less than one-quarter of the latter group expected to sell any of their rural land within the next five-year period. Most nonsellers had not sought the opportunity to sell and did not expect their high reservation demand to waver in the future. Both the research and the planning communities have overlooked these persistent landowners and their strong non-economic motives for holding rural land that may seem "ripe" for development. Sentimental family ties to the land or the prestige of landownership have been suggested as reasons for holding property [1, 8], but no attempt has been made to document the full nature and extent of reservation demand.Conclusions

The decisions of sellers and nonsellers are important because they shape patterns of land
use. Theoretical works have emphasized the importance of landowner expectations on the
decision to sell land, but they have failed to incorporate landowner characteristics in a model
of decision-making. This empirical investigation in the Rochester SMSA identified four charac-
teristics of sellers and nonsellers that should be included in such a model.

Three of the four relevant variables relate to their landholdings—acreage held, recent ac-
quisitions of land, and an interest in selling in the near future. Only one involved a personal
characteristic—the landowner's occupation. Sellers as a group seem less committed to their
land because fewer farm for a living. They see land more as a consumptive good to be bought
and sold than as a productive good to be retained. More sellers than nonsellers have been
acquiring new land and planning future sales; on balance, they currently hold smaller acreages
than nonsellers.

Farmers predominated among nonsellers in the study area, but not all nonsellers keep their land because of its productive capacity. A simplistic economic reason for retaining land rests on expectations for its future sale value. This motive provides sufficient explanation for short-term landowners who hold land in anticipation of its sale at a higher price. Long-term landowners also hold property in areas of rapid growth; their reservation demand exceeded the demand exerted by potential buyers in the past and will likely do so in the future.

Further study of nonsellers is needed, especially those who have held their land for longer
periods despite the land's potential for sale. Their desire to own rural land runs deeper than its
simple economic value. The institution of private property is solidly engrained in our culture
and warrants further attention. More information on the non-economic motives for holding
land is needed before an adequate model of reservation demand can be developed.

A potentially fruitful direction for related research points to the analysis of public records.
They have been underutilized as a source of information on landownership and land use. Craig
explored the potential for using building permits to monitor development [4], but the reams of
forms filed away in state and local government offices generally have been overlooked. The
pattern of land sales should be monitored more closely because ownership transfers usually
signal land use changes, especially near the fringe of urban development. Such information
could be incorporated into local land information systems. Conducting feasibility studies and
pilot demonstration projects on the use of public records are useful projects.

EMPLOYMENT DATA AND THE CLASSIFICATION OF
URBAN SETTLEMENTS

Gordon F. Mulligan and Richard W. Reeves

University of Arizona

Classifications of urban settlements have traditionally used total employment data in place of basic (export-oriented) employment data. While urban analysts agree upon the superiority of basic employment data for these classifications, usually only total employment data are available. This paper uses the Arizona Community Data Set, a unique survey-generated body of information that describes the major economic characteristics of numerous Southwestern communities and includes both basic and total employment data, to explore the extent to which misclassification results when using total employment data. Key Words: classification, urban settlements, basic employment, functional specialization.

The classification of urban settlements has been of long-standing concern to geographers, planners, and other social scientists [2, 19]. The simplest and most traditional forms of such classification have been based solely on the labor force characteristics of urban settlements, but these methods fail to capture the important non-economic (social, demographic, etc.) sources of intercity variation that now characterize the technically more advanced societies. As a result, factor-ecologic approaches have largely replaced these traditional classification methods [2, 3, 10, 19].

The traditional labor-force-based classification methods do remain useful for differentiating among relatively small, free-standing urban agglomerations. As Berry [2, 3] has demonstrated, many nonmetropolitan communities continue to be differentiated by a single dominant economic function, whether that be an industry (e.g., mining) for which nonmetropolitan location factors still prevail or an activity (e.g., education) whose locations are not determined by economic criteria.¹ In such cases the community's wider urban characteristics are largely dictated by its narrower economic characteristics (i.e., its economic base).

¹ The terms "community" and "urban settlement" are used interchangeably in this paper to refer to relatively small, free-standing urban agglomerations.
