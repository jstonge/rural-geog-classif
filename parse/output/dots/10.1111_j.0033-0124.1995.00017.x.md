# Migration Patterns During a Period of Political and Economic Shocks in the Former Soviet Union: A Case Study of Yaroslavl' Oblast*

## Beth Mitchneck and David Plane

*University of Arizona*

This paper examines migration in Russia during the period that preceded the breakup of the former Soviet Union (FSU) and during the current transition period. An unusually rich dataset is used to conceptualize the impact that the political and economic collapse of a world superpower has on a migration system. A regional case study of migration in Yaroslavl' Oblast from 1989 through 1992 is used to examine the relevance of expected outcomes given standard theories of migration, empirical regularities found in capitalist economies, and past trends in the FSU. The data clearly show a migration system undergoing political and economic shocks. A significant decline of the volume of flows and a relative increase in the importance of interrepublic movement indicate disruptions. Increased relative mobility for those in the later years of the working-age population and increased importance of urban-to-rural migration flows are also important changes evident in this migration system undergoing shock. **Key Words:** migration, Russia, USSR, economic shocks.

## Introduction

The unprecedented social and economic changes in successor states of the former Soviet Union have attracted attention largely because of the apparent failure of a political and economic system. But the effects of restructuring on population dynamics, such as migration patterns, remain relatively unknown. Internal migration flows in the former Soviet Union have received little attention. In part this is due to a lack of information on current migration patterns in Russia. The lack of relevant comparative experience in world history as a basis for understanding changing migration patterns in Russia also explains the gap.

The purpose of this paper is to use an unusually rich dataset on current migration patterns in one region to examine population flows during the breakup of the former Soviet Union (FSU). We conceptualize how the political and economic collapse of a world superpower influences a migration system. A regional case study of migration in Yaroslavl' Oblast (Fig. 1) from 1989 through 1992 is used to examine the relevance of expected outcomes given standard theories of migration, empirical

regularities found in capitalist economies, and past trends in the FSU.

Little is known about how population mobility changes during political and economic shocks not occurring during armed conflict. This paper contributes knowledge on the Soviet and Russian cases, which provide an unprecedented example of decolonization, economic depression, and political restructuring. Although a large body of conceptual work exists on migration in market economies and developing economies, few have theorized migration in the centrally planned and transition cases (Mitchneck 1990). The direction and composition of current migration flows in Russia remain largely unexplored. For example, while scholarly literature has begun to look at international migration out of the FSU (Larrabee 1992), "international" flows in the FSU with Russia as a destination have received little attention in the scholarly literature (Dunlop 1993). This paper presents and analyzes information on recent flows using an unparalleled, detailed dataset.

Review of Migration Trends

For Western social scientists conceptualizing
migration patterns in Russia, standard tools
such as Ravenstein's laws of migration, life-cy-
cle theory, the Harris-Todaro model, public
choice models, and many others may be useful.
These models help us to predict migration
patterns according to variables such as dis-
tance, gender, age, unemployment, and local
government policy. The relevancy of these tra-
ditional models, however, to non-Western, de-
veloping countries has been questioned
(Brown and Sanders 1981). Approaches that
relate to both less developed and more devel-
oped contexts should be considered here due
to the prevalence of multiple development
contexts in Russia and the USSR.

In the particular context of the disintegra-
tion of the FSU, we question the relevancy of
previous work on forced refugee flows and
international return migration, particularly re-
patriation. Most of this literature explains refu-
gee flows during times of violent revolution or
temporary labor migrations related to refugeeflows (e.g., Pessar 1988; Marmora 1988; Koehn 1991; Shami 1993). Literature on repatriation often deals with decolonization where the origin country was not contiguous to the receiving country (e.g., Smith 1981) or with a situation of returning exiles (e.g., Pessar 1988). This literature lacks relevance to the Soviet and post-Soviet cases because few places in the FSU violently overthrew the Soviet regime, sending Russians packing. Returning Russians are not exiles—they left their homelands voluntarily, in some cases several generations earlier, to seek fortunes in contiguous territories of the country.

The appearance of crises or major economic "shocks" requires the modification of tradition theory. As described by Long (1981), the 1930s Great Depression in the United States caused a stagnating of the streams of rural-to-urban migration, as people stayed on the farms where at least they could eat. Explanations of the "clean break" of U.S. migration patterns in the 1970s (Vining and Strauss 1977) included consideration of the severe recession early in that decade that was brought on by the shock of the Arab oil embargo of 1973. The persistence of the metropolitan-to-nonmetropolitan movement trends through the late 1970s suggested, however, that a variety of factors were operating synchronously (Long and DeAre 1980). Among those factors was the demographic shock of extraordinarily large baby boom cohorts flooding regional labor markets (Plane and Rogerson 1991; Plane 1992). Interestingly, an oil glut (and recession) shock has been identified to help explain U.S. migration patterns in the 1980s (Plane and Rogerson 1989; McHugh and Gober 1992).

Plane (1984) provided a theory of migration system change involving "steady-state" and "non-steady-state" movement patterns that vary in relative proportions as a nation passes through periods of rapid structural change followed by longer interregnums during which movement patterns exhibit equilibrating trends. Of considerable current interest in regional science are the economies of unscheduled, major events, as attested to by several special theme sessions at the 1994 North American Meetings in Houston. To date, however, we are unaware of any of these emerging ideas having been applied to the impact of a major social and economic dis-

location on interregional population movement patterns.

## Soviet and Russian Case

Questions relevant to the changing direction and composition of migration flows in the Soviet and Russian context during a shock period include: What happens to the direction and composition of migration flows when a socially, economically, and politically integrated multinational state with distinct ethnic homelands breaks apart? What happens when strong government control over social and economic processes begins to fade? When, if ever, do major systemic changes occur?

The answers to these questions lie, in part, within the context of the previous migration system, one that was not well-studied by Western social scientists. Many studies of migration in the FSU successfully predict the volume and direction of migration flows using standard economic models despite the contextual problems related to the noncapitalist setting (Rybakovskiy 1973; Zaytsev 1973; Lysenko 1977; Ball and Demko 1978; Fuchs and Demko 1978; Grandstaff 1980; Maksakova 1986; Mitchneck 1990, 1991). These studies note the improvement of material welfare and quality of life as determining factors in the decision to migrate and destination choice. Standard economic models produced highly accurate predictions of the volume and direction of the flows (Grandstaff 1980; Mitchneck 1990 and 1991). Ball and Demko (1978) note variable influences over migration flows according to level and type of regional development—agrarian, frontier, less industrial, and more industrial—supporting the Brown and Sanders (1981) thesis regarding the importance of contextual influences over migration patterns.

Relatively little information is available about gender, age, and ethnic differentials of migration flows in the FSU. Research has shown generally balanced flows with respect to gender (Lewis and Rowland 1979). Mobility appears to be highest among the youngest age groups in the labor force and among children (Lewis and Rowland 1979). Also, substantial ethnic differentials appear to exist, with the Slavs and several minority ethnic groups, such as Jews, Tatars, and Armenians, having the highest mobility rates (Lewis et al. 1976).During the 1970s and 1980s a relatively small proportion of the total Soviet population moved interregionally each year. Topilin (1988) estimates that about 5% of the Soviet population moved annually, with less than half of those moves between republics or economic regions. The 5% figure places mobility in the FSU well below that of developed countries (Long 1991). Although Long's (1991) data are not directly comparable, the small figure is striking when compared to data on changing place of residence circa 1981, for example, 17.5% in the United States and 9.6% in Great Britain. The majority of the moves were between closely located oblasts and thus were short-distance moves. In the 1970s between 40% and 60% of moves were intraoblast (Rybakovskiy 1973). Evidence of a trend toward long distance moves, i.e., an increasing proportion of interrepublic moves or interoblast moves, would indicate a change to the Russian migration system.

The direction and composition of migration flows in the 1970s and 1980s were largely stable, but we see the first evidence of social upheaval with the changing direction of some net migration interchanges in the 1980s. In a broad regional perspective, the Soviet (and Russian) population traditionally redistributed from central Russia eastward towards Siberia and the Far East, and southward towards Kazakhstan, Central Asia, and the Transcaucasus until the 1980s when the pattern reversed (Lewis and Rowland 1979; Mitchneck 1990).

The reversal of this long-term, established pattern of migration signals social and economic change. Indeed, cities and regions in central Russia appear to have attracted migrants from farther rather than closer origins in the mid-1980s. Destination-specific spatial interaction models of intercity migration in 1985 for cities in central Russia have positive distance coefficients, indicating that migrants were coming from distant rather than nearby origins (Mitchneck 1991).

Large-scale rural-to-urban migration was also an important feature in the migration processes of Russia, in particular in the post-World War I and II periods (Lewis and Rowland 1979). Although rural-to-urban migration was a significant trend in the USSR, it became relatively less important in Russia over time.

Interurban migration became predominant in the 1970s and 1980s in Russia and for the ethnic Russian population (Lewis and Rowland 1979). A change to the migration system would be indicated by the decreasing importance of interurban moves. This did occur in Russia after the 1917 Revolution when many urbanites left the cities of northern Russia for rural places in the south and east in search of food and land resources (Lorimer 1946).

## Yaroslavl' Case Study

The Russian and Soviet migration systems have undergone shocks in the past due to major violent events such as the 1917 Russian Revolution, civil war, and World War II. Characteristics of the migration system persisted despite these events—the north-to-south and west-to-east flows and rural-to-urban flows.

The essential problem here is to conceptualize how the direction and composition of migration flows within the migration system(s) of Russia may change during the post-Soviet period—a time of instability, disintegration, and reintegration, not a wartime period. There are likely to be several overlapping yet distinct phases of migration. The reality of the situation suggests that although the phases may be distinct, no one phase will be evident in isolation in any one place or at any one point in time. Phase I, destabilization, describes the late 1970s and early 1980s when the reversal of the north-to-south net exchange occurred. Phase I foreshadows Phase II, disintegration, when the chaotic patterns occasioned by the current destabilization and political/economic shocks become evident. The initial period of destabilization ended in the late 1980s just as perestroika was ending. Phase II migration should be evident in the 1990s, with 1989 being a transition year when both Phase I and Phase II patterns co-existed.

### *Yaroslavl*

Migration in and out of Yaroslavl' Oblast should be seen as representative of central Russia. Data on migration in the Central Economic Region (a statistical accounting region to which Yaroslavl' belongs) for 1970 through 1989 indicates that Yaroslavl' is not atypical for the region with respect to losses of populationin the 1970s due to migration, gains in the 1980s, persistent inflows of migrants to urban places from the 1970s through 1989, and consistent outflows of migrants from rural places (Goskomstat RSFSR 1990). Although the Central Economic Region has received a large portion of the forced migrants returning from the former Soviet republics, Yaroslavl' Oblast received about the average number for the region (Vitkovskaya 1994). Despite the relatively large number of returning Russians in Yaroslavl', the oblast was not designated as a receiving location by the central government (Schwartz 1993).

Other regional issues also support the description of Yaroslavl' as typical for the region. The politics are not overwhelmingly liberal or conservative, although the region can boast of early "democratic" movements (Hahn 1991). Although the oblast capital is located within a five-hour train ride, Moscow does not dominate the region; the oblast capital, Yaroslavl', is itself a regional center due to its industrial importance and role as a transportation center. Industrial employment in the oblast is concentrated in petrochemicals, machine-building, the automobile industry, and light industry (textiles). These industrial sectors provide the oblast economy with the combination of growth possibilities and an obsolete, fixed capital base. The oblast is not dominated by the military industrial complex, although some factories did produce for it. Yaroslavl' does differ from other central Russian oblasts in that it has a higher proportion of its population living in urban areas (about 80% urban and 20% rural in 1989), but like other oblasts in the central region its population is mainly ethnic Russian (95% in 1989).

## *Data and Methods*

Migration data for Phase II come from the annual population register data collected in Yaroslavl' by the oblast government. Register data collected at the oblast level are probably of higher quality than those presented at the national level; by the time the national level receives the data, they have passed through several government offices, each having the potential to introduce error. Trained sociologists calculated this dataset by hand using the original registry forms. Although human error can occur in the absence of computerization in

Russia, the population accounting in this dataset is the best available to date.

Several major drawbacks, however, exist due to the nature of the registry system. First, inmigration data are more accurate than outmigration data. Individuals must register as they migrate into a place in order to receive social services, including housing, education, and public assistance, but no such incentives exist to register outmigration. Outmigration data are generally underestimated, but due to the absence of place-to-place migration data, we cannot estimate the level of undercounting. Within this particular dataset, however, we can measure 6% undercounting for intraoblast migration. Second, the urban accounting systems are more accurate than the rural ones. Rural inmigration and outmigration are likely undercounted. Third, there are two separate counts of intraoblast migration. Differing levels of accuracy between urban and rural accounting systems and differing levels of self-reporting for inmigration and outmigration explain the latter problem.

Accounting and data quality problems are not unique to Soviet or Russian population data. These problems exist in many contexts, particularly those with less technologically advanced data management systems. These data are, however, the best quality and most detailed available on migration trends in Russia. Our approach is to analyze the time series with respect to trends without giving undue weight to absolute figures. We rely most heavily on the inmigration data—rather than the outmigration data—and the data collected in urban places.

Because the goal of this work is to conceptualize the nature of a migration system undergoing shock and, in part, because of data limitations, simple descriptive techniques rather than causal modeling techniques were used to describe and analyze the migration data. Other than presenting the raw data themselves, we calculate simple ratios to describe the composition of the migration flows, such as a sex ratio and an age ratio of the 15-34 age group to the 35-59 age group. We also calculate demographic effectiveness ratios to measure gains and losses due to the exchange of population at varying geographical levels. A high positive or negative ratio indicates a large imbalance between the stream and counterstream. An in-creasing ratio over time signifies regional net
gains. The ratio is calculated by subtracting the
counterstream from the stream, dividing by
the sum of migrants, and multiplying by 100.

*Hypothesis 3: Women predominate in long-distance moves*

The proportion of women relative to men should increase in long-distance flows as part of the return migration motivated by social conflict and by economic conflict.

If the return migration is politically or socially motivated, then more women than men should make the long-distance moves as is characteristic of international refugee flows.

The long-distance moves would be motivated by social unrest, perceived or real ethnic conflict, and political change rather than job seeking. We expect women to precede men, as wives and children move first while the men stay behind, continue working, or join the military.

While the dataset is rich, it does not include a breakdown of the flows by origin and destination. Using data on the ethnic and gender composition of the flows, we extrapolated long-distance movers by ethnicity, given two considerations. First, non-Russian in-migrants will most likely be long-distance movers. Non-Russian ethnic groups with homelands in the FSU were concentrated in their homelands. Second, Russians will predominate in the refugee inflows to Yaroslavl' as they leave non-Russian regions due to real or perceived ethnic conflict and social unrest.

In support of the hypothesis, Russian women do surpass men for inmigration to Yaroslavl' in each year (Table 4). Ukrainian and Belorussian women do not outnumber men but increase their representation in the flows between 1989 and 1992. The category of "other," comprised of ethnic groups with homelands within Russia (the Mordovian, Dagestani, Osetian, Chechen, Ingush, Bashkortostan, Udmurt, Komi, and Chuvash republics), also increased its proportion of women. These increases are consistent with more traditional refugee flows. Many of the non-Russian ethnic homelands in Russia experienced both armed conflict and social unrest during 1991 and 1992.

*Hypothesis 4: Changing ethnic composition*

Ethnic composition of the migration flows should change during this period. The proportion of Slavs to non-Slavs in-migrating should increase over time, as should the proportion of non-Slavs to Slavs out-migrating.

We expect changing ethnic composition to reflect a trend toward further reconcentration of Soviet national groups within their homelands as a manifestation of social and economic changes during Phase II. Table 5 shows support for this hypothesis. Slavs do increase their

proportion of total inflows from 89% in 1989 to 93% in 1992 and Russians increase from 84% to 89%. This indicates a trend toward the reconcentration of Russians and Slavs in central Russia and a structural change in the composition of migration flows.

The data clearly reflect outmigration from central Russia by non-Slavic ethnic groups. In 1989, more non-Slavs migrated into Yaroslavl' Oblast than migrated out of the oblast (Table 5). Few non-Russian ethnic groups had negative net flows. But by 1992, the situation had reversed with a negative net flow appearing for almost all non-Slavic ethnic groups and with the overall non-Russian proportion of inflow decreasing from 11% to 8%. This supports the hypothesis that non-Slavic groups were leaving central Russia. Although the data do not specify the destinations of out-migrants, it is possible that non-Slavs leaving Yaroslavl' are returning to their ethnic homelands or joining kin in other places in Russia.

Table 5 reveals other notable features of migration patterns by ethnicity. *Hypothesis 5: Changing age composition of migration flows*

The labor force peak of the age schedules of migrants should broaden and the volumes of migrants for all age groups should decrease due to the economic and political shocks. Over the period, mobility for older age groups within the working-age population (including mid-career) should increase relative to the younger age groups. We expect the changing age composition to be evident for men and women in the total and urban flows.

Due to economic and political instability, migration should become a viable response of persons in all age groups, not just of persons

in the younger years of labor force participation with children, as was the case during the Soviet period (Lewis and Rowland 1979). We expect two trends to become evident: (1) older age groups will increase their proportion of the total flows because migration becomes a response of the entire population, and (2) the volume of flows for all age groups will decline at the end of the period because fewer people will be at risk of migrating, and those remaining will choose to wait out the crisis in place.

The ratios decline over the period for gross migration flows, inmigration, and outmigration, signifying greater balance of age-specific flows. The declining ratios hold constant for total, male, and female migrants, further highlighting the pervasive changes in mobility. The largest relative declines are evident for the 15-34 age group, the group with the previously highest mobility rates. The redistribution to rural places at the interrepublic scale most likely reflects the urban residential controls in place at the time that affected the long-distance return migrants. The declining importance of rural-to-urban migration and increased importance of rural destinations may also reflect an element of choice on behalf of the migrants. Urban-to-rural migrants position themselves in places where they have access to productive resources (land and livestock for food production) that are not available in the cities or for which they must pay substantial amounts. The early 1990s was a time of declining availability of food in urban places and rising interest on the part of urban dwellers in obtaining plots of land to grow their own food supply. A second reason that migrants may choose rural places over urban ones is that land reform, including the allocation of land, structures, and farm equipment, was beginning in the experimental stage and seemed an inevitable event. People located in rural places prior to land reform physically

position themselves in places where they are likely to reap benefits to which urban dwellers do not have access.

*Hypothesis 7: Changing interurban migration flows*

The volume and demographic effectiveness of interurban flows should decrease as alternative destinations compete with Yaroslavl' for migrants.

We expect the volume and effectiveness of interurban flows to decrease for Yaroslavl' Oblast on the interoblast scale due to the increasing relative importance of intraoblast migration (see Table 2). The data reflect decreased volume of interurban flows at the intraoblast and interoblast scales (Table 7) and decreased effectiveness of interurban flows within Russia (Table 8). The data also show the striking increased migration to urban places in Yaroslavl' at the interrepublic scale, with the greatest gains occurring in 1992, the year of the breakup (Table 8). These trends are significant because they signal further disruptions to the previous system where interurban moves predominated at all scales.

## Conclusions

One primary conclusion from the empirical analysis of migration in Yaroslavl' Oblast is that despite dramatic political and economic disruption in the FSU, the structure of the migration system is predictable using an analysis of historical trends and standard approaches. Despite dramatic contextual differences between capitalist and socialist economic systems, concepts derived from capitalist contexts can serve as a guide to the analysis of migration flows in socialist contexts. Contextual differences exist, however, and are important for the study of migration flows. For example, the increasing migrant flows to rural places in Yaroslavl' are due, in part, to the lackof a housing market and to government regulation.

The data clearly show a migration system undergoing political and economic shocks. A significant decline in the volume of flows and a relative increase in the importance of long-distance (interrepublic) moves are results of social and economic disruption. This is particularly evident when we look at the demographic composition of the flows. Increased relative mobility for those in the later years of the working-age population, reflected in the declining age ratios, is one of the most important changes evident in the migration system. Also, a significant change of direction of migration flows, in particular the increasing importance of urban-to-rural flows, was found. Despite only minor changes to the migration patterns by ethnic group, the reconcentration of ethnic groups into their respective homelands within the FSU was evident. The process is likely to accelerate during the 1990s and deserves greater attention in future analysis. This change also suggests that whereas Yaroslavl' may be an ideal locale to study many aspects of current changes in the migration system, much of the migration by non-Russian ethnic groups is taking place elsewhere.

Some continuity with the past migration system does exist. For example, Yaroslavl' Oblast had had a net gain of population through migration for a long time; however, during the crisis period the gains increased. Interurban migration is still dominant, but less so relative to the increased importance of urban-to-rural flows at the interrepublic and intraoblast scales. Also, for the total population, gender differentials in mobility are slight; however, when disaggregated by ethnicity, gender differentials increase. These examples highlight the importance of disaggregating the migration data in order to reveal the composition of broad changes in the migration system.

We expect that when the crisis phase ends, the patterns established in Phase II will lay the foundation for a new migration system distinct from previous patterns. There are several possible scenarios, however, about how the system will evolve. If the economic and political crises worsen, we can expect Phase II migration patterns to persist to the extent that at-risk pools remain. A postshock transition phase may be

characterized by stepwise migration evident on a system-wide basis as migrants respond to changing individual and environmental conditions in places. An example of changing environmental conditions would be the lifting or institution of residential controls in different locations in the system. Another phase could be a temporary new stability with a gradual return to certain pre-crisis migration patterns such as interurban moves predominating. But the spatial configuration of the system will differ dramatically from the pre-crisis period. For example, during the 1970s and 1980s, large net inflows were concentrated in relatively few places like Siberian boom towns and capital cities of republics. Provincial capitals were attractive destinations for their own hinterlands but did not present intervening obstacles for nonlocal migrants. New centers of economic growth and cultural primacy are likely to emerge that will change the configuration of the system of origins and destinations. We are likely to see less primacy under capitalism or some economic system other than central planning. This new migration system will ultimately depend on the success of economic reforms in particular places, including changing regional levels of economic prosperity.

The structure and composition of the migration flows and the resulting migration rates after the shock will not look entirely like those in the United States or any other country. The historical social and economic context of the FSU and the dynamic nature of migration flows suggest that while there may be a convergence of economic systems and some related similarities of the migration systems, significant differences will persist. ■

Issues of Spatial and Temporal Variability in
Climate Impact Studies

Gregory J. Carbone

University of South Carolina

The accuracy of impact estimates relating climate change to regional-scale agricultural production is constrained
by the temporal and spatial resolution of climate change projections. Several techniques have been used to
compensate for these limitations in order to provide reasonable estimates of the impact of climate change on crop
yield. One approach assumes that variability over time can substitute for spatial variability, thereby reducing the
need to estimate the impacts at a spatially dense network of stations—an assumption that has not been generally
tested. This study evaluates this assumption using methods similar to those employed in the climate impact
literature. The findings suggest that current practices are generally defensible if the goal is to provide a range of
possible crop responses to climate change. However, the results also show that the assumption is highly sensitive
to specific interactions at the soil-plant-atmosphere interface and, consequently, does not hold under certain
circumstances. Key Words: climate change, agricultural impacts, scale, temporal variability, spatial variability.

**Introduction**

Concern about potential climate change has
led to a number of studies addressing the
possible impacts on agriculture (e.g., Santer
1985; Hodges et al. 1987; Smit et al. 1989;
Cooter 1990; Curry et al. 1990; Rosenzweig
1990). Two themes emerge from these studies.
First, global climate change could alter agricul-
tural productivity, requiring adjustment of cer-
tain agricultural practices. Second, present
strategies used to estimate climate impacts on
agriculture have several shortcomings. Perhaps
the most critical shortcoming involves accurate
representation of climate change at a spatial
scale where agricultural impacts are felt.
Clearly, the impact of climate change over
broad agricultural regions should result from

the aggregated effects at local levels. Yet pro-
jecting climate change down to the local scale
remains difficult. Many climate impact studies
rely on general circulation model (GCM) out-
put to provide initial estimates of climate
change. Even though GCM results presently
represent the most reliable and comprehensive
means of estimating potential climate change,
the spatial and temporal resolution of the out-
put is typically too coarse to use directly with
crop yield models at a local scale (Riebsame
1990; Rosenzweig 1990).

Most researchers have acknowledged the
uncertainty associated with regional and local
scale climate change scenarios and have recog-
nized the need to present a range of potential
impacts that conform to such uncertainty
(Cooter 1990; Riebsame 1990; Rosenzweig
