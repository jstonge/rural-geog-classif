Chi Kin Leung

Abstract. This paper examines the locational structure and economic consequences of Hong Kong's production subcontracting activities in the Zhujiang Delta, China. Based on a comprehensive investigation of firms in the three major Hong Kong industries, I found that the subcontracting activities from the territory were widely distributed in the Delta, with Shenzhen, Dongguan, and Guangzhou as the three major centers of concentration. This pattern is attributable mainly to the extensive use of preexisting kinship and Hong Kong business ties to establish linkages. Other causes are closeness between Hong Kong and the Delta, the efforts of Chinese authorities to attract foreign investments to the Delta, and the locational advantages or specialization of individual locales. The locations of subcontracting linkages vary with different transactional channels. Linkages based on preexisting kinship or Hong Kong business ties orient to the rural, those based on other contacts to the urban, areas of the Delta. The incorporation of preexisting kinship and business ties insured exchange reliability and facilitated further cooperation between firms, which encouraged Hong Kong firms to subcontract and provide equipment and training supports to their Chinese subcontractors. The development of subcontracting activities has promoted rapid industrial development in the Zhujiang Delta, and led to production integration and economic interdependence between the two places. The study shows the relationships between firms, both preexisting social-institutional and subsequent transactional, are important locational and structural determinants of vertically disintegrated activities. It also shows the structure of transactions, while

complex, is organized. A detailed examination of such structure will provide an integrated and context-specific framework for industrial organization and location research.

Key Words: personal contacts, kinship, production subcontracting, development, Hong Kong, Zhujiang Delta, China.

VERTICAL disintegration represents a major strategy of corporation organization in major market economies during the past two decades (Clutterbuck 1985; Lash and Urry 1987). It is an arrangement of production wherein firms externalize their manufacturing activities to other independent firms. Also it represents a system in which firms assume different positions or functions in a chain of production. In major developed market economies such as the U.S., the rise of vertical disintegration is attributable to the performance problems caused by capital redundancy and labor militancy (Cooke 1988; Piore and Sabel 1984). It is also attributable to the strategic responses of firms toward rapid market changes, increased international competition, and the corresponding worldwide development of new modes of corporate competition, based on interfirm consultative coordination and continuous improvement in product, process, and organization (Best 1990). Vertical disintegration provides firms the benefits of reduced investment risks, strengthened control over the labor process, and faster response to technological and market changes (Donaghu and Barff 1990; Holmes 1986; Imrie 1986). In addition, it provides production systems increased viability for long-term growth and development (Sato 1984; Saxenian 1990), andpromotes exchanges between firms in many forms, including production subcontracting, licensing, and joint ventures (Contractor and Lorange 1988).

The development of vertical disintegration is not confined to developed market economies; a number of major developing market economies, including Hong Kong, have also experienced similar changes in recent years. In Hong Kong, unlike its developed counterparts, these changes are attributable mainly to the strategic needs of firms to expand production or to reduce cost pressures associated with labor shortages (Chen 1983; Federation of Hong Kong Industries 1990). A major location of Hong Kong's vertically disintegrated activities is the Zhujiang Delta in southern Guangdong, China; the primary form of exchanges between the two places is production subcontracting. From 1979-89, the Zhujiang Delta accounted for approximately 75 percent of cumulative realized foreign investments, mostly from Hong Kong, in Guangdong; about one-fifth were production subcontracting investments.¹ Since Hong Kong's subcontracting investments involve primarily manufacturing activities, they have had a major impact on the development of industries in the Delta, and have made Hong Kong-Zhujiang Delta one of the world's fastest growing regions and major export economies during the past decade. In 1989 this region was the world's tenth largest exporter, accounting for 2.69 percent of the value of total world exports, a figure second only to Japan in Asia (derived from General Economy Division 1991; Guangdong Province Statistical Bureau 1990; Trade Analysis Section 1990).

Despite this extensive development, a detailed understanding of the locational and structural characteristics of Hong Kong's subcontracting activities in the Zhujiang Delta is lacking. There is no systematic information concerning the locational distribution and transactional characteristics of the subcontracting activities of different types of firms and different industries. The locational and structural determinants of the subcontracting activities are also unknown. A few studies generally agree that the social relationships between firms are the major factors underlying development, but the specific regional economic consequences of these relationships with respect to production subcontracting remain unexamined (Leung 1990; Smart and Smart 1991). This

lack of detailed understanding hampers efforts to assess the regional economic relations between Hong Kong and the Zhujiang Delta, and the development impacts of the subcontracting activities on both places. Based on a comprehensive investigation of firms in the three major Hong Kong industries (garments, textiles, and electronics), this study has three primary objectives. The first is to reveal the characteristics of Hong Kong's subcontracting activities in China, especially in the Zhujiang Delta: the distribution of the subcontracting activities, the nature of the transactional relationships, the stability and contents of exchanges, and the interfirm contractual and extracontractual arrangements with respect to different types of Hong Kong manufacturers and to different channels of subcontracting transactions. The second objective is to examine the major locational and structural determinants of Hong Kong's subcontracting activities in China, especially in the Zhujiang Delta, giving specific emphasis to the significance of social and institutional relationships between firms. The theoretical implications of these relationships on the locational and economic consequences of vertical disintegration are also examined. The final objective is to evaluate the regional economic relations between Hong Kong and the Zhujiang Delta and the development impacts of the subcontracting activities on both places. The development prospects of the regional industrial system will also be highlighted.

This paper has six sections. In the first section, I review the current theoretical perspectives and the significance of social and institutional relationships between firms as major locational and structural determinants of vertically disintegrated activities. The second section sets forth the research context and the historical and contemporary factors underlying the development of Hong Kong's subcontracting activities in China. The third section outlines my research design, followed by a section describing the transactional structure for subcontracting exchanges. In the fifth section, I detail the locational and transactional characteristics. Finally I assess the regional economic relations between and the development impacts of the subcontracting activities. The paper concludes with a discussion of the theoretical and empirical implications of the study for future industrial organization and location research.## Theoretical Perspectives

Production subcontracting is a form of vertical disintegration of production, commonly defined as a business arrangement between two independent firms—one (the contractor) provides the orders and the other (the subcontractor) furnishes the work or services for the processing of materials or the production of parts, components, subassembly or assembly of products according to the production specifications and the marketing arrangements of the contractor (United Nations Industrial Development Organization 1974). Subcontracting has three primary forms based on the contractor's needs: *specialized*, due to inadequate technological know-how or equipment; *current*, due to inadequate capacity to meet a delivery schedule; and *economic*, due to lower-cost inputs of other firms. Watanabe (1980) further identifies two types of subcontracting based on the contents of the work performed: *industrial* (process or component), which involves manufacturing processes or intermediate products, and *commercial* (product), which relates to finished commodities.

Theoretical explanations of the locational and economic consequences of subcontracting or of vertical disintegration of production are based primarily on the transaction costs perspective defined by Scott (1983, 1986, 1988a). This perspective, which follows the work of Coase (1937) and Williamson (1975, 1979, 1984, 1985), states that vertical disintegration promotes locational agglomeration because external transaction costs between firms in the market, including transportation and communication costs, increase with distance. The propensity to agglomerate (locationally) increases further when the transactions include small-scale, irregular, unstandardized, or contact-intensive activities that have high unit linkage costs; or when firms seek to reduce demand fluctuations by increasing their customer base through locational clustering. This locational tendency, according to Storper and Scott (1989), is associated with a flexible regime of capital accumulation or mode of corporate organization characterized by intense external transactions between firms (external economies of scale) as a result of unstable market conditions. On the other hand, the locational dispersal of production occurs when the trans-

actions involve bulky, stable, standardized, or easily manageable activities that have low unit linkage costs. These activities contain primarily routine deskilled production processes and are dispersed to peripheral areas where labor or land costs are low (Scott 1988a, 210). This locational tendency, as stated by Storper and Scott (1989), is associated with a Fordist regime of capital accumulation typified by deepened internal transactions within firms (internal economies of scale) as a result of stable market circumstances. Spatial and international divisions of labor therefore result, with the centers dominating in unstandardized skilled labor and the hinterlands depending on routine unskilled activities (Scott and Storper 1986).

Scott's elaboration provides important insights into the general dynamics of industrial organization and locational processes. His elaboration, however, concerns primarily the effects of linkage costs on the industrial agglomeration process; the other major locational and structural determinants of vertically disintegrated activities, especially in the hinterland or over distance, remain unexamined. An examination of the related literature suggests that vertical disintegration of production does not necessitate locational agglomeration. The spatial distribution is affected not only by linkage costs, but also by a number of macroscale political-economic factors at or above the regional level and microscale managerial-behavioral factors at the firm level. The macrofactors include regional factor supply structure, local entrepreneurial characteristics, and regional or national industrial policies. Locational dispersal of vertically disintegrated activities is likely to occur when industrial agglomerations experience labor shortages (Linge 1991), when central locations lack suitable suppliers (Harper and Goodner 1990), when peripheral locales possess an entrepreneurial culture (Hansen 1991), or when regions or nations adopt certain domestic sourcing industrial policies (Jones and North 1991). This locational tendency is also likely to occur when firms implement inbound transportation planning, using specialist freight services and electronic shipment tracking systems (Ramsdale and Harvey 1990) or when firms adopt specific purchasing management strategies (Ahmed et al. 1991; Porter 1991). The recent development in producer-carrier strategic alliances and in service-sensitive interregional and international deliveriesfurther facilitate the dispersal of vertically disintegrated activities (Bradley 1991, 1992; Ettlinger 1992).

These macro and microfactors aside, the development of vertically disintegrated activities in the hinterland or over distance requires the presence or the establishment of certain relationships between firms as a contractual governance structure. The relationships between firms not only affect the process of dispersal, they also affect the detailed distribution. The relationships between firms, especially preexisting social and institutional relationships, are a major determinant of hinterland or distant linkages because these relationships provide firms confidence in the reliability of remote exchANGES (Walker 1988). This reliability is important to firms because of the prohibitive costs of supervision and replacement of distant counterparts and of production disruption. This is especially true for linkages that traverse social or political space because of differing business practices or legal arrangements (Hakansson 1982), and for linkages that have stringent quality or delivery requirements or both (Willis and Huston 1990). Preexisting social and institutional relationships further enable firms to reduce the costs of establishing hinterland or distant linkages, especially in environments with considerable informational or transactional constraints. Under these circumstances, firms are likely to transact with the remote counterparts familiar to them (Beije and Groenewegen 1992; Granovetter 1985). The locations of the socially or institutionally linked transactional counterparts will therefore determine the spatial linkage patterns of the focal firms (Hamilton 1974). At a regional level, the social and institutional networks between a center and its hinterland will in part define the locational characteristics of the center-hinterland linkage systems.

The relationships between firms are also a major structural determinant of hinterland or distant linkages. A transactional relationship, once established, will gradually develop through time as the reliability of successive exchANGES is assured. This relationship enables firms to strengthen control over their operational environment, thereby securing access to needed resources for performance or development (Burt 1989; Hakansson 1989). A developed relationship is usually stable because linkage change can incur high linkage replacement

costs, reduce exchange efficiency, and increase operational uncertainty (Egan and Mody 1992; Pfeffer and Salancik 1978). This is especially true for relationships involving close social or institutional ties, entailing recurrent exchanges, or containing firm-specific physical assets (Hakansson 1982; Johannisson 1987; Johanson and Mattsson 1987; Rosson and Ford 1982). A developed relationship also tends to be cooperative, facilitating contractual and extracontractual arrangements between firms, which can have significant development consequences for the firms involved (Landeros and Monczka 1989; Mead 1984; Melin 1987). In subcontracting, the contractual and extracontractual arrangements include various kinds of assistance that contractors provide to their subcontractors. This assistance, according to Lall (1980), is of seven major types, including plant establishment, production planning, technical, financial, material sourcing, managerial, and client development. The continuing development of a subcontracting relationship can further lead to technology or product research cooperation between contractors and subcontractors, strengthening the long-term development capabilities of the latter, as the Japanese relational contracting system demonstrates (Dore 1986; Sato 1984). At a regional level, the development of cooperative subcontracting relationships with the center can promote the technological capabilities and development of the hinterland or individual locales (Fredriksson and Lindmark 1979; Russo 1989). The development of cooperative subcontracting linkages internationally can similarly promote the development of countries as studies of the Asian Productivity Organization (1978), Germidis (1980), and UNIDO (1974) illustrate.

A detailed investigation of the locational and structural characteristics of vertically disintegrated activities and the resultant center-hinterland economic relations therefore must consider, among others, the subtle relationships between firms and the detailed characteristics of the transactions. Given the complexity of a production system, the investigation needs to examine the interfirm relationships and transactional characteristics of different types of firms constituting that system (Walker 1988). Firms vary in their internal organizational and functional constraints, channel selection strategies, interfirm transactional relationships, and exchange characteristics (Glasmeier 1990; Kimand Daniels 1991; Taylor and Thrift 1982a, b). Small firms, for example, tend to transact with the local firms they already know because they lack the abilities to search for alternative counterparts and to supervise activities over distance. The relationships between small firms and their transactional counterparts are usually personal, and the exchanges are generally small-scale and irregular (Dias 1986; Taylor and Wood 1973). Large firms, on the other hand, tend to transact with nonlocal firms because they have greater abilities not only to establish and supervise distant linkages, but also to fulfil the requirements of regional or national transactional channels. The relationships between large firms and their transactional counterparts are usually formal, and the exchanges are generally large scale and stable (Egan and Mody 1992; Marshall 1979).² The composition of firm segments and transactional channels of a production system, along with the resultant patterns of contacts, is place-specific. Together they reflect the combined effects of firm characteristics and strategies, sectoral attributes and industrial practices, social rules and cultural traits, and the political-economic and historical organization of production of a particular locality (Sayer 1985, 1989). An examination of interfirm relationships and transactional characteristics with respect to the segmental and channel composition of a production system will therefore provide a context-specific mesoscale explanation of the locational and economic consequences of vertical disintegration of production, thereby enabling a better understanding of the resultant economic relations between a center and its hinterland.

## Research Context

Hong Kong is a British colony on the eastern fringe of the estuary of the Zhujiang (Pearl River) Delta in South China (Fig. 1). Under the 1984 Sino-British agreement, the sovereignty of the territory will revert to China in 1997 (Hong Kong Government 1984). Hong Kong has a predominant Chinese population, composed of migrants and their descendants with hometowns mainly in the Zhujiang Delta and in the Chaozhou area in Guangdong (Vogel 1989). Approximately half a million migrants (about 25 percent of Hong Kong's 1949 population) came to the territory during 1948-49 to

escape communist rule in mainland China (Geiger and Geiger 1973). Among them were some textile and garment entrepreneurs from Shanghai who have been instrumental in developing the territory's industries since the mid-1950s (Wong 1988). In the two and a half decades that followed, Hong Kong underwent rapid industrialization and gradually became a major world exporter of inexpensive textile, garment, and electronic products (Cheng 1982; Youngson 1982). This success, however, created labor shortages and heightened labor and other production costs, which began to weaken the international competitiveness of Hong Kong manufacturers by the late 1970s (Advisory Committee on Diversification 1979; Economist Intelligence Unit 1983).

The investment incentives provided for foreign firms usually vary between special areas because of national strategies or local initiatives or both (Jahiel and Diamond 1986; Khan 1991). The Shenzhen SEZ, for example, has lower income tax rates for foreign high-technology ventures and greater local autonomy to approve foreign investments, as the central government seeks to attract technology- and capital-intensive ventures to this location. These initiatives include an exemption of local income surtax, improvement of transportation and telecommunication systems, development of export processing zones, training of local workers, and provision of financial and otherassistances to local firms engaging in export processing and related activities (Han 1988; Yang, et al. 1988). In addition several Delta locales, including Dongguan and Baoan, have established offices within their respective city or county areas and in other major Delta city locations to hasten the processing of subcontracting investment agreements and related operational documents (Zhou 1988, 1989). To further attract investments from the territory and from other countries, most provincial and local organizations, both public and private, along with many state-owned trading and specialized corporations such as Huarun Jituan (China Resources Company), have maintained their subsidiaries or offices in Hong Kong, providing assistance to Hong Kong and foreign firms intending to invest in China. These trading and investment organizations usually differ in the geographic coverage and sectoral affiliation of their production and business networks, with Yuehai Qiye (Guangdong Enterprises), a business consortium of Guangdong province, having a network of major state-owned and private firms in the province (Linn 1986; Salem 1988).

The needs of Hong Kong manufacturers to reduce production costs and the efforts of Chinese authorities to attract foreign capital have resulted in a significant flow of Hong Kong's investments into China. From 1979-89, Hong Kong was China's most important foreign investor, accounting for 28 percent of cumulative realized foreign investments in China in that period (compiled from SSB 1981-90; Editorial Board of the Almanac 1984-90). Given the social affinity and physical proximity of the two places and the special investment incentives in the area, Hong Kong's investments are geographically concentrated in Guangdong, especially in Zhujiang Delta. In 1989 Zhujiang Delta shared approximately 72 percent of total realized foreign investments in Guangdong, of which about four-fifths were from Hong Kong.⁴ Structurally, the small capital requirement and simple establishment procedure, along with the initiatives of various locales, have made production subcontracting a major form of Hong Kong's investments in the Delta. This is especially true for medium and small Hong Kong firms because of their size constraint and unstable market conditions. Production subcontracting accounted for approximately 88 percent of the total number of foreign invest-

ment agreements and about 20 percent of cumulative realized foreign investments in Guangdong from 1979-89 (compiled from Guangdong Province Statistical Bureau 1985-90). The proclivity of Hong Kong's industrial system to decentralize production activities to China is extensive, with most of the industrial sectors in the territory having varying degrees of subcontracting activities across the border (Chen 1988; Ng 1988; Walden 1987).

## Data and Methodology

This study is a detailed investigation of the locational structure and economic consequences of Hong Kong's subcontracting activities in China, especially in the Zhujiang Delta. It focuses on the locational distribution and transactional characteristics of these linkages, with the segmental composition of Hong Kong's industrial system and the subcontracting channel structure between the two places as the analytical framework. I selected the three major Hong Kong industries—garments, textiles, and electronics—as the targets of investigation. To fully reveal the segmental organization of the three industries, I included the sectors of wearing apparel (HKSIC [Hong Kong Standard Industrial Classification] 320), wearing apparel accessories (322), spinning (325), weaving (326), knitting (327), textile finishing (328), made-up textile goods except wearing apparel (329), electrical and electronic consumer products (383), and electrical and electronic parts and accessories (384) as the basis of analysis. Together these nine sectors accounted for 56 percent of the territory's manufacturing workforce and approximately 49 percent of total Hong Kong-China manufacturing trade in 1989 (Industrial Production Statistics Section 1991; Trade Analysis Section 1990).

The study employs personal interview as the primary data gathering device. A systematic stratified sample, which comprised 1,243 firms in the three industries, was constructed from the 1986 central registry of all manufacturing establishments of the Hong Kong Census and Statistics Department (CSD). The sample uses industrial sectors at the 4-digit HKSIC level and employment size of individual establishments as the stratification criteria. ## The Segmental Organization of the Three Industries

Five segments compose the production system of the three industries studied. As Table 2 shows, these segments are large firm, medium export, medium nonexport, small export, and small nonexport. The large firm segment comprises mostly multiplant corporations with more than 500 employees. Most firms in this

segment have in-house R&D and direct overseas marketing capabilities; they form the core of indigenous export development efforts of the three industries. The relationships between most large firms and their overseas buyers are cooperative and longstanding. This segment of firms, therefore, has high degrees of export stability. The large firm segment has three subsegments which differ mainly in the spatial scale of operation. These three subsegments are global firms that have a worldwide production network, regional firms that have a production network in Asia, and local firms with a production network in the territory.

The medium export and medium nonexport segments comprise mostly single-plant firms with 100-200 employees. The export segment contains two subsegments, product development and international subcontracting, of which the latter predominates. The textiles and the electronics industry, on the other hand, comprise mostly local subcontracting firms, because they have higher degrees of production specialization. The electronics industry further comprises product development firms because of the technological nature of this industry.

## Channels for Hong Kong Firms Subcontracting to China

With respect to the preexisting social and institutional relationships between firms and the internal organizational characteristics, Hong Kong firms have four major channels to subcontract to China. These are kinship, participating, affiliated, and other local Chinese factories. Kinship factories are those that have connections with or are owned by the mainland relatives or close friends of the owners of the respective Hong Kong contractors. These factories usually provide their Hong Kong transactional counterparts greater confidence in exchange reliability, because preexisting kinship or close social relationships establish mutual trusts between the transacting parties. These factories further provide their HongKong counterparts with better local connections to resolve operational problems or to develop production networks (Smart and Smart 1991). Participating factories are those that are fully or partly owned by other Hong Kong firms besides the respective Hong Kong contractors. These factories are a direct extension of Hong Kong's industrial system, endowed with preexisting social and business relationships and customary business practices of the territory; they similarly provide their Hong Kong counterparts greater confidence in exchange reliability. These factories further reduce the investment needs of their Hong Kong counterparts, because the respective Hong Kong investors usually furnish the required production equipment and technical training. Affiliated factories are those that have business affiliations with various Chinese state-owned trading or specialized corporations. These factories do not have preexisting social or institutional ties with their respective Hong Kong contractors. The confidence they provide to their Hong Kong counterparts rests upon the perceived reputation of the state-owned corporations with which they affiliate and upon the formal contractual agreements between firms. Other local factories are those that do not have any preexisting kinship or institutional ties with their Hong Kong contractors or affiliations with Chi-

nese state-owned corporations. The transactional relationships with their Hong Kong counterparts were established through ordinary business contacts, such as company visits, or through remote social ties. The confidence they provide to their Hong Kong counterparts varies between pairs of firms and depends on the particular situations concerned. Organically, kinship and participating factories tend to be medium-sized private firms. Affiliated factories tend to be large and state-owned, whereas other local factories have mixed size and ownership characteristics.

As regards the structure of transactions among responding firms, subcontracting changes with kinship or other local factories contained exclusively direct linkages (exchange relationships involving no intermediaries). Exchanges with participating or affiliated factories, on the other hand, comprised both direct and indirect (exchange relationships involving intermediaries) linkages. The intermediaries for participating factories included Hong Kong trading firms and manufacturers with investments or business connections in China, whereas those for affiliated factories included the mainland and Hong Kong offices of various state-owned corporations. ## Locational Distribution

The propensity of firms to transact with kinship and participating factories highlights the importance of preexisting social and institutional relationships between firms in subcontracting exchanges between Hong Kong and China. These preexisting relationships, along with China's efforts to attract foreign investments, led to a concentration of Hong Kong's subcontracting activities in the Zhujiang Delta (Fig. 3), the home area of the owners of most responding firms; the kinship and social networks provide a trusted environment (Fig. 4). The Delta is also the area where most of Hong Kong's nonsubcontracting manufacturing investments are located (Leung 1990); their pres-

ence provides Hong Kong firms another reliable channel to subcontract to China. The importance of preexisting kinship and Hong Kong business ties was reflected by the frequent use of the terms *xiangxia* (hometown), *shuren* (close acquaintances), and *guanxi* (relationships) as replies to the survey questions concerning their criteria for primary subcontractor and location selection. The concentration of subcontracting linkages in the Zhujiang Delta was further attributed to the closeness of the two places, which enables Hong Kong firms to reduce transportation and delivery problems, and also to supervise the production quality of their subcontractors. The Delta locations that received most of the subcontracting activities were Shenzhen, Dongguan, and Guangzhou. Together these three locations accounted for 66 percent of the subcontracting linkages of the firms in the Delta.

## Transactional Characteristics and Interfirm Arrangements

The use of preexisting kinship and business ties and the proximity of the two places have enabled Hong Kong firms to secure reliable exchanges from their Chinese subcontractors. There was a general absence of major transactional problems between the firms and their subcontractors; only two firms expressed strong dissatisfaction, while the other thirteen indicated minor concerns about the work quality or punctuality of delivery. Most of the minor concerns did not hamper the normal functioning and continuing development of the transactional relationships. The relative reliability of exchanges has encouraged Hong Kong firms to develop a cooperative transactional relationship with their Chinese subcontractors for securing access to dependable labor supplies or export quotas. The relatively concentrated flow of work between firms reflected this co-

operation. Each responding firm subcontracted an average of 30 percent of its total output to only two subcontractors. The stability of the relationships and exchanges also showed cooperativeness. Only three firms had their key Chinese subcontractors replaced during 1981-86; most subcontracted at least once a month to their Chinese subcontractors with an average order size of US$1,000 in processing fees.

The extent of subcontracting and the size of subcontractor base varied slightly between different types of firms (Table 6). Garment and textile firms usually subcontracted a smaller portion of their output to China than electronic firms because of the rules of origin and export quota constraints. Large and medium firms also subcontracted a smaller portion of their output to China because of their relatively large base volume of production, but the absolute size of their subcontract orders was usually larger than their small-firm counterparts. There was no significant difference in the size of subcontractor base between the three industries. Except for three small export firms that had a large subcontractor base in order to gain flexibility, small firms generally had only one Chinese subcontractor because of their limited ability to coordinate production over distance. The stability of exchanges varied slightly between different types of firms. Firms that had a stable flow of production orders or plans for constantly utilizing China's labor or quotas generally subcontracted a set amount of work regularly. This was especially true for several large firms, which had formal agreements with their subcontractors for regular, fixed amounts of work. Firms that had a less stable flow of production orders, i.e., medium and small export firms, usually subcontracted on a variable size and frequency basis. This practice was reflected by the variable subcontract order characteristic of a small manufacturer of light bulbs, which, on an individual order basis, ranged from several hundred to a thousand US dollars in processing fee and from three days to a month in frequency, depending on the demand fluctuations in its North American and European markets. In some cases, the variability in order characteristics was attributed to the practice of concurrent subcontracting, as exemplified by the occasional small rush orders of a headgear manufacturer due to its inability to meet certain delivery schedules. As regards the strength of the subcontracting relations, transactional relationships that were exclusive and central to both parties were close and highly cooperative. This was especially true for several small electronics firms that had only one Chinese subcontractor as their sole branch plant catering to all or most of their production needs. Transactional relationships that were based on preexisting kinship or Hong Kong business ties were also more cooperative than the others, as the established mutual trusts or customary business practices promoted understanding and reduced conflicts between firms. Transactional relationships that were based on other contacts tended to be loosely cooperative, as most were formal and limited in scope. The differences in the strength of the relationships can be reflected by two specific examples. The first is a small manufacturer of handbags that had a kinship-

based subcontracting relationship in Shunde. The relationship was close and cooperative; the manufacturer subcontracted for all its production work to the Shunde factory; both firms worked together to resolve any operational problems. The owner of the firm was satisfied with the Shunde subcontractor and expected the relationship to continue. By comparison, the relationships between a small adaptor manufacturer and its three subcontractors were loosely cooperative. The lack of close collaboration was attributable to the absence of preexisting kinship or business ties in the exchange relationships and to the subsequent strategy of the firm to avoid overdependence on subcontractors. The provision of work to a particular subcontractor, as the owner of the firm stated, was dependent upon the available capacity of that subcontractor and its relationship with the firm. Subcontractors who were willing to meet the demands of the firm were awarded more work, given the available capacity. The loose cooperativeness was further reflected by the lack of plans for future cooperation between the firms, as the owner of the firm was dissatisfied with the short-term profit orientation of the three subcontractors. Al-though this study did not specifically gather information on the subcontractors, the manufacturers suggested that the strength of the relationships had little effect on the short-term performance of the subcontractors. The performance was related primarily to the export success of their contractors. The strength of the relationships had significant effects on the long-term development potentials of the subcontractors, as close cooperation facilitated greater diffusion and better absorption of managerial know-how and technology.

Given the lower labor costs in China, Hong Kong firms generally subcontracted labor-intensive assembly activities to their Chinese subcontractors. These activities, while routine, contained a significant amount of work that was technologically and functionally similar to those conducted in the territory. Approximately 56 percent of the key subcontracting linkages of the firms contained the same types of work that these firms undertook domestically. The technological and functional similarity of the subcontract work was due to the lack of process or product differentiation and of functional diversity of most firms, a result of their small operational scale or international or local subcontractor status. This situation was typified by a small electronic manufacturer, which itself was an international subcontractor assembling telephones for foreign buyers; it subcontracted the same production process to factories in Dongguan and Huizhou. Large and medium firms, on the other hand, usually subcontracted work that was technologically less sophisticated than their domestic production mix, as most of them had higher degrees of process or product differentiation and were increasingly oriented to high value-added production in the territory. This was especially true for large and medium garment firms as they had to upgrade their production lines in the territory to maintain profitable use of their domestic export quotas. It should be noted that the subcontract work of large or medium firms was in general technologically more sophisticated than their small-firm counterparts because of the higher value-added contents of their products. The similarities of the work subcontracted to China and that performed in the territory indicate that hinterland or distant linkages are not necessarily more routine or less skilled than center linkages. The technological and functional characteristics of vertically disintegrated activities in the hinterland or over a distance are deter-

mined not only by the costs of communications and management, but also by the position of the center in the international production system and the production strategies of the firms in the center.

The subcontracting of technologically similar work required Hong Kong firms to provide needed production equipment and technical training to their Chinese subcontractors because of the backwardness of China's industrial system. The propensity to provide equipment and training was high among garment firms, especially large ones, because most undertook product subcontracting, which generally had greater equipment and operational requirements than process subcontracting. The propensity was also high among the electronics firms, of which most were small nonexport firms, because of the technological nature of this industry. Among the four types of Chinese subcontractors, kinship, affiliated, and other local factories received strong equipment and training supports from their Hong Kong contractors. Participating factories had relatively weak equipment and training supports, as most were generally well equipped by their respective Hong Kong investors. The types of equipment provided ranged from sewing to garment finishing machines and from electronic component to computer manufacturing equipment, with approximately half of the support linkages containing new equipment.

The provision of other supports or know-how was limited. Responding firms maintained their product development, marketing, and other advanced managerial functions in the territory. This lack of provision of other supports or know-how was attributed to the inadequacy of local physical and human infrastructures in China, and to the needs to retain these advanced functions in the territory for maintaining established local and overseas contacts. The inadequacy of infrastructures further led to the lack of local input linkages associated with Hong Kong's subcontracting activities in China. Hong Kong manufacturers were usually unable to obtain quality or reliable supplies from local Chinese sources, as reflected by the failed attempt of an electronics manufacturer to purchase painting materials from local Chinese firms for its mainland subcontractors. This finding confirms the results of other studies that local supply problems are an important factor constraining the development of local input linkages of nonlocal activities (Kli-masewski 1978; McDermott 1976). Of the 54 responding firms subcontracting to China, only four had some of their materials supplied from local Chinese sources.

## Conclusion

Based on a comprehensive investigation of firms in Hong Kong's three major industries, this study found that the subcontracting activities of Hong Kong were widely distributed in Zhujiang Delta, with Shenzhen, Dongguan, and Guangzhou as the three major centers of concentration. The widespread distribution was attributed mainly to the extensive use of preexisting kinship and Hong Kong business ties to establish linkages, and to a lesser extent, the locational proximity of the two places, the efforts of Chinese authorities to attract foreign investments to the Delta, and the locational advantages or specialization of individual locales in the area. Subcontracting linkages established through preexisting kinship or business ties tended to be in county or minor city locations in the Delta, whereas those based on other contacts generally were in major urban locations. The extensive use of preexisting social and institutional ties to establish linkages has promoted rapid industrial growth in the rural Zhujiang Delta and has reduced the spatial disparity in industrial production between urban and rural areas. In terms of transactions

the incorporation of preexisting kinship or business ties has enabled Hong Kong manufacturers to secure reliable exchanges from their Chinese subcontractors, which encouraged further cooperation between the firms. Hong Kong firms were willing to subcontract a significant amount of work and to provide equipment and training supports. The work subcontracted to China contained a vast array of products and processes, which were, to a significant extent, technologically and functionally similar to those in the territory. The development of Hong Kong's subcontracting activities has therefore contributed to significant qualitative improvement of the industrial system in the Zhujiang Delta. The regional economic relationship between Hong Kong and the Delta is interdependent, with Hong Kong relying on China's low-cost labor to maintain its export competitiveness and the Delta depending on the territory's production and marketing know-how to reach overseas markets.

Given the uncertain future of the territory, the long-term development of the regional industrial system will require concerted efforts of the Chinese and the British government to maintain the confidence of the people in Hong Kong. The efforts need to include a positive and unequivocal interpretation of the Basic Law (a special constitution governing the people and the society of Hong Kong after 1997) by the Chinese government, and sincere cooperation between the two governments on major political and economic issues concerning the future development of the territory. The long-term development will also require the Hong Kong government to actively promote the indigenous product development and marketing capabilities of Hong Kong firms, and to coordinate with Guangdong's provincial and local governments for the planning and development of industries in the region. The Hong Kong government has made limited progress toward the promotion of technology-intensive industries in the territory (Federation of Hong Kong Industries 1992), but substantial efforts, especially those related to intergovernmental cooperation, are required if the viability of the system is to be maintained.

The findings of this study highlight the inadequacies of existing theories of industrial organization and location, led by Scott, in explaining the locational and structural characteristics of vertical disintegration of production (Phelps 1992; Sayer and Walker 1992). The develop-ment of vertical disintegration does not neces-
sarily promote locational agglomeration. Hin-
terland or distant linkages do not necessarily lie
in locations of low labor or land costs, nor
necessarily involve bulky or stable transactions,
nor are these linkages necessarily more routine
or less skilled than center linkages. These
findings point to the need for a theoretical re-
formulation in industrial organization and loca-
tion research. The reformulation needs to con-
sider not only the effects of transaction costs
but also the effects of interfirm relationships,
both preexisting social-institutional and sub-
sequent transactional, on the locational and
structural characteristics of vertically disinte-
grated activities. Preexisting relationships pri-
marily affect the choice of with whom and
where to invest, because firms usually need to
insure the reliability of exchanges. Subsequent
relationships, on the other hand, affect what
firms plan to do with their transactional coun-
terparts, because firms generally want to se-
cure needed resources and operational stabil-
ity. These operational considerations are im-
portant to firms, for unreliable exchanges or
instability can incur high potential linkage costs
such as losses due to production disruption.
Accordingly, in situations where potential link-
age costs are high or prohibitive, interfirm re-
lationships are likely to be a more important
linkage decision criterion than actual linkage
costs. Future theories of industrial organization
and location must therefore consider interfirm
relationships, both preexisting social-institu-
tional and subsequent transactional, as major
determinants of the locational and economic
consequences of vertical disintegration of pro-
duction.

The findings also show the inadequacies in
the field of industrial organization and location
in conceptualizing the transactional structure of
a production system. Existing perspectives,
such as the transaction costs approach, simply
view the transactional structure of a production
system as a network of linkages (Scott 1988a).
This view ignores the multitudinous characters
of firms and their transactional counterparts, or,
as Sayer (1989) noted, the vertical organiza-
tion, of a production system, hence disengag-
ing the system from its internal social-institu-
tional environment and from its external politi-
cal-economic context. The transactional struc-
ture of a production system, while complex, is
usually organized comprising a number of in-
dustrial segments of differing operational and

functional characteristics, and several transac-
tional channels of varying social and institu-
tional attributes. Given the differences in op-
erational and functional characteristics, the
channel selection strategies, interfirm transac-
tional relationships, and the subsequent spatial
and economic impacts of individual segments
are therefore likely to vary. The segmental and
channel composition of a production system
and the resultant patterns of contacts are place-
specific, reflecting the peculiar sociocultural,
political, economical, and historical organiza-
tion of production of a particular locality. The
examination of interfirm relationships and
transactional characteristics with respect to the
detailed transactional structure of a production
system can therefore provide a context-spe-
cific mesoscale explanation of the locational
and economic consequences of vertical disin-
tegration of production. By placing a firm in its
specific social and institutional environment
and by placing a production system in its
proper national and international context, the
issue of contingency and indeterminacy in in-
dustrial organization and location research can
be resolved (Lovering 1990, 1991; Scott 1991a,
b).⁶ The heterogeneity of vertically disinte-
grated production systems or "new industrial
spaces" can be better understood (Amin and
Robins 1990; Best 1990; Markusen 1991; Scott
1988b). Future studies of industrial organization
and location must therefore examine the de-
tailed transactional structure of a production
system and the subsequent contact patterns
and interfirm transactional arrangements. The
research approach must be comprehensive,
integrating the microscale decision patterns of
different types of firms in different industries to
the macroscale development processes of re-
gional economies.

There are at least three important areas that
merit further investigation. The first area con-
cerns the spatial structure of major transac-
tional channels and the associated determi-
nants. Certain channels such as kinship or affili-
ated factories in the present study are geo-
graphically noncentral or interregional in
scope. An understanding of the geographical
organization of these transactional channels
and their underlying locational determinants,
from a policy perspective, can help to promote
greater decentralization of vertically disinte-
grated activities from central to noncentral lo-
cations. The second area regards the societal
conditions that facilitate interfirm cooperation.Preexisting kinship and personal ties, while important in the Chinese context, may neither be necessary nor sufficient conditions for fostering interfirm cooperation in other social settings. An entrepreneurial culture that fulfils transactional obligations and an institutional structure that supports interfirm transactions, as other studies have shown, may assume greater importance in alternative social context (Friedman 1988; Whitley 1990). A detailed understanding of these conditions therefore will enable successful development of interfirm cooperation and of vertically disintegrated production systems in a given social and political environment. The third area concerns the evolution of the transactional structure of a production system. The importance of kinship-based transactional channels may decline through time as other avenues develop because of the advances in transportation and telecommunications. An examination of the evolution of the transactional structure will therefore allow assessments of the changes in center-hinterland economic relations through time. The relationships between firms and the structures of transactions as major locational and structural determinants of vertical disintegration of production are of growing theoretical and empirical importance, as firms increasingly seek to establish strategically-allied linkages with the others.
