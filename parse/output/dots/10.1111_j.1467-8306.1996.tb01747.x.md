Countryside Recreational Access in the
United States: A Statistical Comparison
of Rural Districts

Hugh Millward

Department of Geography, Saint Mary's University

In the period since 1945 the demand for recreational sites and activities has increased greatly in all developed nations as a consequence of both increased leisure time and greater mobility (Hillman 1984; Pigram 1983; Simmons 1975:10-15). These changes notwithstanding, our knowledge of recreational areas and their accessibility to the public remains modest. In particular, little attention has been focused on settled rural areas—the countryside—which afford the most extensive and most varied spaces for informal outdoor recreation.

Most outdoor recreational activity takes place within a few hours' travel of major urban centers rather than in isolated areas of wilderness (Glyptis 1991:106-108; Greer and Wall 1979; Travis and Veal 1976). Yet despite the importance of readily accessible "everyday" or "vernacular" landscapes as recreation resources in the United States (Aschmann 1969; Jackson 1984; Penning-Rowsell and Lowenthal 1986; Wilson 1989), there has been little empirical research on recreational access within such areas. While geographers have thoroughly studied recreation in the British countryside, often with emphasis on planning and policy implications (Glyptis 1991), there is no comparable body of work for the United States' countryside.

Demand for "intensive-use" or "user-oriented" activities such as swimming, camping, golf, or downhill skiing can be accommodated in limited spaces through the provision of specific facilities, often of a commercial nature (Bryant, Russwurm, and McLellan 1982:125-140). But the more popular "extensive-use" activities, termed "intermediate" and "resource-based" (Clawson and Knetsch 1960), require extensive tracts of land and/or landscape as a

recreational resource. Such activities (e.g., automobile touring, walking, cycling, canoeing, cross-country skiing, snowmobiling, fishing, and hunting) typically involve movement as an integral part of the recreational experience, hence the provision of publicly accessible routes and zones within the countryside becomes an important component on the supply side of the recreational equation.

The aim of this study is to gauge the supply of roads, tracks, lands, and waterways that are available for public recreational movement in representative rural districts of the United States. The concept of access rigor will be employed to categorize routes and zones according to the recreational groups capable of using them and to identify regional and local variations in access availability and the reasons for them.

## Access Supply, Land Ownership, and Land Value

In a recent paper, the author outlined a conceptual framework which positioned recreational access in a broad spectrum of environmental and cultural settings and suggested measures of access supply suitable for statistical analysis (Millward 1991). The concepts and measures were applied to 13 Canadian countryside districts (Millward 1992) and 15 West European districts (Millward 1993); in each study regional variations in the quality and quantity of public access were identified and related to intensity of land use, amplitude of relief, the type of natural vegetation, and the history of land settlement and survey. This paper on recreational access in the United States complements these earlier surveys. Given thesize and diversity of the United States, regional variations in recreational access provision are expected to be considerable; older and more densely settled regions such as the Northeast should resemble the West European (and particularly British) case, while recently settled lands in the West should have more in common with areas of western Canada.

Recreational access is supplied or provided wherever the public may freely use routes or zones for recreational movement (Cloke and Park 1985:334). The amount supplied is related to the geographic extent of opportunities for recreation, irrespective of the degree of utilization. Access routes include roads (paved and unpaved), tracks, paths, and waterways, while access zones range from public parks to areas of exploitation (e.g., cropland and pasture) and natural areas (e.g., bush, forest, beach, barren). The two categories often overlap in that access zones may be criss-crossed with pathways, while linear features such as rivers, beaches, and lakes may be regarded as either routes or zones depending on scale. The two categories are also complementary in that routes allow movement to zones, while adjacent zones increase the recreational value of routes.

In the case of both routes and zones, public access to recreation may be assured or prohibited by right (de jure), or by custom, convention, or permission/denial (de facto) (Millward 1991:243; Sidaway 1986:26). In this study, routes and zones are considered open to the public if there is either de jure or de facto access and closed if there are legal, physical, monetary, or psychological barriers to access.

## Access Routes

The network of public roads and tracks allows physical and visual entry into the countryside even when adjacent lands are barred. For many motorists, a "trip in the country" is a leisure pursuit in its own right (Patmore 1974:7-71; 1987). Access is increased in areas of high agricultural value, because such areas require a dense network of exploitation roads—most of which become public rights-of-way. Access is also affected by the scale of original landholdings: an area with smaller landholdings has a finer cadastral mesh which requires more public roads. Landholding size is

often related in turn to the period of settlement and to the character of earlier farming systems. The shape of original landholdings also affects the subsequent availability of public access routes: in formal land survey systems, compact parcels require denser road networks than do long lots of the same size, particularly when the latter are ranged in double-front fashion (Thrower 1966). Irregular surveys (metes and bounds) also tend to have lower road densities. Even where subsequent farm enlargement and/or farmland abandonment reduces the economic justification for a dense road network, rights-of-way are likely to remain as residual elements in the landscape, and hence as routes of access for public recreational use.

## Access Zones

One third of the United States is federal land, most of which is accessible to the public (Garrett 1985:40-41. See Clawson 1972; 1983; and Wolf 1981 for detailed descriptions of federal landownership and access policies). These lands, however, are almost entirely located beyond the recreational hinterlands of urban America; although important as vacation resources, they rarely satisfy demands for day-use or weekend recreation.

More accessible for recreational purposes is land around cities. This land is often used intensively for agriculture and is higher in value. Theoretically, high-value land is more likely to be in private ownership and protected against trespass (Bromley 1991:141-7). Lands with high economic value for production, privacy, or game are usually guarded through fencing, posting, and threats of prosecution or even physical harm. By contrast, where land is plentiful and cheap, owners spend less time and money fencing or policing their lands; uninhabited users thus may acquire de facto (and in some jurisdictions de jure) access to the land. The entrenchment of legal or customary rights acquired during a period when population pressure and land values are low may cause friction with subsequent increases in recreational pressures.

Access Demand and
Recreational Specialization

The demand for recreational access varies inversely with distance from the city (Glyptis 1991:106-108) and directly with the attractiveness of a locality for a range of popular recreational pursuits. In general, the most popular activities are non-specialized and require ready access by car. Bryan (1977:175) defines recreation specialization as a continuum of behavior from the general to the particular as reflected in the equipment and skills needed, and the activity settings (sites or locales) required. As specialization increases, users require more specific settings (contrast the needs of walkers or cyclists with those of canoeists or rock-climbers), and are more concerned to avoid crowding and disturbance (Hollenhorst 1990:82). In terms of access, therefore, specialization often translates into the distance one is willing to move away from one's car and the difficulty or rigor of that movement. Clark and Stankey (1979) suggested four levels of access difficulty, and subsequent refinements to their scheme have specified up to 15 levels (Lichtkoppler and Clonts 1990; U.S. Department of Agriculture, Forest Service 1986). Almost all of these access levels relate to the type and quality of routes, and most require field identification, so that they are applicable only in relatively small study areas (e.g., Buist and Hoots 1982; Daniels and Krannich 1990).

Millward (1991:242-244) has suggested five
levels of access *rigor* vis-à-vis modes of move-
ment and the types of routes and zones over
which movement occurs. These are:

*Passive access.* This is the least-rigorous level of access. Movement is restricted to routes accessible year-round by automobiles or to areas of "easy going" (e.g., short grass or sand beaches) within 100 m of such routes. This level is employed by the largest proportion of recreationists and in the great majority of recreational trips.

*Casual access.* In this case, recreationists move more than 100 m from the road, making short forays on tracks and footpaths or on off-track areas of easy going for no more than a one-hour round-trip and a distance of 3 km from the road.

*Vigorous access.* At this level, recreationistsmove between 3 and 20 km from the road (the latter being a day's round-trip), yet remain on tracks, paths, or areas of easy going. Vigorous users seek specific recreational activities that require either physical training (e.g., hiking, Nordic skiing, mountain biking) or the purchase of expensive equipment (e.g., all-terrain vehicle, snowmobile).

*Rugged access.* This level involves movement of 100 m or more into areas of hard going, yet within 3 km of roads or paths and 20 route-kilometers of an all-season road. By trekking into the bush, meadow, or barrens, or by navigating a lake or river, the recreationist gains access to zones of activity, often for hunting and fishing. Included in the rugged zone are lakes, navigable rivers, and sheltered coastal waters 3 km or less from shore.

*Arduous access.* The most rigorous access level is defined by zones that are 20 route-kilometers or more from an all-season road and beyond 3 km from other route-ways, i.e., in truly remote wilderness. Movement is also arduous in particularly ill-favored terrain within these distance cut-offs, such as marsh, swamp, permanent ice, scree, cliff, and very steep slopes (over 35 degrees). The arduous zone is effectively closed to all but a handful of travellers since access generally requires overnight camping in the wild. Activities in this zone include wilderness trekking, mountaineering, and guided hunting and fishing trips.

## The Study Areas: Selection and Appraisal

The only feasible way of measuring access availability nationwide is through the use of topographic maps combined with field verification. Since the great bulk of recreational activity takes place in the settled or semi-settled countryside, our interest focuses on these areas. More specifically, the study areas selected are located in regions having continuous blocks of ecumene with rural densities exceeding two people per square kilometer (as shown in Figure 1) (Espenshade and Morrison 1986:86). Extensive areas of desert, mountain,

and northern forest are excluded from study, as are regions containing only small pockets of scattered settlement.

To facilitate analysis and permit comparison with other studies, the United States was divided into five regions differentiated with respect to their natural vegetation, dates of settlement, and culture. The five units range in size from 100,000 to 500,000 square km, and their boundaries are drawn through thinly populated areas or along major rivers (Figure 1). Within each region, three representative settled areas were selected, each being 1,000 square kilometers in area (40 km by 25 km). The study areas met three criteria; they are: 1) largely rural with a few tracts of urban or suburban development; 2) mixed in topography, water features, land cover, and settlement types, while accentuating features typical of the local setting; and 3) representative of the landscape mix in each region and of the nation as a whole. The fifteen selected areas, which are named after their major settlement, are mapped in Figure 1 and listed in Tables 1 and 2.

The study areas in the Northeast afford a variety of topography (see amplitude of relief, Table 2), ranging from coastal plain (Plymouth, Massachusetts) to the incised Allegheny Plateau (Corning, New York). They also vary in settlement vintage, from the early 1600s (Plymouth), to the early 1700s (Red Lion, Pennsylvania), to the early 1800s (Corning). Irregular survey schemes and road layouts are typical in all three areas. Pennsylvania's Red Lion is the most intensely agricultural, with a strong orientation to dairy farming. Plymouth and Corning are less agricultural owing to the reversion of farmland to forest—a process that has been extensive in Plymouth and moderate in Corning (see percent land cover, Table 2). Plymouth has also experienced considerable exurban development.

The three Southern study areas exemplify the region's diversity. Danville (Kentucky) was settled earliest (c. 1790); it occupies a portion of the well-settled Bluegrass Plateau in the north (mostly livestock and tobacco farming) and a section of the indented and heavily wooded Cumberland Plateau in the south. Statesboro (Georgia) includes swampy coastal plain in the southeast and gently rolling coastal terrace in the northwest. The latter was well-settled in the early 1800s, and remains in mixed farming. Clarksdale (Mississippi) lies entirely in the Yazoo Basin floodplain (the Delta), which is heavily farmed for cotton. Both Statesboro and Danville exhibit irregular surveys, while Mississippi's Clarksdale area, settled around 1840, has a modified version of the sectional survey.

On the Great Plains, Meridian (Texas) was originally open woodland, while Bedford (Iowa) and Healy (Kansas) were tall-grass and short-grass prairie, respectively. Meridian and

Bedford were settled at moderate densities around 1850, while Healy's more marginal environment was thinly settled in the 1880s. Sectional surveys were used in all three areas, though with many irregularities in the hillier sections of Meridian. Farming specializations are beef cattle (Meridian), feed-grain (Bedford), and wheat (Healy).

The California study areas exemplify the naturally wooded coastal valleys and hills (Watsonville) and the natural grasslands of the Sacramento Valley (Maxwell) and San Joaquin Valley (Delano). Lowland portions of Watsonville, settled around 1860, are characterized by fruit and truck farming with some exurban development. The Maxwell and Delano areas, settled by means of sectional survey around 1870 and 1880, respectively, are now irrigated for orchard and field crops.

Topographic maps at a scale of 1:24,000 were used to identify all routeways in the study areas and to categorize them by access rigor. Hard or improved-surface roads, including light duty roads, were classed as passive, except short lanes leading to a single house or farmstead, which were assumed to be private roads. Unimproved dirt roads, tracks, and trails were classed as casual or vigorous depending on distance from an improved road (the 3 km and 20 km cut-offs described earlier), again excepting private lanes. Waterways wide enough to be shown by double lines on the map were eligible for designation as rugged provided they had vehicle access. Further details of the method are provided in Millward (1991:244-246).

To identify off-route zones open to the public (and thus generally rugged or arduous), certain assumptions were made regarding *de facto* access, and corroborated through literature review and correspondence with state and federal agencies (U.S. National Park Service, Bureau of Land Management, and Forest Service, and a selection of state departments responsible for state parks, state forests, and fish and game). Field verification was undertaken in the Corning, Red Lion, Great Smoky, Danville, and Lancaster study areas in the spring of 1990; the author also drew on the experience of earlier field excursions in many parts of the country. Detailed guidelines were then prepared for gauging probable *de facto* access from map evidence. To summarize, lands open to public access include national parks (and variants), state parks, national and state forests, game

sanctuaries, and preserves, as well as woodlands, barrens, marsh, and unfenced rangeland 500 m or more from a building (a rule of thumb derived from fieldwork) and accessible from a public route over other open land. Off-route zones identified as open to the public were classed as rugged or arduous, depending on the cut-offs and conditions described earlier. As for closed lands, these include military property, lands demarcated for other government facilities, airports, docks, urban-industrial lands, and clear (white) areas associated with farms or other settlement, including orchards and golf courses.

In intensely settled areas, exemplified by Red Lion in Figure 2b, a dense network of public roads allows nearly ubiquitous passive access, but the prevalence of private farmland affords little opportunity for off-road recreation (a few tracks and a stretch of the wide Susquehanna River). All off-trail land is open to the public, and is mostly in the rugged category, with steeper slopes classed as arduous.

## Example Access Patterns


Access was determined for each counter on a nominal basis of presence or absence, beginning with the easiest mode (passive), and then testing for increasingly rigorous modes. In the case of route access, a counter must simply contain a section of the appropriate type of public route, while for zonal access (rugged and arduous) a majority of the counter must be open to the public. Each counter was assigned to the easiest available rigor category, and if none were available, it was classed as "closed." For each quadrat the percentage of counters (and hence of area) in each rigor category was then calculated.

## Access Profiles

Table 2 summarizes access measures for all 20 study areas. The table reports mean quadrat percentages for five levels of access (passive through arduous) and for areas closed to the public. The "access profile" for a district or region is provided by the percentage distribution of counters within this descending sequence of access modes. For the United States as a whole (all districts), three modes are well represented (passive, rugged, and closed, with 33, 24, and 24 percent of area, respectively), and three are much less common (casual, arduous, and vigorous, with 12, 5, and 3 percent). However, this national average represents neither the typical settled area nor the typical park; indeed, only two areas—Watsonville and Meridian—resemble the mean access

distribution for the U.S. These two areas are topographically varied and economically marginal.

Strong contrasts are evident in the access profiles for settled districts and parks. Consider parks. Only 7 percent of their area is accessible by car (passive), but in compensation there is greater provision of tracks and trails, which increase access to back-country areas by the vigorous mode (8 percent). By their nature, parklands are almost entirely open to cross-country travel; hence rugged access is plentiful (60 percent). In mountainous parks, however, much terrain falls into the arduous level of access (30 percent in Sequoia). In contrast, settled districts are typified by high road access and large proportions of closed lands—53 percent on average. Passive access (car) prevails in 46 percent of the counters, casual access (non-car routes) in 12 percent, and rugged access (cross-country) in 6 percent. Passive access is greatest in the Northeast, followed by the Midwest, South, California, and Great Plains. Regions and districts with lower passive access tend to have more casual access, though Plymouth in the Northeast has high levels of both.

By definition, vigorous access is almost non-existent in settled areas, so that its mean value rounds to zero. Rather, off-road access in such areas is almost entirely casual or rugged. Casual access averages 12 percent, albeit with noticeably lower figures in the Midwest (3 percent) and notably higher ones in California (19 percent). Districts with little casual access typically contain intensive agriculture and smaller farms (e.g., Red Lion, Portage, Bedford). Rugged access averages 6 percent in settled districts, with only the Midwest varying to any degree (3 percent). The rugged percentage rises in districts with stronger relief, greater forest cover, or lower settlement density (e.g., Watsonville, Meridian, Corning, and Statesboro).

The proportion of closed land (no public access) is highest in areas which are fully farmed, settled later, and coarse-grained in their cadastral survey. The latter results in larger landholdings served by fewer public roads (access for land exploitation being supplemented by private farm tracks). Examples are Healy and Bedford on the Great Plains, Maxwell and Delano in California, and, less explicably, Danville, Kentucky.

**Figure 2.** Access routes and zones in portions of five selected study areas. The areas illustrate a range of access regimes varying by type, availability, and pattern.## Weighted Index of Access Availability

The potential for access is of little use if few people wish to take advantage of it. In this sense, the more rigorous modes of access are less "available" to the general public than are easier and more leisurely modes. To weight supply by potential demand, the percentage of counters at each level of access rigor is multiplied by an estimate of the proportion of the public willing to participate at that level. Based on conceptual considerations (Millward 1991; Figure 2) and empirical evidence (e.g., Glyptis 1991:106-121; Simmons 1975:26-29), these weights are set at 1.0 (passive), 0.5 (casual), 0.25 (vigorous), 0.1 (rugged), and 0.02 (arduous). Closed lands are weighted at zero, though it is recognized that they may host more recreational activity (through trespass) than do remote areas with legal but arduous access. Summing for each quadrat the weighted percentages in each mode yields a single index of access availability which ranges from zero to one hundred.

Weighted availability indices averaged for each district, park, and region appear in Table 2. They confirm many of the points already noted. Note that access availability is high in the intensively settled Northeast and Midwest (averages of 67 and 57, respectively) below-average in the more thinly settled areas of the Plains and California (41 and 46), and intermediate (52) in the South (which is similar to the national average of 53 for settled districts). In the settled districts, index values range from 74 (Red Lion) to 31 (Healy). The latter's low score, like that of some other districts with unfenced cropland, reflects summer conditions: during winter, when most closed land would shift to the rugged category, this district's score would increase to around 36.

With the exception of Shenandoah, national parks all have weighted scores below those of settled districts. Paradoxically, therefore, but inherent in their opposed functions of conservation versus recreation (Lemons and Stout 1982; Runte 1987), the national parks provide measure access for the general public (who restrict themselves to peripheral and often rationed "honeypot" areas) while reserving the great bulk of their area for a small minority of specialized or expert recreationists. Sequoia is the most extreme in this regard: its weighted score of 12 is remarkably consistent with Yoho in the

Canadian Rockies and La Vanoise in the French Alps (Millward 1992; 1993).

## Statistical Analysis of Access Determinants

Quadrat scores for each access mode may be regarded as dependent variables, and related to such independent variables as settlement density, topography, and land cover. These variables were analyzed statistically because they can be measured simply and objectively on topographic maps; they cannot be viewed, however, as the only determinants. Various forms of regression analysis were performed, but owing to the non-linear nature of almost all relationships, multiple regression proved to be an inappropriate technique. Fourth-order polynomial regressions were found to offer a good approximation of the curvilinear relationships and these serve as the focus of the following discussion.

## Land Cover

Topographic maps at 1:24,000 scale were used to measure the three main categories of land cover (woodland, open country, and built-up areas). The woodland and built-up

categories are homogenous, each with fairly uniform economic (i.e., land use), physical, and visual character. Open or clear land, however, though typically farmland, may also be natural grasslands, semi-natural range, badlands, alpine meadow, or bare rock. This presents some problems of interpretation. For each quadrat, the percentage area in each cover category was estimated by summing the number of counters dominated by the cover type. Mean figures for districts and regions appear in Table 2. Overall, 66 percent of all study areas is in open cover, 33 percent is wooded, and only 1 percent is built-up. The national parks are more wooded than open (59 percent to 41 percent), while settled areas are highly open (78 percent). The Northeast, being the least agricultural region, has an even mix of woodland and open cover (50 and 47 percent, respectively).

## Road Density (Intensity of Land Use)

It was earlier argued that the degree to which land is productively utilized affects its economic valuation by owners, and hence the degree of access they are willing to allow the public. The intensity of land use may be gauged indirectly from the presence and density of buildings and roads on topographic maps. The ubiquity of buildings is a problematic measure, since it is much affected by cadastral and settlement patterns (notably, whether farmsteads are nucleated or dispersed). Rural roads, however, almost all originated to enable land exploitation, irrespective of settlement pattern. The index of passive access is a direct measure of road density (it shows the percentage of counters containing a section of all-season road), and thus is an

excellent surrogate measure of the intensity of land use. When viewed in this light, it may be regarded as an independent variable affecting other modes of countryside access. At one end of the intensity scale, wilderness regions with no roads will by definition offer largely rugged or arduous access. As all-season roads become more prevalent (i.e., as land-use intensity increases), an increasing proportion of land area should shift to easier access modes, though we also expect more land to be closed to the public.

When access supply is weighted by estimates of demand, we find that access availability is highest in the intensely settled North-east, low in the Great Plains, and extremely low in the national parks.

The average access profile in the United States is intermediate between those of Western Europe and Canada. For settled districts, the weighted index of accessibility is 53, as compared to 73 in Western Europe (Millward 1993) and 42 in Canada (Millward 1992). It is worth noting, however, that the Northeast scores more highly than Great Britain. At most levels of land-use intensity (as measured by road density), the United States offers more opportunities for casual access than Canada, but less than Europe. The amount of land closed to the public is greater than in either Canada or Western Europe at low density levels, but intermediate between the two at higher density levels.

Polynomial regressions indicate strong relationships between the availability of access types and the independent (but interrelated)

variables of topography, land cover, and road density. Though correlations between local relief and the access categories are generally high, the key determinant of access in all except mountainous areas is the intensity of rural land use, as indicated by road density. In intensely utilized areas, road access is plentiful, but there are few trails and almost no zonal or cross-country access. Landowners find it worthwhile to exclude the public from lands having high economic value, and particularly necessary to do so in areas of high recreational demand. Lightly settled districts, by contrast, provide more opportunities for cross-country travel. The observed relationships of access to land cover and topography also may be explained by "land-use intensity," in that farming activity is more likely in areas of low relief and, in natural woodlands at least, corresponds spatially to areas of open land cover. In natural grasslands, even land of low intrinsic value may be used productively, so that cross-country access is denied even where properties are large and roads are few. Many legal and customary rights and constraints related to public access may be viewed as outcomes of the land-value factor (Bromley 1991:143).

Several other variables seem to affect the degree and type of public access, but these were not investigated statistically. Variables such as the date of settlement, the type of land allotment and survey, the original size of landholdings, and the dominant type of farming (grouped under the label "scale of original landholdings") can be seen as having an effect separate from land-use intensity, though often interrelated with it. For a given level of intensity, large landholdings require a coarser cadaster with fewer public rights-of-way; this is reflected in less access of the passive and casual types, and more closed land. Original landholdings were smallest in areas settled earliest, particularly in the Northeast, since the technology of the time limited the area exploitable by a single family. Where family labor was supplemented by slaves or hired hands, particularly in the South, original holdings were often larger; the same applies in regions settled for mechanized agriculture (particularly the Plains and California). In areas where farm enlargement and farmland abandonment have subsequently reduced the economic justification for dense public access networks, roads and tracks remain as residual features available to the public. In a more egalitarian society, pow-erful landowners have been less able to erase rights-of-way than they were in eighteenth-century Britain (Millward 1993); that said, landowners in high-value areas have probably encountered considerably on customary footpaths.

The empirical research reported here should underscore the importance of roads as resources for informal outdoor recreation. Although much of the countryside in the United States contains few trails and little cross-country access, it does contain a dense and little-used network of local roads. The recreational potential of this network for passive and casual movement could be more fully realized by policies that provide interpretive information, signposting, roadside pull-offs, and rest areas. Policymakers might also address the lack of cross-country access in settled areas: land trusts are one important means by which wider access may be gained (Wright 1993), while a second is the British policy of negotiated access agreements (Beard 1994).

Millward, Hugh. 1996. Countryside Recreational Access in the United States: A Statistical Comparison of Rural Districts. *Annals of the Association of American Geographers* 86(1):102-122. *Abstract*.

This comparative study employs the concept of physical rigor to map and measure recreational routes and zones available to the public. The comparison is made for 20 representative countryside districts in five regional settings (Northeast, Midwest, South, Great Plains, and California). Five of the districts are national parks, while the rest are 40 x 25 km settled areas chosen to typify landscapes of their region.

Statistical measures of access availability are derived from 1:24,000 topographic maps for five levels of access rigor. Access was determined for small quadrats (500 m square) on a presence/absence basis, beginning with the easiest access mode (passive) and then testing for increasingly rigorous modes (casual, vigorous, rugged, arduous, and closed). Polynomial regressions show strong relationships between levels of access and the independent variables of topography, land cover, road density (a surrogate for land-use intensity), and natural vegetation. In highly utilized areas of low relief there is plentiful access via the road network, but few trails, and much closed land. By contrast, remote wilderness areas, exemplified by national parks, offer little road access, but provide ubiquitous cross-country access for a small minority of specialized or expert recreationists.

Regional variations in access availability are also tentatively related to the scale of original landholdings, in turn related both to date of settlement and type of land allotment and survey. For given levels of land-use intensity, areas of large-scale landholdings have fewer public rights-of-way, and more land closed to the public. **Key Words:** access, countryside, landowners, public, recreation, United States.
