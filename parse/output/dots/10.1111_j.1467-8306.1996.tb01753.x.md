# The 1948 Israeli-Palestinian War and its Aftermath: The Transformation and De-Signification of Palestine's Cultural Landscape

Ghazi Falah

Department of Geography, University of Wales, Lampeter

Change in the cultural landscape generally occurs slowly; unless there are good reasons for altering the visible appearance of a place, it continues to preserve its original features. Having invested effort, money, and time in these livable places, inhabitants are usually unreceptive to radical change. Indeed places even resist death and cultural dissolution as when, for example, residents displaced by war return to their destroyed homes and make a new start. This attachment to habitations is heightened perhaps because their appearance merges into the texture of everyday life. From this perspective, changes in the cultural landscape tend to be evolutionary rather than revolutionary because places are malleable and change with the surrounding landscape.

Various empirical studies serve as guideposts for a humanistic approach to place and landscape (Cosgrove and Jackson 1987; Gregory and Ley 1988; Agnew and Duncan 1989; Cosgrove and Daniels 1988; Baker and Biger 1992; Duncan and Ley 1993a). As Norton pointed out “[a] cultural landscape can be seen as the end product or, more correctly, the dynamic product of many individual decisions made at different times within social and physical environments” (1989:76). These decisions can be attributed to the “actions and attitudes” of ideologically motivated individuals or societies (Baker 1992:7). By viewing ideologies as a manifestation of authority, Baker reminds us that “[i]deologies compete with each other, ...” that “a given society and landscape may have several different systems of symbolic representations existing within it simultaneously and antagonistically” (1992:4-5). Landscape thus may be conceived as the outcome of a

struggle among conflicting interest groups seeking domination over an immediate environment. When one group imposes its well-being over the places of another, the former completely or partially replaces the landscape symbols of the latter. In such cases, landscape (re)creation requires an appreciation of the ideological “sediment” (Duncan and Duncan 1988) buried beneath the layer deposited by the last group in power. But that reading alone does not free the interpreter from distortion (Stock 1993). Pivotal questions require answers prior to generalization: Who is the reader? In which discourse is a representation made? As Ley and Duncan conclude: “any presentation of a landscape, whether popular or scholarly, is best thought of as a representation, that is, a construction that is contingent, partial and unfinished” (1993:329; emphasis in original). Indeed, the association of ideology with landscape has sparked renewed interest in landscapes and their translations into metaphors of text, theater, and spectacle (Duncan and Duncan 1988; Cosgrove and Daniels 1989; Gregory and Ley 1988; Ley and Olds 1988; Cosgrove 1990). These studies share one common denominator—a critical reading of landscape in search of alternative meanings.

The case under scrutiny here is the exodus/expulsion of the Palestinian people during the 1948 war that preceded the establishment of State of Israel. The act of expulsion is conceptualized as part of an Israeli strategy of “total war” that targeted both Palestinian civilian population and their landscapes. In a total war, an entire society is mobilized (economically, politically, and ideologically) in support of the military struggle (Shaw 1987:151). Yet the 1948 war did not end this process. Mindful of thecentrality of control of the land, landscape, and its meanings for political hegemony, Israeli authorities pursued a strategy which, by removing the past cultural traces of other peoples from the landscape, undercut and weakened Palestinian claims to this territory, i.e., a strategy of "de-signification." A cultural landscape that reflected hundreds of years of subsequent occupance was eradicated in some four decades.

In this process of cultural landscape transformation, one party systematically attempted to eliminate the other's attachment to their habitat. Places that were the loci for Palestinian culture and national identity, the vessels of a collective memory of the region's palimpsest-like cultural landscape, were obliterated in acts of de-signification. Understanding that process involves an articulation of factors central to Israeli ideology and political claims to the land of Palestine. Israel's denial of the reality of the Palestinian connection to their homeland has a long history,¹ which continues in reconstituted form even as the peace process moves forward. Toward these ends, this paper addresses two interrelated themes: first, the war's uprooting of people from their places; second, the political strategy obliterating vacated places. With these themes in mind, the analysis is organized as follows: after presenting the conceptual framework of total war, I review the controversial historiography of the 1948 Israeli-Palestinian war and then discuss the nature of this war with respect to its civilian aspect. The paper then turns to an in-depth analysis of the spatial pattern of depopulation in one region, i.e., the Safad sub-district. The ensuing section concentrates on another kind of war aimed against the "enemy's" former places, i.e., the 418 depopulated Palestinian villages left behind. I use field research data to assess the level of destruction at the village sites over the past forty years and to report on the current status and utility of the remaining public structures and holy places. A concluding section sums up the findings and their implications. It should be pointed out that the Israeli strategy of removing the cultural imprint of another group from the landscape is not unique. In post-World War II Czechoslovakia, for example, a similar process of landscape erasure has unfolded: "The desire to eradicate all signs of the centuries-long presence of Germans in the Sudetenland led not only to the prohibition of German place names

and removal of German signs and street name-plates, but in some places even to the removal of German inscriptions on tomb stones" (Schieder 1960:91).

## Conceptual Framework: Total War, "Ethnic Cleansing," and Place Annihilation

Three concepts of war provide the theoretical framework for this paper. As the term implies, total war involves unrestrained violence (Liddell-Hart 1967:9) that often produces large-scale devastation (Shaw 1987:151). The concept thus contains elements of both "ethnic cleansing" and place annihilation. Historic buildings and other treasures of civilization are subject to destruction. From a strictly military perspective, according to Gyorgy, "[g]reat cities, densely populated capitals have become the focal points of new war strategy, mainly on account of their helplessness and poor defensibility." (1943:359). Total war differs significantly from "limited war" in which adversaries agree to the rules of engagement (Aron 1954; Edmonds 1972:11-13) and with the probable levels of damage they are prepared to accept. In the case of total war, the rules designed to protect civilian populations are callously violated in many respects (Liddell-Hart 1967:61). Civilian populations are often directly targeted because military leaders regard them as decisive for the collapse of the enemy. A case in point is the strategy of a "starvation blockade." This essentially inhumane method of warfare has its greatest impact on non-combatants, especially the weak and the old; it functions to undermine the resistance of the opposing armies by inflicting misery on their families (Liddell-Hart 1967:63). In short, these means are justified by the ultimate end of victory.

"Ethnic cleansing" is a key component of total war. It targets specific population groups (Bell-Fialkoff 1993; Cox and Eibner 1993) and takes many forms. In the Middle Ages, religious cleansing sought to "purify" the society of "nonbelievers." In modern times, cleansing has targeted "politically unreliable" segments of the population for forced resettlement or expulsion. In cases where the aggressor seeks the ultimate aim of purifying the space of "aliengroups," expulsion orders may be issued without warning and implemented within a short time. Some of these orders may be aimed at terrorizing the largely unarmed and defenseless civilian population. In such situations, massacre and rape of women are not uncommon, and may be carried out by civilian combatants and/or the regular government troops (Szaz 1960:94). Because of the inhumane aspect of such atrocities, governments and military leaders usually decline to admit war-crime responsibility once the war has been terminated.

"Ethnic cleansing" is currently taking place in Bosnia-Hercegovina where Muslims are being driven from their homes (Meron 1993; Bell-Fialkoff 1993; Pfaff 1993). There are a number of other such cases in the present century where complete settlements have been wiped out or houses abandoned to decay or reoccupation by others, while the original inhabitants have little alternative but to go in to exile. Ethnic cleansing reached its height with the Nazi campaigns against the Jews (1933-1945)—campaigns that were intended to remove Jews from the territories of the Reich (Bell-Fialkoff 1993:114). The Armenians of Turkey's eastern provinces experienced a similar fate in 1915 (Walker 1980; Ternon 1981:209-219). The expulsion of the German population from the territories east of the Oder-Neisse-Line (East Germany and Poland) and Czechoslovakia toward the end of World War II is also a well-documented case (Schieder n.d.; 1960). Less well-known is the expulsion of 500,000 Asians from Uganda in 1972 (Twaddle 1975; Ramchandani 1976). To underscore the universal nature of these tragic events does not imply that I condone their perpetrators nor wish to console (or downplay the burden of) one group of victims by citing the suffering of others. Such events should be known and documented in order to prevent their repetition.

Total war also involves the annihilation of living space i.e., of place (Hewitt 1983; 1987). In studying these annihilations, it is important to differentiate between places that were annihilated as side effects of the battlefield (i.e., a village caught in the midst of heavy fighting is devastated by the fire of both armies) and those that were destroyed as a result of military strategies deliberately designed to devastate civilian and non-military areas. Warfare during the American Civil War was often of this latter type (Weigley 1973:128-152; Liddell-Hart

1967:68). In either case, places that shelter human beings or symbolize their existence are erased. The 1948 Israeli-Palestinian war, described in Israeli literature as "Israel's War of Independence" and in Palestinian (or Arab) writing as "al-Nakba" (the catastrophe or disaster), arguably offers an instance of total war characterized by ethnic cleansing and place annihilation, i.e., landscape erasure. In this case, Jewish and Israeli forces were mobilized in behalf of a total war against the majority Palestinian population. Palestinian places consequently experienced significant devastation and depopulation during the 1948 war and into 1949.

## The 1948 al-Nakba War Revisited

The 1948 war uprooted the Palestinian people from their places. Palestinians were radi-cally and involuntarily separated from their habitations, and sections of their homeland were totally evacuated. Two key terms, "fled" and "expelled," offer a clue to the differing interpretations (and ideological stances) of Is-raelis and Palestinians (Glazer 1980; Hitchens 1988). Palestinian and some Western scholars (including Jews) have attributed the Palestinian "exodus" to deliberate action on the part of the Zionist leadership in Palestine during the war. Most Israeli commentators claimed meanwhile that Palestinians "fled," in response to appeals by Arab leaders. The latter thus bore responsibility for depopulation (e.g., via radio messages broadcast from various Arab capitals ordering the flight of most of the Palestinians; see Glazer 1980:97-100). Proof of this claim has never been furnished, however (Palumbo 1987; Hitchens 1988). In an attempt to document the Arab role in depopulation, the Irish journalist Dr. Erskine Childers conducted a meticulous search. Visiting Israel in 1958 as the guest of the Foreign Office, he asked to see the proof of the Arab radio broadcasts. "I was assured they existed, and was promised them. None had been offered when I left, but I was again assured. I asked to have the material sent on to me. I am still waiting" (Childers 1971:796). Childers then decided that the only scholarly recourse was to test the undocumented charge by other means. He found that the BBC had monitored all Middle East broadcasts throughout 1948, and that the transcripts,corroborated by those from an American
monitoring unit, were available at the British
Museum. Childers read them all and con-
cluded: "There was not a single order, or ap-
peal, or suggestion about evacuation from Pal-
estine from any Arab radio station, inside or
outside Palestine, in 1948. There is repeated
monitored record of Arab appeals, even flat
orders, to the civilians of Palestine to stay put"
(italics in the original) (Childers 1971:798). A
similar conclusion had been reached earlier by
Khalidi (1959; 1961). In his review of official
papers of the Arab Higher Committee, Khalidi
furnished evidence that Arab and Palestinian
leaders ordered Palestinian natives to stay in
their places. Khalidi also noted a Zionist war
plan known as Plan Dalet (Plan D) which in-
volved 13 military operations aimed at driving
Palestinians from their villages. Some of the
code names given to these operations—Matate
(Broom), and Bi'ur Chametz (Passover Clean-
up)—suggest that the war involved aspects of
ethnic cleansing. Palumbo's (1987) and
Masalha's (1992a; 1992b) recent work also
supports Khalidi's arguments in noting that Plan
D was drafted several years before the war
began.

According to Meir Pa'il, an Israeli historian
and veteran of the 1948 war, Plan D was the
first strategic plan and its underlying objective
was the nationwide conquest and control of
territories (1972:11). As officially stated, Plan
D's broad objectives were to gain control of
the area allotted to the Jewish State (within the
framework of the 1947 United Nations' parti-
tion of Palestine; see inset map in Figure 1) and
to defend its borders as well as the blocs of
Jewish settlements and such Jewish population
as were outside those borders against a regular
or pararegular enemy operating from bases
outside or inside the area of the Jewish State
(1972:11). According to Lorch, commanders of
territorial brigades were instructed to plan for
and execute several tactical objectives includ-
ing occupying "all police fortresses/stations
evacuated by British forces, and of Arab vil-
lages close to Jewish settlements; creating con-
tinuity between Jewish cities and neighbouring
Jewish settlements; gaining control of lines of
communications; besieging enemy cities; cap-
turing forward bases of the enemy; counter
attacks both inside and outside the borders of
the State" (1968:90-91). Pa'il observed that
Arab villages near to existing Jewish settle-

ments were to be cleansed (in Hebrew *tihur*)
in order to prevent the possibilities of their
being used as "bases for active (enemy) armed
bases" (1972:11). According to Pa'il, this proc-
ess of cleansing² was meant to be imple-
mented at three levels of intensity: 1) a mod-
erate level that involved only police cleansing;
2) a medium cleansing that involved besieging
the village and undertaking search operations
inside (in case of opposition, enemy armed
forces should be destroyed and the population
expelled across the state border); and 3) the
high intensity level that involved complete de-
struction of villages by blowing up the remain-
ing debris. The latter type of cleansing was
applied to those villages that presented diffi-
culties in maintaining continuous control
(1972:11-12).

Contextually, these strategies of cleansing left
just two alternatives for local Arab villages. First,
they had little choice but to accept any condi-
tion dictated by the Jewish forces/Israeli Army.
From this perspective, the Israeli tactical defini-
tions of "hostile population" and "who attacks
whom?" are crucial for Israeli decisions
whether to completely destroy a village or
leave it intact. Nor should one dismiss the pos-
sibility of an intentional provocation on the part
of the advancing army units in order to classify
a village as militarily hostile. Second, even vil-
lages whose inhabitants did not wish to resist
could not be assured that there would be no
expulsion. Indeed some villages were already
"listed" and singled out for cleansing because
they fell within the category of the third level
of intensity in Plan D. This partly explains why
some Arab villages friendly with pre-1948
Jewish settlements were not exempted from
expulsion (Finkelstein 1991:74-75; Masalha
1992b:168-173).

## The 1948 War as a "Total War"

It is commonly agreed that the origins of war can be traced to the U.N. General Assembly's adoption of resolution no. 181 (November 29, 1947). This resolution partitioned Palestine into two states, one Palestinian-Arab and the other Jewish, with Jerusalem and Bethlehem constituting an international zone. The city of Jaffa was left as an Arab enclave surrounded by the territory of the Jewish state. The U.N. proposal suggested increasing Jewish landholdings from about 7 percent of the Palestine Mandate to 55 percent, while placing 42 percent of the Palestinian population under the control and sovereignty of the proposed Jewish state. The war broke out on December 3, 1947 in Jerusalem. Not until May 15, 1948, the day the last British soldiers left the country (after 30 years of Mandatory rule), did any Arab army enter Palestine to assist the Palestinian civil population against the Jewish forces.³ Hence, prior to the departure of the British Army, Jewish forces initiated a war of demographic and territorial expansion which took on the dimension of space purification-expulsion and prevention of the return of the expellees. At least until May 15, 1948, there

was no regular army pitched against Jewish forces. Their enemy was essentially the indigenous civilian Palestinian population. Expulsions proceeded in accordance with Plan D. As Palumbo comments: "It is clear . . . that when Ben-Gurion and his associates drew up Plan D they were well aware that victory in the war with Arabs would be meaningless unless the resulting Jewish state was territorially and demographically viable" (1987:41)

The Jewish forces thus opted to launch a war against the Palestinian masses with the aim of capturing territory inside the enemy frontier. The task was a formidable one given the relative weakness of the Jewish forces on the eve of the war. Jews constituted one-third (estimated at 608,225 persons on December 31, 1946) of the country's total population (1,972,559 for the same date) (Khalidi 1992a:102). The Jewish armed forces of 65,000 also lacked the manpower (Khalidi 1971:858; Gilmour 1980:64) for a multi-dimensional war against an indigenous population. In order to overcome this disadvantage, Zionist leaders mobilized all able-bodied Jews in Palestine to fight with the army—a situation described by Liddell-Hart as a "nation-in-arms" (1967:9). As part of their army duties, civilian Jews were required, among other things, to settle in the depopulated Palestinian villages already occupied by Jewish army units so as to prevent the return of Palestinians (Masalha 1992a:189-190). They also assisted the army in demolishing villages that posed strategic threats.

The Jewish civilian population also performed two other types of military duties. These included the imposition of economic sanctions, the dissemination of war propaganda, and, with the assistance of the army, prevention of Palestinian villagers from harvesting their fields (Nazal 1978; Morris 1986b; Galan 1992). As Morris (1986b) notes "[i]t was in the Negev, where the wheat and barley fields ripened earliest, that a general policy of destruction of Arab fields was first considered" (1986b:673). Morris points out several examples of Jewish settlements harvesting the fields of both existing and abandoned Arab villages (in the coastal area, Mount Carmel and the Galilee)—an activity that was seen as "crucial to the war effort" (1986b:675). Expulsion also involved a propaganda campaign, a sort of "gentle" cleansing (Bell-Fialkoff 1993:119). Members of Jewish settlements in contact with Palestinian villages were urged by military commandsto "whisper" in the ears of Arabs that a great Jewish reinforcement had arrived to burn all the villages of the area and that these Arab friends should escape while there was still time (Khalidi 1961:28; Glazer 1980:103).

Jewish military weakness is likewise reflected in the massacres perpetrated in selected villages and towns (Sayigh 1979:75-77; Schechla 1993:260). This calculated aspect of war was carried out in small villages (e.g., the villages of Nasir al-Din and khirbat al-Wa'ra al-Sawda' in Tiberias sub-district and al-Husayniyya in Safad sub-district) and in villages which had good neighborly relations with the pre-1948 Jewish population and settlements (e.g., Dayer Yasin in Jerusalem sub-district and Zarnuqa in al-Ramla sub-district). Such massacres did not reflect the level of local resistance, but rather were intended to provide "lessons in toughness" to the Palestinians so that other neighboring villagers would panic and leave voluntarily. Based on British and Israeli archival material, Masalha was able to reconstruct (for the period May 20, 1947-March 31, 1948) at least 92 cases of terrorist activities and massacres perpetrated against the Palestinians (Figure 2). These were organized by regular Jewish forces (Hagana) and by the Irgun and Stern Gang (1992b:142-8). As Figure 2 indicates, there is a clear pattern where small villages were carefully selected as exemplary "victims." Moreover, these chosen villages were located in close proximity to major cities with Palestinian populations. Perhaps the most famous case is the April 9, 1948 massacre of at least 254 Arab civilians of Dayr Yasin village (Reynier 1950:69-74; Gilmour 1980:62) located near the western suburbs of Jerusalem. Other examples include the April 11 or 12, 1948 massacre in Nasir al-Din village near Tiberias; six massacres in villages near Safad (i.e., the villages of 'Ayn al-Zaytun, Jish, al-Saliha, Safsaf, a-Husayniyya, and Sa'sa') between March 12 and October 30, 1948; the massacre in Balad al-Shaykh village near Haifa (committed on December 31, 1947); and two massacres in Eilabun (on October 30, 1948) and Arab Al-Mawasi (Khirbat al-Wa'ra al-Sawda' in late October or early November, 1948) villages located half-way between Safad and Nazareth city. In these cases, Jewish forces singled out the weakest group in the rural community for massacre. They encountered minimum resistance even when dispatching small military units for such tasks. In

two cases massacres in the town of Lydda (al-Ramla sub-district) and the village of Ijzim (Haifa sub-district) were carried out during a truce-fire period. The Israelis thus not only violated one of the rules of war, but they used these rules to dispatch large-size military units and organize a successful operation without worrying about ongoing operations on other fronts.

No less calculated a war crime than massacres was the rape of Palestinian women by Jewish soldiers. Two well-known cases of combined massacres and rapes occurred in Safsaf village (Safad sub-district) and in al-Dawayima (Hebron sub-district). Both massacres were carried out on October 29, 1948 during army operations Hiram (for Safsaf) and "Yo'av" (for al-Dawayima). In these cases, gender was subordinated to the ultimate end of military triumph. Palestinian society (composed of both Muslims and Christians), like many other traditional societies, places great value on the honor of their women.

If Israeli strategy and tactics derived from weakness, observers may be inclined to justify their actions. A case in point is the criticism of Morris's work by the Israeli geographer, Ben-Artzi. By accusing Morris of ignoring the frustrations of those who experienced the war (i.e., the Jewish forces) and those who were apprehensive about its outcome (1992:164), he indirectly justifies Jewish war crimes. Similar sentiments have been expressed by the Israeli historian Pappe': "By recognizing the acute fear of another Holocaust among the Jews of Palestine at the time, we may understand part of Israel's behaviour both in the war of 1948 and that of 1967, and perhaps later also" (1992:45).

On the eve of the war, the Safad sub-district contained 82 permanently inhabited Arab localities (see Table 1); an additional 820 persons were classified as "nomads." The total number of Arabs was estimated at 46,920 persons in 1945. The sub-district also contained 6,700 Jews, more than one-third of whom lived in the town of Safad (Government of Palestine 1946). The rest of the Jewish population resided in a dozen colonies located mainly in the northern part of the sub-district known as the Hula Valley. Although the Jewish population of 6,700 did not exceed 12.5 percent of the total population of this sub-district, the U.N Partition Plan of 1947 allotted most of the area, including the town of Safad, to the Jewish state (see Figure 1). The 1948 war sharply altered the region's ethno-demographic composition. Over 95 percent of the sub-district's Palestinian Arab population was expelled or fled to Lebanon and Syria, leaving behind 77 abandoned villages and one town (Safad). Only five Arab villages remained when the war ended (see discussion below). Based on Morris (1987) and Khalidi's (1992b) work on the 77 depopulated villages, 32 villages (41.5 percent) were definitely depopulated before May 15, 1948; an additional 13 villages (17 percent) were depopulated during the war, though no precise date has been established, and; the remaining 32 villages (41.5 percent) were depopulated after May 15. The limited areas captured by the Lebanese and the Syrian army after May 15, 1948 (Figure 1) contained no more than nine villages—the only ones that could have experienced the side-effects of battles between the regular armies. Yet, as Figure 1 indicates, four of these villages (Tulayl, al-Husayniyya, Kirad al-Ghannama, and Kirad al-Baqqara) were depopulated before May 5, 1948 and the inva-

sion of the Syrian army into Palestine. That leaves 72 villages, constituting 93.5 percent of the depopulated villages in the sub-district, that were depopulated by clashes between civilian and Jewish forces.

Using Morris's (1987) categorizations of expulsions and Khalid's (1992b) dates of depopulation, it is possible to identify the causes of depopulation for 59 of the localities (out of total 77) in our study area (Figure 3). Depopulation occurred in three general phases. First, 21 villages were depopulated before the conquest of the town Safad on May 10-11, 1948. Second, 27 villages were depopulated between the fall of Safad and the end of May. Third, 11 villages were depopulated five months later. Morris (based on Israeli military declassified data) attributes the first and third depopulations to "military assault" and "expulsion" associated with two military operations (sub-operation Matate and Hiram). The second depopulation he attributes to military operation Yiftach and more specifically to "military assault," especially in the case of the district capital Safad and another seven villages. Causes such as "psychological warfare," "influence of the fall of a neighboring town," and "fear of Jewish attack" appear to coincide with those localities subject to "military assault." None of the 59 villages or any other locality in Safad sub-district, according to Morris (1987), was subject to "abandonment by Arab orders"—a popular reason cited in Israeli geographic writing (Reichman 1990).

The spatial pattern of depopulation over time and space is illustrated in Figure 1. The Safad sub-district provides an excellent test case of the operational objectives of Plan D. Village depopulation occurred in four phases or waves. The first wave (before May 5, 1948) in Safad sub-district focused on military objec-tives: 1) creating a continuous territory be-tween Jewish settlements in lower Galilee (specifically in Tiberias sub-district) and Upper Galilee via the settlement of Rosh Pinna. (Operation Matate, Operation "Broom"-a sub-component of Operation Yiftach-was intended to cleanse all Arab localities along the Rosh Pinna-Tiberias road; Morris 1987:121); 2) taking control over strategic points along the Syrian border by means of emptying Arab vil-lages suspected as serving as enemy bases or susceptible to enlisting on the Syrian side in a critical situation; and 3) strengthening the defen-sive capability of existing Jewish settlements by the removal of the nearby Arab villages.

The second wave of depopulation occurred during the week of May 9. Among the major objectives of the Jewish forces was the occu-pation of the town of Safad, establishment of control over all communication lines connect-ing the town from the west and east, and the occupation of police fortress/stations evacu-ated by British forces (at Mount Canaan and al Jau'na/Rosh Pinna). The police fortress of al-Nabi Yusa' was considered the most vital since it stood on a major road from Lebanon into the Hula Valley. Jewish forces made at least two attempts to capture this fortress before it was finally captured on the night of May 16-17, at a cost of heavy Jewish casualties (Lorch 1968:107, 179). The village of al-Nabi Yusha' was occupied and depopulated on May 16.

The remaining villages occupied and depopu-lated during this week were all to the north of al-Nabi Yusha', along the major road in the Hula Valley. Two explanations can be offered here for the depopulation of these villages. First, Jewish forces were attempting to take control over villages located along the north-south main road and, ultimately, to occupy al Khalisa village (ca. 1,840 inhabitants in 1945), the sec-ond largest Palestinian locality after Safad in the sub-district, a popular market place for many Lebanese, Syrian, and Palestinian villages in the area, and the site of a police station and Friday mosque (Khalidi 1992b:462-463). Second, in order to enhance communication between (and eventually control over) the Jewish settle-ments in this area and that of the rest of the region, Jewish forces apparently thought it was necessary to remove some of the Arab villages on the internal roads leading to Jewish settle-ments (Oren 1983:827-831).

The third wave of village depopulation came during the last ten days of May (19-28). It can be regarded as a turning point because this wave coincided with the termination of Opera-tion Yiftach. By this juncture, a major part of the sub-district had been cleansed of its Palestinian population. Depopulation thus was probably guided by demography, i.e., the desire for the creation of a territory devoid of Arabs after the long isolation of Jewish settlement in this part of Palestine. The removal of the remaining Arab villages from the Hula Valley would pro-vide Jewish colonies with plenty of land.

The fourth wave of occupation and depopu-lation involved nine villages on October 29-30. This wave marked the last exodus of Palestin-ian villages from Safad sub-district in war time. Morris (1987:230) attributed these depopula-tions to "military assault," in which extensive atrocities were committed, principally by the 7th Brigade within Operation Hiram. The vil-lages of the Safad subdistrict constituted, how-ever, only a small portion of a much more extensive depopulation process that took place under Operation Hiram (July 18-November 1948). Palumbo (1987:166-167) noted two main reasons for the more brutal Israeli actions during the October offensive. First, Israelis re-alized that no one could stop them from cre-ating a larger Jewish state with a relatively small Arab population; second, the Israelis encoun-tered more difficulty in expelling the Arabs who had learned that if they left their homes,they probably would never return. Historical and geographical factors contributed to the obliteration of a cultural heritage. Historically, villages in the plains of Palestine have been small and sometimes even seasonally inhabited. Obviously, small villages with small built-up areas have less cultural material, and hence it is easier to "purge" their remains. It should also be emphasized that the building materials for houses in the plains villages are traditionally different from hilly and mountainous villages (Conder 1879:237-38). Solid stone houses with concrete roofs are more common in rocky areas. In contrast, houses in the villages on the plains were often made of less durable materials, including adobe brick and wood for roofs, rendering them more vulnerable to human destruction.

## Major Destruction and Partial Occupancy

The 126 villages in this destruction level comprise some 30 percent of the total depopulated villages. The first sublevel consists of 74 villages where some houses remain intact while the rest have been demolished (Figure 9). ## Remaining Public Buildings and Cultural Relicts

Controversy over the plight of the Palestinians should not obfuscate the importance of

cultural material produced by that society before it took the road to exile. From this perspective, the works of Graham-Brown (1980) and Khalidi (1984) are invaluable sources. Assembling hundreds of photographs, they present a diversified portrait of everyday Palestinian life from about 1880 down to May 15, 1948. With their captions and accompanying text, the photographs present a population engaged in a broad spectrum of activities. An earlier work, Neil's *Everyday Life in the Holy Land* (1913), contains an equally valuable description of Palestinian traditions and customs based on thirty-two paintings.

Yet by the turn of the twentieth century, following the introduction of new building material (such as reinforced concrete and cement) and new transportation systems, villages in the plains significantly altered their use of building materials. The expansion of citrus plantations in the 1930s and 1940s helped to intensify agriculture, improve standards of living, and spur the construction of modern houses, some of which were two-storey buildings with artificial ponds on the roof (e.g., in the villages of Bayyarat Hannun and Ghabat Kafr Sur).

The concept of place among Palestinian peasants was not confined to the village core, i.e., as defined by an agglomeration of houses; a place irradiated outward to include village fields, terraces, orchards, springs, roads lead-

Given the small size of the villages, and the absence of gender segregation of house space (Sayigh 1979:21), outdoor activities rendered peasant social life more "visible" in villages than in towns or bedouin encampments; the village's spatiality was more "externalized." Contextually, the peasant home was extended, its boundaries amplified.

Collective management of resources was another salient aspect of village life. This was imperative in a society where, as Sayigh observed, the village population was a "family of families," and the language of kinship dominated everyday life (1979:21). Neil provided a detailed description of the annual assembly of all the villagers in the guest-house of the village or the main square (saha) for the public distribution of land (1913:89-91). Conder noted the existence of a custom where some land is "cultivated by the villagers for any one of their [me]mbers who is unable to till it himself"

(1879:256). While a collective mode of life was adapted as a defensive mechanism against tax collectors and bedouin raids (Sayigh 1979:15-17), it was also necessary for dealing with natural hazards such as floods and combatting locusts. In connection with village preparations to fight locusts, Goodrich-Freer referred to an incident in March 1923 when villagers in the plains had to take their children from school to help in the fight against locusts: an event which "occupied the mind of the people far more than the World War" (1924:244). Nature remained harsh.

To accommodate their economic, social and spiritual needs, the villagers established various public places and sacred structures. The guest-house or coffee house (diwan) was the popular informal gathering place in the village. It was the locus for "the discussion of public events, and for story-telling . . . playing games, and, especially during ramadan and at other festivals, is often enlivened with music. The coffeehouse is . . . the substitute for the public-house, theatres, concert-halls, and all else" (Goodrich-Freer 1924:153). Visitors could stay in the village's guest-house and entertain themselves freely. For longer stays, visitors and foreign travellers preferred the *khans* (caravansaries) and monasteries usually found in towns and along major thoroughfares (Leary 1911:142-145).

Educational institutions in the modern sense were sparse in Palestinian villages. These were staffed by teachers invited from towns to the villages. Conder (1879) noted that in his time peasant children received no formal education; nonetheless, "they acquire a wonderfully accurate knowledge of the country round the village . . . [and] are the great authorities as to the names of ruins or springs" (1879:252). Until 1908 the Turkish Government had neglected educational services in Palestine. This situation improved slightly after the establishment of a constitutional government in Turkey in 1908 (Abcarius 1946:100) and significantly during the British administration of Palestine between 1922 and 1947 (Khalidi 1984:163). School-houses in Arab villages were often among the most sturdy and largest structures, constructed of stone and concrete. The quality of construction reflects financing from various sources including the village council, the government authorities, and the Islamic *waqif* for the purchase of materials and the services of good workmen and builders. Schools were generally located at the edge of the village.

The group of remaining physical structures in the Palestinian villages depopulated in 1948, identified and mapped in Figure 12, reflects the diverse functions of villages within the regional settlement system. Some were located at crossroads. Others with railway stations or police stations had long been service centers for their subregions. Certain cultural relics such as a *khan* or caravansary, castle, and fortress indicated the rich history of certain localities in the countryside. In addition, the presence of structures such as the *diwan* or coffee houses, mills, and artificial pools or ponds illustrated community life and resource management. Most of the items identified in Figure 12 are in a state of neglect and decay. The Israeli authorities made no attempt to preserve such cultural relics, and only a few buildings, particularly schools in areas where villages were also occupied by Jewish families, have been maintained for local use.

But why have these structures not been demolished along with the abandoned houses in their villages? The answer is not entirely clear, but some speculations are possible. In the first place, assuming that Israeli policy aimed at demolishing residences in order to prevent the return of the original inhabitants, non-residential structures probably were not regarded as high-priority candidates for demolition. Their continued presence in a de-signified topography may not have been considered as threatening. Second, the Israelis may not have demolished them because of their utility for some future function.

The absence of any Israeli government policy to preserve Palestinian cultural relics as monuments to the region's Palestinian past has been tantamount, in the long run, to their elimination. Abandoned and unprotected, these relics have been subject to destruction once they are viewed as negative externalities to local development (Dumper 1994:55-60). Furthermore, since state authorities and most Jewish citizens regard Palestinian cultural relics as "abandoned property," these relics are, in effect, state property (Golan 1992; Dumper 1994:30-35). The physical existence of such property thus depends ultimately on the good will of government authorities who are generally ignorant of or disinterested in the history and significance of such cultural items. Indeed, removing the Palestinian structures that might serve as potential cultural signifiers serves the political goals of diminishing the Palestinian past and weakening Palestinian claims to the land. In cases where certain cultural relics have been preserved for tourist purposes, e.g., the mountain village of Sataf (Jerusalem sub-district), the official historical records usually indicate an existence that extends back to biblical times. In these ways, the Palestinian Arab cultural contributions to the village site have been trivialized as Jewish history has been fore-grounded. In a kind of reverse palimpsest, the ancient layers beneath the Palestinian site have been highlighted.

Beliefs are reflected in the religious affiliations of the inhabitants of a particular village (Moslems, Christians, or both) and in landscape structures such as mosques, churches, and cemeteries in the built-up area of each village. Customs are reflected in the numerous sacred sites associated with saints. The term *makamat* (singular *makam* or standing-place) is often used to designate these places. The *makam* is usually a white stone building capped by a small dome. *Makamat* have various degrees of importance depending on the beliefs connected with them (Conder 1879:266-268).

Having examined the relations of sacred sites to human habitations, Canaan contended that “[s]acred shrines are innumerable in Palestine. Nearly everywhere—in villages, on the mountains, in valleys, in the fields—do we meet with them. There is hardly a village, however small it may be, which does not honour at least one local saint. . . . As every village possesses lands which stretch for miles beyond the settlement itself, every shrine situated in such land belongs to that village, and is also honoured primarily by its inhabitants” (1927:2-3). Canaan’s (1927) observation strengthens the conception of the village as thus a locus for an array of meaningful places, a package that attaches the peasant to the village and its landscape.

The scope of de-Arabization would be even more striking if it encompassed Israel’s treatment of non-Jewish holy places in the Palestinian cities depopulated during the years 1948-1950 (Safad, Tiberias, Baysan, Beersheba, and al-Majdal) as well as in the five mixed Arab-Jewish cities (Jaffa, Lydda, al-Ramla, Haifa, and Acre). These contained a broad and rich array of Christian and Muslim holy places. Here, however, I focus only on the holy places in the 418 depopulated Palestinian villages. Village de-Arabization assumed a variety of manifestations depending on the nature of the place. Figure 13 maps the surviving Muslim and Christian holy places in the depopulated villages. The symbols on the map represent the type of holy place, not their actual numbers. No regional pattern is discernible for the 68 villages where such sites remain; more important, however, is the current physical status of these places.

Field research revealed that the Israelis

adopted three general principles for dealing with holy places. First, monasteries and convents generally have survived and continue to function as they did before 1948. The authorities did not touch this type of church property in the countryside, apparently out of respect for the Western Christian world and the Vatican. Monasteries are directly connected with the outside Christian world and not with the Arab citizens of Palestine. Some of the monks and nuns living there barely speak Arabic. Second, shrines and sheik’s tombs (*makamat*) were largely untouched, but no state effort has been made to preserve them. The shrine of al-Nabi Yusha’ in the village of the same name (Safad sub-district) is a huge abandoned structure. The shrine is closed off with wire fencing—not for protection but to prevent access to the site. These shrines may have been spared by bulldozer operators at the time village houses were being destroyed because of the widespread belief that such shrines have the mystical power to punish those who desecrate them. Nevertheless, their destruction was left to the forces of nature.

The town of Safad, mentioned earlier, also experienced desecration of its former places of worship, Christian and Moslem. Its Friday Mosque has been used for the past three decades as a spacious general exhibition hall by the Safad Artists' Association. Engraved Koranic inscriptions attest to its former function. The massive and much older Red Mosque is owned by the municipality and used on occasion for staging rock and folk concerts. A former church in the heart of the former Arab Christian quarter was converted in the 1960s to private galleries and apartments for two Jewish artists, its belltower looking down anomalously on the lanes of the Artists' Quarter. Directly opposite the churchtower lies a large complex that was formerly a monastery and now houses a municipally-run art museum. Stone-engraved crosses still adorn its inner courtyard. The main Moslem hillside cemetery, located just below the principal access road at the northern edge of town, is in a shambles, its numerous stone tombstones in disarray and overgrown by brush. It is puzzling to note the persistence of such practices within Israel's sovereign territory, when at the same time any desecration of a Jewish synagogue or cemetery in Europe brings forth immediate accusations of wilful anti-Semitism, and when Arab holy sites and cemeteries are nominally protected by Israeli state law.

## Concluding Remarks

"When the Portuguese colonized, they built churches; when the British colonized, they built trading stations; when the French colonize, they build schools" (Gordon 1962:7). It is my thesis that when the Israelis colonized Palestine, they destroyed most of the Palestinian landscapes that they found and deconstructed their cultural topographies. As this paper has attempted to show, the events of the 1948 Israeli-Palestinian war served as catalytic agents for the transformation of Palestine's cultural landscape. This was a "total" war, one marked by unlimited violence and by the depopulation of Palestinian places.

The spatial obliteration of village landscapes was an integral component of Israeli military strategy during the 1948 war, and one that persisted into the post-war period. For the Palestinians, the ruins of the villages represent the

remains of a lost cultural topography. Yet these very ruins may "provide the incentive for restoration, and for a return to origins" (Jackson 1980:102); their survival constitutes material evidence refuting Israel's claim that the Palestinians are not part of Palestine (Peters 1984; Abu-Lughod 1988; Finkelstein 1988), that past Palestinian identity has been obliterated from the "Judaicized" landscape and cleansed of its Palestinian markers.

This paper has reconsidered the way in which the state of Israel was created and superimposed on the landscape in Palestine, supplanting in course the Palestinian landscape. Israel's current cultural landscape, I suggest, is one that has been established by the obliteration of another via de-signification. The predominant Israeli interpretations of the war and the making of the modern Israeli cultural landscape are, of course, quite different. Generations of children at Israeli state schools have been taught a one-sided story in the name of the Israeli "civic religion," one that has engendered negative attitudes toward non-Jewish cultural places, a kind of cultural xenophobia. These attitudes toward Palestinian localities (whose residents are officially defined as "enemies") blinds most Israelis to the value of these places for their former inhabitants. Israelis thus tend to view the Palestinian cultural landscape that endures in depopulated villages and cities in terms of its present utility rather than its past function.

Authenticating Palestine's geographical record may help to redress this situation. The data presented here—a portion of which has been classified—afford insights on the historical geography of a landscape's de-signification. Having adapted an ideological and geographical paradigm that has been shaped by the discursive regime of Israeli nationalism, Israeli geographers understandably have been hard put to provide a complete and unbiased picture of the empirical realities of the Palestinian cultural landscape and its transformation since 1948. However well-intentioned, members of a society that carried out (or benefited from) the dispossession of the Palestinians are unlikely to inventory the Palestinian cultural relicts that survive or to chronicle the violence of dispossession or the historical events surrounding it. Doubtless the same objections could be raised about committed Palestinian researchers were it not for the massive archival and photo-graphic evidence that they have helped ad-
duce. In any event, the time seems ripe for a
more open and less ideological era in scholar-
ship on the geographical palimpsest of Palest-
tine/Israel. The alternative reading of its land-
scape realities presented here is perhaps a
small step on that way.

Falah, Ghazi. 1996. The 1948 Israeli-Palestinian War and its Aftermath: The Transformation and De-Signification of Palestine's Cultural Landscape. *Annals of the Association of American Geographers* 86(2):256-285. Abstract.

Violence against persons and places may result in profound and radical transformations in the cultural landscape. In Palestine, political processes during war and its aftermath resulted in radical changes in culturescape. While the 1948 plight of some three-quarters of a million Palestinian refugees has been the subject of considerable analysis and controversy, far less attention has been paid to the physical destruction of the spatial world they inhabited. Using recently released classified material on the 1948 Israeli-Palestinian War, this paper seeks to interpret the war and its aftermath from the Palestinian civilian perspective. Particular attention is given to the enforced uprooting of the Palestinians and the obliteration of their rural villages. Empirical findings based on visits to all Palestinian villages (418) depopulated during that war corroborate the radical transformation of the Arab cultural landscape and its concomitant de-signification. The paper postulates six different categories of state intervention in the Arab cultural landscape; these range from obliteration (hardly any physical remains are discernable) to the selective survival of Arab houses currently occupied by Jewish families. This paper delineates what is essentially a "forgotten landscape" concealed under the palimpsest of the countryside of modern Israel/Palestine. **Key Words:** cultural landscape transformation, depopulated villages, de-signification, ethnic cleansing, field research, Jewish forces, Israel-Palestine, the 1948 war, Safad sub-district.
