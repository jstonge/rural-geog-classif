PLACE MAKING, HAZARDOUS WASTE, AND THE
DEVELOPMENT OF TOOELE COUNTY, UTAH*

J. MATTHEW SHUMWAY and RICHARD H. JACKSON

ABSTRACT. Declining populations, aging inhabitants and infrastructure, limited economic opportunities, and under- or unappreciated natural environments characterize a number of rural communities in the western United States. Faced with the challenges of providing for their residents, some of these communities have chosen to permit undesirable land-use activities, including the disposal of hazardous or nuclear waste. Central to the development of such sites is how a place is perceived and portrayed. Our purpose in this article is to examine how a dominant perception and portrayal of one such place—Tooele County, Utah—was created and used to facilitate the development of hazardous-waste-disposal sites. We use the geographical concept of “place” to illustrate how meanings and values are attached to a region in order to justify its becoming what it is and how such views persist. *Keywords: hazardous waste, NIMBY, perception, place, Tooele County, Utah.*

Except for localized boom-and-bust cycles of mineral exploitation, many rural areas in the intermontane U.S. West have always been economically marginal. Because of the typically arid climatic conditions at the lower elevations, which limited agricultural development without irrigation, and the difficulty of settlement at higher elevations, the rural parts of this region have two overriding traits that set it apart from other regions in the United States: sparse and widely scattered rural settlement patterns and federal government ownership of large swaths of land. These two factors, along with the general aridity of the region, provide the context within which ongoing globalization has and is creating new rural geographies.

Globalization has accelerated competition for the products that the rural West produces, making many traditional activities economically marginal (Galston and Baehler 1995; Beyers and Lindahl 1996; Power 1996; McGranahan 1999, Deller and others 2001; Shumway and Otterstrom 2001; Stauber 2001; Walker 2003; Green, Deller, and Marcouiller 2005). This has led to economic restructuring and the creation of new spatial patterns of rural development, which can be classified

A landscape of relatively isolated, small farms, abandoned mines, overgrazed land, and sparse forests characterizes these marginalized areas of the rural, inter-montane West. The communities that make up the marginalized West are characterized by slow-growing, stable, or declining populations, aging inhabitants and infrastructure, limited economic opportunities, and under- or unappreciated natural environments that seem to be forgotten by most, but not all, of society. A number of them have become locales of undesirable land uses. Activities ranging from waste disposal to prisons, to nuclear repositories, and to power plants seek places that are so desperate for investment they agree to accept land uses that more prosperous communities will not accept. The result is the location of undesirable land uses known as "NIMBY" (Not In My Back Yard). Residents of more prosperous places not only avoid but also protest vehemently should a NIMBY be suggested for their community. However, people in areas experiencing economic difficulties often see NIMBYS as opportunities for economic development (Kousis 1998; Wulfhorst 2000; Eser and Luloff 2003; Kasperson 2005; van der Horst 2007). Whether to accept a so-called NIMBY must be seen within the context of rural restructuring, which has limited the ability of economically marginal communities to engage in what were traditional economic activities: mining, ranching, farming, and timber. These traditional types of economic activities provided the rationale for the initial settlement of such rural communities but can no longer be used as a means for local sustainability (Power 1996).

As Jeffrey Davis points out in his work on nuclear testing on the Bikini Atoll (2005), how a place under consideration is perceived and presented, both by those who live within it and by those who live outside it, is crucial to the acceptance of different types of noxious or dangerous land uses. The perception must be created that, whatever the activity, its location is "natural" and backed by "common sense," not created by a particular set of historical and social processes. However, following the logic of J. D. Wulfhorst's findings (2000), the reaction to such labeling by local residents is much more complex than simply accepting a proffered label, for it depends on local economic circumstances. In those counties with great economic needs, residents may either be nonaccepting of or ambivalent about the label that their local area is the "natural" place for a specific NIMBY but willingly accept the asso-ciated long-term risk from hazardous or noxious land uses because of its short-
term economic gains. In some cases local residents will even accept the label if they
believe it will maintain their local community's social and cultural cohesion in the
face of what is perceived to be another type of short-term, high-risk activity, rapid
residential growth. Once a particular view of a place becomes dominant, a "percep-
tual lock in" occurs that is difficult to change because it provided a justification for
a NIMBY in the first place. The locals then accept, adopt, and adapt the label for their
own use, and the local area becomes more like the label.

CONCEPTUALIZING PLACE AND PLACE PERCEPTION

Geographers have developed a number of concepts to help understand human modification of the earth's surface, including the idea of "place." Robert Sack defined place as the milieu in which humans transform the earth into the home of humankind (1999). The juxtaposition of the physical environment, social relationships, and place perception create milieus in which what Sack calls "projects" happen. Projects are what we as humans do—everything from teaching students, to incarcerating criminals, to creating industrial wastes that are toxic. In order for such a project to occur, a place must be created: Every activity has to happen somewhere, and every project we undertake either creates a new place as a requirement for its fulfillment or appropriates another place that was designed for another project. Projects often occur in multiple places that must be linked by communication, transportation, and socioeconomic networks. Thus the geography of our world results from the fact that our lives and our projects require a geographical structure of specific places and associated networks of flows that must be maintained—at least until they are no longer useful—in order for the original projects to succeed.

Places and networks gain strength and power through rules, selectivity, and
continuity. Rules determine what projects can occur at a given location and the
types of spatial interaction with other places. For example, one outcome of an
urbanized, industrial society is the creation of waste products with varying levels
of toxicity. Finding a place for toxic wastes that does not despoil the local milieu,
generally defined as "where we live," can be viewed as a particular project that
relies on a differentiated space where the construction of disposal sites becomes
necessary for successful industrialization. Local "growth machines" (Logan and
Molotch 1987), together with state and federal regulators, establish rules for site
selection and use of the site based on the origin of the waste products, toxicity
levels, perceived threats to nearby population centers, political jurisdiction, local
environmental conditions, landownership, and economic issues. Once established,
the rules tend to favor specific locations—which limits competition from other
places—and provide an institutional framework that gives the initial location(s)
continued advantages. One caveat: The creation of rules is a political process with
a great deal of uncertainty, so it is difficult to predict specific rules or how they will
be used. Rules determine the appropriateness of specific activities in specific places
and embed asymmetrical power structures in different places.Selectivity is the process of attracting participants, both human and nonhuman, with specific characteristics necessary for projects to be successful. Selectivity provides a crucial linkage between place and project while simultaneously reinforcing the rationale for which the place was originally constructed. Rules influence selectivity by legitimizing the unique synthesis of nature, meaning, and social relationships into a place (Sack 1999). Again with respect to toxic waste disposal, the successful location of one type of toxic waste in an area, with its associated rules, selectively draws new types of waste to the area because the institutional and physical infrastructure is already in place. Previous institutional and physical infrastructure development lowers the cost of future waste disposal. The selective flow of new waste then justifies the original investment and institutional processes and makes it more economically and politically feasible for future flows.

We all view different places differently, because how we view a place is based on our experience with it, our information about it, and our position with respect to it—for example, whether we live there and, if so, for how long, whether the place is in the news, and whether we have visited it. In Sack’s conceptualization (1999), place has three elements: nature, social relations, and meanings (values, ideas, and beliefs). The natural and the social intersect to create project-specific places, but related meanings are necessary for continuity of the original creation. We create and use meanings to influence views, beliefs, and values concerning various places (Davis 2005). Frequent transmission of specific place representations to both local and nonlocal communities creates a consistent place meaning. The representation of places and the medium of transmission of what a place is brings additional force to the establishment of one meaning over other possible place meanings. Once established, dominant place meanings link the perceptual and the real. Such a link is necessary in order for the continuity of the project.

According to Rob Shields (1991), dominant views about a place come through three main processes: reducing the place to one or two traits, amplifying those traits as the most important, and labeling, or giving a place its essential identity through a label. Once a place meaning achieves coherence and longevity the meaning becomes a semipermanent place myth that provides legitimacy. The power of place myths is that they naturalize that which is contingent and historical, provide a justification for certain types of events while limiting others, and give places an identity in terms not of an open and changing milieu but as a statement of truth. Place myths also legitimize particular types of social practices that provide the structure for altering or maintaining the material landscape in order to bring it into line with or maintain a particular place meaning. That is, different social groups—both local and multilocal—with competing views of what a place should be create place myths to legitimize and justify their particular view and attempt to change or maintain the material landscape in order to reflect these place myths and to delegitimize other potential projects (Vandergeest and DuPuis 1996). For example, the view that the entire U.S. Great Basin, including the West Desert, is a wasteland facilitates the observation of both local and nonlocal audiences that this is a natural place for thelocation of noxious projects (Larsen and Brock 2005). These views can then lead to policy decisions—rules and the formation of institutional infrastructures—such as the creation of a hazardous waste zone in Tooele County by the County Commission. Such an action legitimized the view of the area as a natural place for hazardous wastes and simultaneously justified similar land uses in the future.

## THE FORGOTTEN AND MARGINALIZED RURAL WEST

Most of the recent focus in the rural western United States has been on areas where economic growth is occurring—the “New West.” Steven Deller and his coauthors demonstrated that economic growth in the rural West is concentrated in high-amenity areas (2001), whereas stagnant economic growth characterizes most low-amenity areas. This can lead to the perception that low-amenity areas with stagnant economies are good locales for activities that other places are unwilling to accept: waste deposits, prisons, material testing and disposing of toxic material, and industrial-scale livestock production. Whereas more affluent communities may resist and more powerful communities may retard such activities, struggling communities will often compete with each other for the right to host such waste flows, arguing that any economic development is better than none. This “rhetoric of despair” creates a perceptual context that facilitates the economic rationale for the location of activities that are generally thought of as undesirable by more prosperous communities and simultaneously allow negative labels to become dominant in order to justify the location of such activities (Bourke 1994). An examination of one such locale illustrates how place meanings are created and then used to influence future development.

Although modern transportation has alleviated many of the concerns associated with traversing Tooele County, the West Desert still presents a generally arid and unprepossessing landscape to observers. For example, in *The Hidden West*, Rob Schultheis referred to the Great Basin region, in which Tooele is located, as “America’s geographic purgatory” (1996, 138).

Even though it can safely be said that the West Desert of Tooele County possesses few of the traits that modern American society values for recreation destinations, retirement living, or other purposes that currently drive economic growth in the rural West (Shumway and Otterstrom 2001), that does not mean that othershave not found value in the region. A number of authors document the continued
importance of the region, particularly its spiritual importance, to its first inhabit-
ant as well as to those who came later and stayed (Raymond 1997; Francaviglia
2003; Goin and Starrs 2005). On the other hand, some groups have used the per-
ception of the region as dry, distant, and marginal as justification for using the
region as a "national sacrifice zone" (N. Campbell 2000; Beck 2001; Johnson and

with private industries, created an institutional framework that used Tooele's geo-
graphical characteristics—aridity, government landownership, small population
size, few economically extractable resources, but good relative accessibility—to
create the ideal sacrifice zone.

Tooele County's earliest industries were similar to those in many other Moun-
tain West communities from 1850 to 1940: Farming, ranching, and mining formed
the lifeblood of the local economy. Ranching and grazing interests created Tooele
County's first economy. In 1847 Mormon pioneers settled neighboring Salt Lake
Valley, and by 1848 the influx of people and accompanying livestock into that
valley had quickly outstripped its livestock capacity. New grazing lands were needed,
and one of the places to which Mormons pushed was westward over the Oquirrh
Mountains into the eastern part of what is now Tooele County (Blanthorn 1998,
63). Early ranchers grazed many head of cattle and initially found great success.
However, competition for the rangeland, coupled with gross overuse, resulted in
serious depletion of the land. After cattle came sheep, creating massive overgraz-
ing. The early impact of grazing activities in Tooele County caused the Grantsville
newspaper to report on 25 September 1879 that "This country, which was one of
the best ranges for stock in the territory, is now among the poorest. The myriads of
sheep that have been herded here for the past few years have almost entirely de-
royed our range" (quoted in Blanthorn 1998, 232). Ranching activities continue,
but at a much-reduced rate. The early ranching industry provides our first glimpse
into a trend in Tooele County that sees short-lived industries prosper, then falter,
leaving the community to cope with the consequences.

Mining in Tooele County owes its origins to prospecting by soldiers from Cali-
fornia who were assigned to Fort Douglas in Salt Lake City. The Rush Valley Mining
District was organized by some of these soldiers in 1864, and by 1865 they had regis-
tered more than 500 mining claims for copper, gold, silver, lead, and zinc. By the
1870s mining in the Oquirrh Mountains dominated the economy of Tooele County,
with ephemeral boomtowns such as Stockton, Mercur, and Ophir (Blanthorn 1998,
13, 227). The mining boom in Tooele County was only part of the larger mining
boom in Utah's mountains, including Bingham Canyon on the western side of the
Salt Lake Valley and the Park City areas of the Rocky Mountains. Much of the min-
ing in Tooele County and elsewhere in Utah relied on non-Mormon prospectors
and entrepreneurs because Brigham Young, the Mormon leader, was committed
to an agrarian society. Rapid population growth among the Mormon settlers and
the arrival of the transcontinental railroad in 1869 prompted growth of industrialactivities, especially mining, agricultural equipment, and ore smelters. Like the boomtowns with which they were associated, many of the early smelters were small and short-lived, but by the 1890s large-scale smelters began to replace the smaller ones near the mining towns. Growth in mining and smelting employment prompted immigration of non-Mormons into the state, primarily from eastern and southern Europe (Notarianni 1994).

Growth of mining in Bingham Canyon in the 1890s was associated with ever-larger smelters, so that, by the end of the nineteenth century, many Salt Lake City residents considered the smoke and other negative externalities associated with at least six different smelters and refineries in the Salt Lake Valley undesirable (Notarianni 1994). Government and industrial leaders responded by spearheading a campaign to encourage the construction of a large smelter on the western side of the Oquirrh Mountains, in Tooele County, that would relieve the residents of populous Salt Lake Valley. By 1910 the Anaconda Copper Company-backed International Smelter in Tooele County just east of Tooele City began accepting lead and copper ore and other concentrates from the Salt Lake Valley mining districts as well as the rest of Utah, Idaho, and Nevada (Murphy 1994). The relocation of the smelter and the growth of the mining industry in Tooele County sparked the creation of a local culture willing to accept potentially noxious facilities as a trade-off for economic stability. The early move of the smelter to Tooele County laid the foundation for the perception of Tooele as useful for NIMBYS.

The Great Depression dealt a heavy blow to mining activities in Tooele County. Many mines closed, but perhaps most devastating was the closure of the International Smelter from 1931 to 1934, which resulted in the loss of nearly 2,000 jobs. The county had the highest per capita relief expenditures in the state, and Utah had the third highest of all the states (E. Campbell 1983). Ranching and grazing activities were practiced only at a small scale, the mining industry became considerably less significant when the International Smelter closed, and both of these factors were compounded by the general discouragement during the depression. Like many of its contemporary communities, Tooele County searched for economic development, including tourism, to revive its dying economy.

## THIS LAND IS MY LAND

understand anything about land use in the West without knowing that much of what takes place has been and continues to be influenced by U.S. government policy. For example, when the United States entered World War II and needed to quickly create military bases, testing ranges, and munitions depots, the government chose to develop sites on sparsely inhabited land it already controlled (Figure 2). Previous research suggests that the federal government tended to locate such facilities in rural areas experiencing economic difficulties, had a conservative population, and were accustomed to the omnipresent role played by federal agencies (Rothman 1998; Girdner 2002). Tooele County meets these criteria.

During World War II the U.S. military injected tens of millions of dollars and thousands of jobs into the Tooele County economy by constructing the TooeleArmy Depot, Deseret Chemical Depot, and Dugway Proving Grounds (Figure 3). Following the logic of Dale Rothman and, more recently, of Eddie Girdner and Jack Smith, Tooele County was particularly well suited to these types of military establishments, in part because of the very low population density and the necessary expansive buffer zones required (Rothman 1998; Girdner and Smith 2002). The federal facilities promised Tooele County relief from its economic and demographic depression and offered the county a way to showcase its patriotism. Moreover, the fact that Tooele County residents had already experienced the location of a noxious facility in their midst by a larger and more powerful entity—Salt Lake County—provided the appropriate perceptual framework for acceptance.

The immediate economic impact of the military facilities in Tooele County cannot be overstated. In just two years following the military's entrance into the area, both the population and the assessed value of Tooele County tripled. By 1943, $52 million had been spent on war facilities in Tooele County (Malmquist 1943). Employment was the prime concern for most Tooele residents, and the new facilities provided an estimated 4,000 jobs. Tooele City's population increased from 5,000 to 15,000 in the months during and after the construction of these facilities (E. Campbell 1983). As the cold war wound down, however, so did the economic impact of the military in Tooele County. Nevertheless, as late as 1989 the defense industry still accounted for more than 5,000 jobs, or 49 percent of the total Tooele County employment (State of Utah 2000). The long-term effect of the military facilities in Tooele County also changed the perception regarding the arid expanses of the county. Land that was once of little economic value was now viewed as a major asset, even if it was valuable only for industries engaged in storing and disposing of hazardous and noxious materials. The initial location and subsequent growth of dangerous federal facilities provided another "fact on the ground" that demonstrated the usefulness of Tooele County for these types of activities. As Dan van der Horst shows (2007), people's perception of NIMBYS are generally determined by proximity, local economic context, and the value of the land being used. Because the military facilities are relatively isolated, the local economy was weak, and the foremost view of the land was that it was of little value, the perception of the military locating in Tooele County tended to be positive (Blanthorn 1998).

## "SMELLS LIKE JOBS TO ME"

The perception of Tooele County as a national sacrifice zone had been woven together from early strands of its physical geographical characteristics—the view that the land was valueless for most traditional types of economic activities—together with a set of social relationships between the local population and larger, wealthier, and more powerful neighboring counties, the state of Utah, and ultimately the federal government. Local residents came to believe that, whatever the activity might be, they had little choice in the decision-making process. A general feeling was prevalent that weapons depots and smelters were the only types of industries capable of economically exploiting the "valueless" land and that jobs in them were preferable to unemployment (Bauman 2001). An excel-lent example of how these perceptions continued to affect land use in Tooele County was the establishment of the Magnesium Corporation of America, or MagCorp, a magnesium-processing facility, in 1972.

The mining industry that had struggled since the 1930s rebounded when MagCorp located its facility on the western shores of the Great Salt Lake (Figure 4). It represented the first development on the Great Salt Lake since Saltair opened. When the facility was first announced in 1968 the Tooele County residents were in full support of the “magnesium project.” After overcoming one hurdle in the development process, Utah Governor Calvin L. Rampton proclaimed the facility “an important breakthrough toward the establishment of a major complex on the Great Salt Lake. It will have a huge impact upon the economy and the industrial growth of Utah” (TT-B 1968). MagCorp leases 100,000 acres of land owned by the Bureau of Land Management land and produces approximately 45,000 dry tons of magnesium a year, making it the third-largest producer of the mineral in the world. The facility, which includes evaporation ponds and a refinery, employs between 500 and 700 people, making it the fourth-largest employer in the county (Thomas Consultants 2002). Supporting industries for MagCorp and various tax-revenue streams associated with the company and affiliated economic enterprises also directly benefit the economy of Tooele County.

Although MagCorp has been extolled for its economic benefits, the company's noxious NIMBY aspects are dramatic. Magnesium chloride is obtained by evaporating water from the Great Salt Lake in open-air enclosures. The evaporate is processed to separate the magnesium from the chloride in the MagCorp plant, so tens of thousands of tons of deadly chlorine pollutes the atmosphere every year. In 2001, for example, 55,000 tons escaped into the atmosphere. MagCorp had the dubious distinction of being ranked by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency as the nation's worst toxic polluter for many years, often outpacing its closest competitor by a large margin (EPA 2001).

The closest town to the MagCorp plant in Tooele County is Grantsville. Temperature inversions, common in mountainous landscapes during the winter, are particularly severe in the town because so much errant chlorine is in the air. MagCorp has reduced its pollutant emissions, but on days when the odor is particularly bad many residents, particularly children, choose to stay indoors while others brave the toxic weather, calmly remarking that it “smells like jobs to me” (Ward 1999, 190–191). The acceptance of noxious NIMBYS in Tooele County and the willingness to live with the negative side effects illustrate the widespread acquiescence to the dominant label of toxic as a natural part of how Tooele County should be viewed and the overriding importance of land-use activities that provide employment for local residents regardless of their potential negative effects.

As it did in Warren County, hazardous waste disposal first came to Tooele County as the uninvited guest of state regulators. The Vitro uranium smelter in southern Salt Lake County had left a heritage of more than 4 million tons of radioactive tailings from the uranium boom of the 1950s. Salt Lake City and its suburbs petitioned the state to clean up these tailings, and, after identifying a number of potential locations, officials selected Tooele County's West Desert (see Figure 3). The site seemed perfect for hazardous waste storage activities: remote, with an arid climate and low-permeability soils, state owned, and with little apparent value. Tooele County officials, though, were not anxious to house the Vitro tailings because they believed that the county already had more than its fair shareof hazardous materials. For ten years the county fought the proposed state action, but the protests only delayed the inevitable: Between 1985 and 1987 the state transported the Vitro uranium tailings 50 miles westward to Clive because Salt Lake County residents and the State of Utah viewed Tooele County as the most convenient local place for a toxic landfill (Verdoia 2001; Mogren 2002). That is, the initial geographical characteristics of Tooele County that limited agricultural development and population settlement eventually provided a geographical ratio-nale for the location of noxious facilities (Figure 5). The tailings are stored in above-grade disposable cells designed to contain them for 1,000 years. Perhaps the most important outcome of the long negotiation is that Tooele County managed to gain some economic benefit from their new NIMBY. The county would receive $100,000 annually for fifteen years from Salt Lake County in exchange for hosting the waste (Bern 1987b).

This event became an important benchmark for Tooele County residents regarding acceptable land uses and a wake-up call showing how little control they had over their own destiny. Although county residents and officials had viewed the ore-processing mill and the military facilities as economically beneficial and even patriotic, the imposition of the Vitro tailings was a clear demonstration of the county's economic and political weakness. The realization reinforced the willingness of local leaders to maximize the economic benefits of their situation by accepting potentially toxic NIMBYS. In a 2001 interview, County Commissioner Teryl Hunsaker recalled the paradigm shift: "The commissioners then at that time decided that because of what had happened it would be well if they commercialized on the hazardous waste industry out on the west desert" (Verdoia 2001). Although the county leaders had vociferously opposed the Vitro tailings, the money they eventually convinced the state to pay them opened their eyes to the potential wealth associated with hazardous waste storage facilities.

Before the first load of the Vitro tailings arrived at Clive, the county approved two more proposals for hazardous waste landfills; and by the time the operator opened the first disposal facility, the Tooele County Commission was considering two proposals for hazardous-waste incinerators (Figure 6). Between 1981 and 1993 the commission approved the development of Envirocare, a large hazardous waste disposal, and two waste incinerators began full operations in the West Desert. County Commission members saw these actions as logical. One commissioner remarked, "The ground where the Vitro tailings are . . . is already tainted. It has no other commercial use" (quoted in Bern 1987a).

Other hazardous waste managers recognized the potential financial rewards for using the West Desert as a hazardous waste storage and disposal site. In February 1987 two hazardous waste handlers, USPCI and Aptus Environmental, approached Tooele County for permission to operate waste incinerators in the West Desert. County officials favored the proposals from the outset, apparently concluding that the desert would not be negatively impacted by hazardous waste activity. Moreover, they believed that locating an incinerator in the county wouldentice other industries into the area, further helping their economy. As one county commissioner concluded when discussing noxious land uses and hazardous wastes, "We're [Tooele County residents] not new at this type of thing" (TT-B 1987).

In 1987 Tooele County commissioners voted to create Hazardous Industries Zoning Districts—the first in the United States—in order to “provide areas in appropriate remote locations where hazardous and radioactive wastes may be stored, treated and disposed of in a safe manner” (TCED 2005). Between 1988 and 1993 all of the districts’ current tenants arrived. Envirocare (now Energy Solutions) was the first to begin operations near Clive, followed by Aptus at Aragonite, and then USPCI at Clive. By 1993 the zone was complete, with two functioning hazardous waste landfills (Grassy Mountain and Envirocare) and two operating hazardous waste incinerators (Aragonite and Clive). Each facility paid the county a tippage fee (a fee for the right to dump or burn hazardous waste), initially set at $25.00 per ton for Envirocare and $180,000 per year for the Aragonite incinerator. The fees from hazardous waste dumping continue to be the largest single source of revenue for Tooele County (Thomas Consultants 2002).

## NONLOCAL PUBLIC PERCEPTION

Although locals accepted the idea that the West Desert is a sacrifice zone, the national press did not view what was happening in such a favorable light. A firestorm of negative attention erupted concerning Tooele County's willingness to accept hazardous NIMBYS, and the press castigated the county for allowing such noxious activities. Outside magazine ran a story in 1993 entitled "Happiness Is a Toxic Waste Zone" and claimed that "Tooele County is the most polluted place in America" (Webster 1993, 61).² Fortune magazine titled its article "IMBY, Please" and said that "in Tooele County, it's toxins over taxes any day" (Martin 1993, 13). Similarly, the New York Times ran at least three articles that discussed the county's hazardous activities (Brooke 1996; Egan 1996). Salt Lake City newspapers also expressed negative commentary regarding the hazardous waste industry in Tooele County—which is ironic, given that Salt Lake County and the State of Utah used their political muscle to site the Vitro tailings in Tooele County despite the resistance of Tooele residents. The publication of articles in the popular press identifying Tooele County as polluted and toxic is part of the labeling process and helped to create the dominant place myth that Tooele is toxic.

One result of this negative perception was that state legislators became concerned that a number of hazardous waste companies were selecting the West Desert for the development of additional facilities, so they enacted legislation that required any new facilities to be approved by both the legislature and the governor, taking control of the process away from Tooele County. Except for a proposal by the Goshute Indian Reservation (see Figure 3), over which the state has no control, no new proposals have emerged since the state passed that legislation (Spangler and Spangler 2001).

PERCEPTION AND PATH DEPENDENCE

Although the proposed Goshute nuclear waste storage site marked a turning point in statewide attitudes toward the increasing amount of toxic waste in the West Desert (Spangler and Spangler 2001), the attitudes of local residents have been less san- guine about the turning tide of opinion. The ambivalence of local attitudes stems from the fact that some longer-term residents would rather have toxic waste facili- ties that pay a living wage than increased population growth from new arrivals who are not economically tied to the county. Some residents do not mind the dominant label of “Toxic Tooele” if it keeps people from moving into, and changing the na- ture of, their small communities (Janofsky 2002). In fact, some residents embrace the perception of Tooele County as one of the nation’s premier dumping grounds because these residents do not believe in immediate health risks, and the percep- tion acts as a disincentive for people to move to Tooele County, which the local residents see as a more immediate threat (Wulfhorst 2000).

Local residents would like to be recognized for the sacrifices they have made by allowing hazardous land uses that are necessary for U.S. military and industrial projects; that is, for accepting waste products from more prosperous communities whose residents then censure them for doing so. Having so much toxic waste in their backyard has forced Tooele residents to face one of the main dialectical issuesof our day: Our current lifestyle generates enormous amounts of waste, some of which is toxic and harmful, and that waste has to go somewhere. A place has to be created—through the specificity of the natural environment (arid and isolated), social relationships (economically struggling and politically weak), and meanings (“We’re used to this type of stuff”)—in order for our society to function. As one local plan- ner remarked when confronted with disparaging statements concerning Tooele, “I know where my waste is, do you know where yours is?” (quoted in Janofsky 2002). Most of us do not, nor do we want to, know where our wastes go, thus places like Tooele are necessary. Tooele’s problem is that it is not quite isolated enough. As Salt Lake’s metropolitan population has grown and continues to grow, it is spilling over into Tooele County, where some residents see population growth as a more immediate threat than the risk that hazardous waste poses.

During the 1990s, particularly in the last part of the decade, Tooele County had the highest rates of population growth of any county in Utah. From 1990 to 2000, the county's population increased from 27,000 to 42,000 residents, and by 2007 another 16,000 individuals had moved to the county (U.S. Census Bureau 2008). Relatively low housing costs, availability of land, and proximity to Salt Lake City attracted the new residents, people who are not linked to the “old” economy of Tooele County and thus do not generally support the storage of addi- tional waste—especially high-level nuclear waste—just down the road. New popu- lation growth with limited economic ties to the local extractive, hazardous waste, or military economies will likely work to change the perception of Tooele County as a natural sacrifice zone.

## WASTING THE WEST

Tooele County's locational characteristics provide a compelling geographical con- text for selecting the area as a sacrifice zone, and, although these geographical char- acteristics are necessary for understanding the creation of “Toxic Tooele,” they are not sufficient. The development of rules with both intended and unintended con- sequences created an institutional infrastructure that facilitated the creation of Tooele as a sacrifice zone. Clearly the relatively weak local political economy of Tooele County vis-à-vis Salt Lake County, the state of Utah, and the federal government was an important factor in facilitating the idea of Tooele as a toxic waste dump. After being forced to accept hazardous waste from Salt Lake County and having the U.S. military locate numerous noxious facilities in its midst, Tooele County sought to capitalize on its (mis)fortune by creating Hazardous Industries Zoning Districts (specific place, with specific rules) to selectively attract similar land-use activities. The county's response reinforced the perception of Tooele as a toxic wasteland and sacrifice zone. Finally, labeling was used to justify the activities as legitimate and natural. In Tooele's case the dominant label came to be that of “Toxic Tooele,” a barren, godforsaken, unfit-for-living natural sacrifice zone.

Tooele's experience has important ramifications for other rural areas in the Mountain West. Numerous counties in the rural West can be characterized as iso-lated, with few natural amenities, stagnant economies, and out-migration. One result is that residents who are either unable or unwilling to relocate often accept the perception that their only hope for economic sustainability hinges on their willingness to accept noxious activities. In Utah alone a number of counties accept waste from outside the region. In one county, where mining was once the dominant economic activity and is still important, a local community constructed a large municipal waste facility in order to attract waste from all over the country; another county recently accepted low-level radioactive nuclear material from Japan for reprocessing; and a third is now home to the West's largest hog farm, producing more than 1 million hogs and their assorted wastes annually. In all of these counties local residents and their leaders have developed a rhetoric of despair in which the undesirable characteristics of their places become paramount in conversations, perceptions, and decisions, leading to accepting hazardous wastes and other so-called rich-area NIMBYS.

Desperate to stem the tide of out-migration and to provide jobs and a tax base, places have been and are being "created"—that is, rules developed, participants selected, meanings attached—not only for hazardous or toxic wastes but also for other activities, such as prisons, large-scale corporate feedlots for cattle, mega hog and dairy farms, and coal-fired power plants. More than merely acceptable to local populations, these activities are sought after and embraced. The economic difficulty and political weakness of these places enables negative place myths to be created, thus legitimizing the use of desperate and weak places as locations for the wastes from the rest of the country.

A number of other questions concerning the geography of waste in the rural West are worth pursuing. Probably the most important task is simply to document the growth of these types of land use over the past few decades, for no easily accessible data sources with this information exist. Other foci should include the different locational strategies of the different types of activities—both extensively and with numerous historical case studies—along with research on the differential impacts by type of activity. Although high-amenity areas have had and will continue to have a disproportionate impact on how the rural West is viewed, a different and not so glamorous rural geography is also being created. It is important that we understand the processes that are creating and maintaining a forgotten West and what the unintended consequences may be of locating noxious NIMBYS within fragile natural and social ecosystems.

Statehood for Utah in 1896 was accompanied by an "unwritten agreement" that it would change from a theocracy to a democracy in which the role of the Mormon Church in business and politics would be similar to the role of religion elsewhere in the Untied States. Consequently, during the early years of the twentieth century the church sold off its controlling interest in most businesses, keeping an interest only in those that provided jobs for the poor and needy (Arrington 1958). Anxious to prove that they were patriotic citizens of the United States the Mormons rapidly transformed their cultural milieu from one of public or private support for a quasi-independent Mormon Empire to one of vocal patriotism. By the time the United States entered World War I in 1917, "Unlike much of the rest of thecountry, where United States involvement originally met with widespread opposition, Utahans strongly supported the war effort" (McCormick and McCormick 2002, 177). At the time the U.S. military began to construct military bases in Tooele County the church's role was very indirect. It supported federal spending in Tooele primarily because it provided jobs in one of the most economically depressed parts of the state following the Great Depression, and because Utah's congressional representatives were Mormon it could be argued that the church was indirectly involved in attracting military activities to Tooele County. However, the official support for the military is found primarily in the non-Mormon newspapers, the *Salt Lake Tribune* and the *Tooele Transcript-Bulletin*.
