MEXICAN URBAN COLONIAS IN THE
SALT RIVER VALLEY OF ARIZONA

CHRIS LUKINBEAL, DANIEL D. ARREOLA, and D. DREW LUCIO

**ABSTRACT.** The history of the Salt River Valley is often characterized as a boomtown narrative that highlights the exploding Anglo population and rapid suburban sprawl that followed World War II. Frequently overlooked is the fundamental role that Mexicans played in the early suburbanization process through the formation of urban *colonias*, or suburban Mexican settlements. Early Mexican suburbs, despite their rural atmosphere, were connected to the pre-World War II urbanization process in the Salt River Valley. Our research seeks to amend and clarify the urban historical record by documenting the diverse and dispersed array of Mexican suburban settlements, a process generated by industrial agriculture, decentralization, and residential segregation. *Keywords: cotton camps, industrial agriculture, Mexican settlement patterns, suburban history, urban colonias.*

In 2005 the Salt River Valley,¹ commonly known as the “Phoenix metropolitan area,” comprised a vast sprawling, horizontal geography with 3.7 million residents. Modern settlement of this region derives from a massive post–World War II Anglo population immigration and suburbanization (Gammage 1999; Gober 2005).² This characterization comes from the “popular perception that suburban development is essentially a postwar phenomenon, with the 1950s being the touchstone decade” (Harris and Larkham 1999, 1). Mexicans were central to the early suburbanization process of the Salt River Valley through *colonia* settlements on the urban periphery, yet the notion that Phoenix developed as an almost entirely Anglo metropolis persists despite evidence of substantial Mexican roots in the city (Oberle and Arreola 2008).

In this article we show how and why urban colonias emerged across the Salt River Valley before the post–World War II–boom industrial agriculture practiced on the periphery shaped the area’s early-twentieth-century economy. “Industrial agriculture” refers to the extraction of a primary resource and achieves economies of scale when high levels of agricultural productivity and the size of production (in acreage) leads to low costs and high profits. Mexicans were critical to the success of this enterprise owing to their presence in the valley, their underclass status, and their knowledge of irrigation skills and labor practices in arid environments.

Urban colonias in the Salt River Valley formed in one of three ways: as working-class neighborhoods on the fringe that filtered down from Anglos to Mexicans, as settlements based on real estate promotions in environmentally sensitive areas or on unproductive lands, known as “disamenity zones,” or as settlements forged by company-sponsored housing schemes. The first process is characteristic of Phoenix’s southern fringe beyond the Salt River, neighborhoods abandoned by Anglos after

The second method is responsible for the majority of urban colonias, most of which were proximate to industrial agricultural areas or existing Anglo towns (Vinson 1991; Reynolds 1998; Harner 2000; Benitez 2002). The third type came in the form of cotton camps associated with industrial agriculture (Figure 1). We contend that the latter two formation processes were central to the history of early suburbanization in the Salt River Valley. In the United States suburbs are commonly “defined as political entities, democratically run and with constitutional power” (Harris 2001, 259; see also, Ashton 1984; Binford 1985; Keating 1988). But this definition only describes a portion of the formation and experience of the suburbanization process, especially as it relates to the United States before the 1950s (Harris and Lewis 2001). Suburbs are conceived in a number of different ways, which include being peripheral to the urban core (Clawson and Hall 1973; Gardner 2001;Harris 2001, 2004), being residential or industrial (Douglass 1925; Harris and Ullman 1945), comprising low-density settlements (Clawson and Hall 1973; Gardner 2001; Harris 2001, 2004), having high levels of owner occupance (Jackson 1985), and exhibiting a distinctive way of life that is characteristic of the middle and upper classes (Sies 2001).

Though not politically autonomous communities or emphasizing the middle to upper classes, urban colonias in the Salt River Valley fit within the new urban history definition of suburban formation in the pre–World War II era, especially as it relates to industrial decentralization and the marketing of inexpensive land on the urban fringe (Harris 1996, 2001, 2004; Harris and Larkham 1999; Harris and Lewis 1998, 2001; Gardner 2001, Wiese 2001). Industrial decentralization in the Salt River Valley led to the formation of suburbs in the pre–World War II era through the creation of cotton camps and through real estate ventures that marketed cheap land in and outside disamenity zones (Curtis 2004). These processes allowed Mexicans to become homeowners at a period in the past when homeownership by members of the group was uncommon.

Our research follows a hermeneutical method, an interpretative and iterative process of querying existing textual assumptions and primary source materials to develop new knowledge (Bohman 1993; Gadamer 2004). We stitch disparate theories and information from urban history, the history of the Salt River Valley, and ethnic geography to expand our knowledge of the process of Mexican urban colonia formation in the region. Most of the primary sources we consulted came from three special collections at the Arizona State University Libraries: the Chicano Research Collection; the Arizona Collection; and the Litchfield Park Collection. Other primary documentation came from the Goodyear Tire and Rubber Company archives at the University of Akron and from the Litchfield Park Historical Society. In addition, we interviewed key informants about the Mexican history of Litchfield Park. Historical maps and aerial photographs, along with field reconnaissance, substantiate claims from those interviews and corroborate map data.

We focus first on the early years of settlement (1865–1910) in the Salt River Valley to highlight the role of Mexican irrigators and laborers in establishing an urban economy based on agriculture. Next, we show how Mexican labor was central to the formation of industrial agriculture, especially as it relates to the production of cotton where Mexicans came to serve as an agricultural labor force. These sections lay the foundation for understanding how the Salt River Valley’s decentralized urban economy created a dispersed urban colonia settlement pattern. Finally, drawing on this foundation, we map and discuss the urban colonias that formed throughout the valley from the early 1900s through 1945.

## MEXICAN IRRIGATORS AND FARM LABORERS

In 1865 the U.S. Army founded Fort McDowell, on the eastern edge of the Salt River Valley, to protect residents in the mining districts of Prescott and Wickenburg to the north. The military post also attracted Mexicans to the valley (Simkins 1989), themajority being Sonorans with roots in Arizona that stretched back to the Mexican and Spanish periods (Rosales n.d.). At almost every step, Mexicans guided their Anglo counterparts in developing the valley's agricultural economy, digging the trunk canals and providing labor to plant and harvest crops. Mexicans not only engaged in canal construction as common laborers and to benefit their own agricultural homesteads but also served as contractors on canal projects (Kupel 1993; Solliday 1993; Dimas 1999, 24–26). They inherited their knowledge of irrigation farming from their Spanish forebears, who, under royal charter, had founded settlements in the river valleys of the Sonoran desert (Sheridan 1986; Officer 1987). Mexicans brought that know-how to the Salt River Valley settlements, where skilled workers became “ditch bosses” who oversaw irrigation systems (Solliday 1993, 44) (Figure 2).

The construction of early canals led to further economic development, increasing the labor market, which Mexicans filled. In the 1870 U.S. federal census half of Phoenix's population was Mexican (Luckingham 1994, 17). Mexicans worked on the original survey of the town site and maintained the first public streets. They also built many of the early structures in the valley, relying on adobe to create a vernacular landscape that reflected their Sonoran roots (Oberle and Arreola 2008). Ironically, they built many of those earthen structures and dwellings for Anglos (Garcia 2008).

The completion of the Maricopa and Phoenix Railway brought intercontinental service to the Salt River Valley in 1887, an event that hastened the destruction of the early adobe built environment (Lamb 1981; Pierce and Parish 1996; Dean and Reynolds 2006). The arrival of the railroad caused a wave of Anglo immigration and replacement of adobe buildings with brick construction, allowing Anglo transplants to re-create the familiar cultural landscapes of their eastern homes (Martínez 2001, 107; Luckingham 1994).

Land-acquisition practices transformed the once-significant Mexican farming class into a landless peasantry (Dimas 1999, 2–10). During the recession of the 1870s many Mexicans could not obtain enough capital or supplies to sustain an agricultural homestead. This made them susceptible to financial arrangements that placed liens on their property so that they could receive money for seeds. Through these means, land speculators, such as Michael Wormser, a merchant from Prescott, held title to many Mexican homesteads and owned the canal system for those homesteads as well. Wormser, therefore, could persuade reluctant landowners to sell their homesteads at his price by cutting off their water allotment, thus rendering them unproductive (Dean and Reynolds 2006). Wormser eventually controlled 9,000 acres south of the Salt River, between the towns of Phoenix and Tempe (Dean and Reynolds 2006; Rosales n.d.).

## THE AGRICULTURAL UNDERCLASS AND THE COTTON ECONOMY

An urban economy based on industrial agriculture in the Salt River Valley depended on stabilizing the water supply. The capricious nature of the Salt River thwarted the ambitions of local farmers: The river displayed cycles of violent flood and dry periods lasting nearly a decade. Local leaders envisioned a water-storage system that would provide stability and sought federal funding through the National Reclamation Act of 1902. However, the act limited corporate use of water, thus restricting farmers in the Salt River Valley, where private water rights meant that users were outside the scope of the public interest. The principal sticking point was removal of the overlapping water rights of multiple users, especially large landowners who stood to gain at the expense of potential small farmers, the principal beneficiaries of the reclamation work. The result was the incorporation of the Salt River Valley Water Users Association (SRVWUA) in February 1903, which consolidated individual water rights under the authority of a public-private cooperative. By eliminating the contentious issue of multiple water users, the secretary of interior deemed the Salt River Valley suitable for federal reclamation funds on 14 March 1903. Despite arguments for local oversight instead of centralized control, the SRVWUA subscribed more than 142,000acres of land by June 1903; the cap was set at 200,000 acres (K. L. Smith 1986, 45). The crowning achievement of the National Reclamation Act for the SRVWUA was the Roosevelt Dam, completed in 1911 east of the valley in an upland zone. The dam created the major keystone reservoir to impound and regulate water use in the region.

Construction of the Roosevelt Dam created one serious hurdle: full repayment of debt in ten years. The project cost four times the original estimate, but users were still required to repay it in the original time allotted. New settlers had to shoulder these costs in addition to their normal operating expenses, such as home construction, clearing and improving land, and regular SRVWUA water assessments. Responsibility for this outcome lay with the Reclamation Service, which failed to coordinate the physical resources of the project with the human resources (K. L. Smith 1986, 112–114). Although farmers in the Salt River Valley had the highest average crop value per farm, paying for project overruns left little profit at the end of the year. For sufficient profit, local farmers had to expand production while reducing costs, so they turned to migrant Mexican labor (Reisler 1976, 38–39).

The Mexican Revolution ignited in 1911, sending large numbers of refugees into the United States, many of whom came to the Salt River Valley. They too were incorporated into the burgeoning Mexican agricultural underclass. Farmers exploited Mexicans to realize their profits from the increased acreage delivered by water from the Roosevelt Dam. The crop that most epitomized this profit motive was cotton. Long-staple Egyptian cotton became important to the manufacture of tires and airplane cloth, especially as the United States entered World War I. Industries scrambled to acquire adequate supplies to manufacture these goods because Great Britain had embargoed imports from Egypt to protect its own supplies in 1914, and a boll-weevil infestation had decimated the only United States domestic source, Sea Island, South Carolina (Hill 2007).

The introduction of cotton to the Salt River Valley began in 1870, but not on an industrial scale (Christy 1920). In 1901 the U.S. government opened an experimental farm at Yuma, Arizona, to develop a variety of cotton amenable to the state's climate. After successive tests, the Department of Agriculture's Office of Foreign Seed and Plant Introduction released a domestic variety, known as “Pima” (Christy 1920; S. M. Smith 1948; Hill 2007). This achievement led to the first commercial planting in 1912; and, with that success, local growers formed cotton exchanges. These cooperative enterprises provided proper ginning facilities and secured sufficient labor to pick the cotton. The Goodyear Tire and Rubber Company of Akron, Ohio, a major consumer of long-staple cotton, conducted extensive tests of the cotton grown in the Salt River Valley. It concluded Arizona Pima to be the best available cotton. Goodyear purchased land, invested in the construction of ginning facilities, and granted loans to individual growers in the valley. As the price of long-staple cotton soared, the small, family homesteads gave way to speculative operators who created “desert sweatshops” practicing “a large-scale industrialized type of agriculture” (McWilliams 1942, 73). For cotton producers in the valley, revenues of $350,000 in a single season during the 1910s were not uncommon.Cotton agriculture demanded a stable labor supply, but the 1917 Immigration Act—the nation’s first comprehensive immigration policy—placed the supply in peril. Because Mexican nationals were obliged to appear before local draft boards, large numbers of migrant laborers returned home for fear of military conscription (Reisler 1976, 26). The Arizona Cotton Growers Association (ACGA) estimated more than 15,000 Mexicans had been repatriated by fears of conscription (Knox 1918). Furthermore, the 1917 act effectively banned a corporation, association, or society from paying for contracting of foreign laborers, either skilled or unskilled. From 1918 to 1919, the ACGA was short 28,000 laborers.

The 1917 Immigration Act contained a discretionary clause, however, that authorized the commissioner of immigration, with prior approval from the secretary of labor, to “exact bonds to control admission of those otherwise inadmissible aliens applying for admission” in the event of a labor shortage (Bennett 1963, 28; Scruggs 1988, 75). Local farmers and organizations lobbied the secretary of labor to grant the commissioner authority to exempt Mexican labor. The secretary did so, validating his actions because of the reliance on Mexican labor. During the 1918–1919 growing season, special trains ferried thousands of Mexicans and their families across the border (Luckingham 1994, 32–33; Dimas 1999, 26–30).

The ACGA enlisted the services of labor recruiters, or *enganchadores*, an influential force in the regional labor market since the late nineteenth century. The recruiters contracted Mexicans at border crossings, at railroad stops inside the United States, or by traveling into Mexico (Solliday 1993). In total, the ACGA had brought 33,460 Mexican laborers into the valley by the end of the 1920–1921 growing season, for which it had paid nearly $320,000 (U.S. Congress 1926). The savings to valley growers was an estimated $28,000,000 (J. Smith 2004, 219). The cotton boom saw the Mexican population of Maricopa County clustered in the Salt River Valley increase to 17,915, or 20 percent of the population in 1920 (Dean and Reynolds 2006, 27).

Planting of cotton peaked in the 1920–1921 season, with a record 180,000 acres in production. The large harvest required a substantial labor force, but falling cotton prices led farmers to withhold laborers’ pay. An investigation found that Mexican wages went unpaid and that commissaries operated at the various cotton camps built by growers were charging exorbitant prices. Excessive transportation costs deducted from Mexicans’ wages caused additional suffering. By 1921 incomes averaged only $18 a week; half went to cover transportation costs, and the remainder stood as credit at the commissaries (McWilliams 1942, 76–77). Due to falling cotton prices, thousands of Mexicans were unemployed or stranded with no means of returning home (Peterson 1975, 28–30; Luckingham 1994, 32–33). Unemployed farmworkers drifted to Glendale, Tempe, Mesa, or Phoenix, where employment opportunities and relief were available. Local newspapers reported that most were without shelter and “literally starving” (Kotlanger 1983, 426).

Growers had to pay for return transportation to Mexico, but due to the 1921 cotton bust, many could not pay this cost. Other farmers chose to ignore their obli-gations, thus saving by not paying to transport additional workers for the following
season. In Phoenix, the city manager collected hundreds of Mexican families and
deposited them at the doorstep of the ACGA headquarters in Tempe, instructing
them "to take these people of yours until they can be deported" (McWilliams 1942,
78). The ACGA reached an agreement with the Mexican government to return Mexi-
cans free of charge and reimburse them for all unpaid wages. Of those stranded,
estimates suggest that repatriation amounted to only one-tenth of the 15,000–20,000
Mexicans in the Salt River Valley (Hoffman 1974, 123; Luckingham 1994, 43).

Although the price of cotton revived in 1922, valley growers had learned a hard lesson and diversified agricultural production, harvesting cotton alongside other labor-intensive cash crops, such as citrus, vegetables, and other produce. The result of the cotton boom, however, kept agriculture a corporate enterprise. The scale of this agriculture forced operators to continue to rely on inexpensive Mexican labor. U.S. Department of Labor statistics revealed the importance of Mexicans to the regional economy: They constituted 85 percent of all railroad-track workers, 75 percent of all vegetable, fruit, and beet pickers, and 50 percent of all cotton pickers (Reisler 1976, 57).

New immigration legislation in 1921 and 1924, using country-of-origin quotas,
continued to exclude Mexicans from immigration control. This exclusion drew pro-
tests from anti-immigrant forces in the Congress, prompting representatives to tes-
tify before the Committee on Immigration and Naturalization in early 1926 to defend
the practice of bringing seasonal farm labor in from Mexico (U.S. Congress 1926).
One representative, appealing to the need for the continued exemption of Mexi-
cans, testified to the committee that without the requisite labor the valley would go
back to growing grains, “[sending] upon the market a commodity of which a dis-
tressing surplus” already existed (p. 32). The ACGA representative testified before the
committee that the association’s attempts to contract domestic labor had proved
unfruitful.

The stock market crash of 1929 silenced these debates, as local and state govern-
ments repatriated Mexicans in order to reduce welfare rolls (Hoffman 1974). Some
Mexican nationals decided to voluntarily leave the United States, thus avoiding offi-
cial repatriation (Titcomb 1984). Southwestern growers fought repatriation, seeing
the frenzied actions of government agents as potentially impacting their very liveli-
hoods. Repatriated Mexicans reported that contractors were attempting to recruit
workers to pick cotton in Texas and Arizona on their arrival home (Balderrama and
Rodriguez 1995).

Despite the repatriation of thousands in the 1930s, Mexicans remained firmly
entrenched in both resident and migratory farm-labor positions in Maricopa County
(Urtuzuastegui 1972; Hoffman 1974, 122; Dimas 1999, 27). The importance of Mexi-
cans to Salt River Valley cotton producers was underscored in 1932 when cotton
growers came up short 7,500 field hands because local Mexicans refused to work for
low wages. Farmers asked the governor to order welfare boards to introduce a “no
work, no eat” policy to force pickers back into the fields (Kotlanger 1983, 443).These historic events shaped the rise of a decentralized urban economy based on agriculture and cotton. To achieve economies of scale utilizing Mexican labor, urban colonias, the settlements that housed many in this population, needed to be close to places of employment.

## URBAN COLONIAS IN THE SALT RIVER VALLEY

The earliest Mexican settlers in the Salt River Valley labored for Anglos, building canals and working agricultural fields (D. Smith 1990; Solliday 1993; Cofflet St. Clair and St. Clair 2006; Burruel 2007; Barrios 2008; Vega 2009). The large influx of Mexicans in the 1910s coincided with the full maturation of Anglo capitalism across the Southwest, evidenced in an industrial-style agricultural economy (Camarillo 1979; Gonzalez 1994). As industrial agriculture gave birth to cotton gins and packing sheds, urban colonias spread throughout the Salt River Valley. In this way, Mexicans became active agents in the early suburbanization of the region. In many cases they participated in suburbanization by purchasing real estate, for the lack of onerous building regulations in unincorporated fringes meant that they could build their own houses.

Most of these urban colonias formed along Phoenix's south side, extending from the Southern Pacific railroad tracks to the Salt River (Figure 3). The original Mexican community of Phoenix had been established much earlier, during the formative 1870s. That community existed in a four-block neighborhood east of Central Avenue, the city's main north-south arterial, along Monroe Street (Oberle and Arreola 2008). The local Anglo press dubbed this area the “Sonoran Corner of Phoenix.”

South side settlements began to emerge in the 1870s, when unemployed Mexican and Chinese railroad workers migrated to the Salt River Valley to work in nearby agricultural fields (Murray and Solliday 2007). The zone became a working-class area after the railroad arrived in 1887, as factories and warehouses took over the best sites along the route (Barrios 2008, 7–8). A major flood in 1891 created a classic disamenity zone, and later redlining of properties effectively deeded the area to Mexicans (Luckingham 1994; Brunk 1996). Anglos departed for higher ground north of town, and areas on the south side became ethnically and racially diverse (Reynolds 1998; Arreola 2007). The oldest colonia to develop on the south side was La Palestina (later Grant Park), which began when a local real estate speculator sold and mortgaged land to people of Mexican ancestry. Property records suggest that most of the property owners in the neighborhood had Spanish surnames by 1916 (Reynolds 1998). The Mexican communities of Grant Park, La Sonorita, and Harmon Park evolved from the subdivision of agricultural land west of Central Avenue (Dean and Reynolds 2006, 32–37).

Several blocks west of Golden Gate the colonia of Cuatro Milpas formed in a similar fashion (Dean and Reynolds 2006, 33). The original four property owners subdivided land in the mid-1920s, offering commercial and residential lots to prospective buyers. Mexicans lived east of 7th Street; Anglos, on the west side. Social researchers of the time described the smaller Little Hollywood as a “foul slum, the like of which can probably not be found elsewhere in the United States” (Phoenix Community Chest 1946, 3). The destitution of some residents forced them to rent their property and set up tents or fashion adobe bricks from local soil to make a home. These colonias formed outside the city limits and lacked all manner of city services.

The original Mexican community, known as Sonorita, formed on the south side of the trunk line built to the sugar-beet factory (Figure 5). Mexican residents in Glendale found work in the town's agricultural economy (Cofflet St. Clair 2006). During the Great Depression, Mexican miners from the copper towns in central Arizona migrated to the Glendale area seeking work as agricultural laborers. They congregated at a location west of the original Mexican colonia, across the tracks of the Atchison, Topeka, & Santa Fe Railroad. The community came to be known as “Gunnysack Barrio,” a comment on the residents’ habit of using gunnysacks filled with water as a form of early evaporative cooling (Smith and Ilardo 2000).

The farming town of Mesa, which developed in the 1870s as a Mormon colony, witnessed the formation of several Mexican colonias along its unincorporated fringe during the early twentieth century (Simkins 1989). The soil being extremely fertile, pioneers set about turning the area into a veritable cornucopia of agricultural production, growing alfalfa, sugar beets, citrus, almonds, grapes, and cantaloupes (J. Smith 2004) (Figure 6). Mexicans began to arrive in large numbers around 1910, taking jobs as agricultural workers, especially with the increasing production of cotton in the Mesa area (Melvin 1933). On the northern border of Mesa real estate speculators created a new residential subdivision for Mexicans. Verde Vista had small lots, narrow streets, and no deed restrictions that allowed industrial uses in an otherwise strictly residential zone. The major north-south thoroughfares of Verde Vista had Spanish surnames, “García” and “Martínez,” later changed to “Sirrine” and “Pasadena,” respectively (City of Mesa 1997). This community grew into the barrio of Washington Park /Escobedo, which included a black neighborhood created in 1910 from the homestead of a veterinarian named James Livingstone (City of Mesa 1981) (Figure 7).

In Mesa more colonias formed around agricultural centers. The largest was on the south side of town, along the railroad tracks, near the citrus packinghouse and cotton gin. A smaller colonia, Little Juarez, was nestled in citrus groves west of the town cemetery (Benitez 2002; J. Smith 2004).

A Mexican colonia developed in Scottsdale around 1910, a town located about 5 miles north of Tempe (Harner 2000). The land that would become the city of Scottsdale had originally been part of a 640-acre tract purchased by Winfield Scott in 1888. The land was agriculturally productive, for it received sufficient water from the Arizona Canal (see Figure 1). Scott transformed his homestead into a fruit ranch and became one of the principal commercial citrus producers in the valley. The need for labor brought Sonorans from Mexico to the area, most of whom sought job opportunities and refuge from the Mexican Revolution. The first homes constructed in Scottsdale's Mexican colonia were located on the east side of the townsite. Over time, the original makeshift structures built by the first Mexican residents became adobe homes. By 1926 local residents called Scottsdale's Mexican community “Eskatel” (Burruel 2007, 36–37).

The first Mexicans in the Chandler area were ranch hands attached to the 18,000-acre ranch of Dr. Alexander John Chandler. Unable to profit from land he had acquired through speculative activities as manager of the Consolidated Canal Company, Chandler subdivided the property into lots and founded a town (Simkins 1989). Chandler, the town, exemplified how design and amenities could promote urban growth. The centerpiece was the Hotel San Marcos, which grew into one of the nation's leading winter resorts (Solliday 1993). The first Mexicans settlers in Chandler were contractors who built many of the new town's structures, including the San Marcos Hotel, and agricultural workers on the farms that developed from the subdivision of Chandler's land (Figure 8).

The first colonia in Chandler, known as “Southside,” was located in an area zoned for industrial use. The community expanded southward toward Pecos Road. Residents who lived outside the corporate limits were squatters on land deemed unproductive by farmers because it comprised sand hills. No Mexicans lived north of Chandler Boulevard, the main east-west thoroughfare, because deed restrictions there required homes to be appraised at $2,000 or more (Jensen 2004). Mexicans also founded a colonia west of town first known as “El Chamizo,” a reference to a type of scrub brush that grew in the area. Its residents were refugees from the Mexican Revolution who provided labor to nearby farms. The population of El Chamizo increased during the 1920s and 1930s, causing the landowner to separate the tract from his land and register the subdivision of Hightown, or Pueblo Alto (Killoren n.d.) (see Figure 8). Residents built their own homes, using whatever materials were available, such as salvaged metal, scrap lumber, and cardboard.

Cotton production first came to the fields around Chandler in 1912 (Reynolds 2006). The crop's success prompted the formation of the Egyptian Cotton Growers Association, which subsequently received permission from the Chandler Improvement Company to build a cotton gin and warehouse (Christy 1920). Chandler and his team of promoters marketed his former ranch land as prime real estate for cotton production (Reynolds 2006). Such activity attracted the attention of the Goodyear Tire and Rubber Company, which sought new supplies of cotton to augment diminishing supplies in the midst of World War I. Goodyear, acting through its local subsidiary, the Southwest Cotton Company, purchased 8,000 acres of Chandler's land. The presence of such a large corporate industry in the Salt River Valley commercialized agriculture and provided another pathway for Mexicans to participate in the suburbanization process via cotton camps.

Such actions were an expression of welfare capitalism or corporate paternalism, meant to extract loyalty from the workers (Alamillo 2006). Local producers in the Salt River Valley followed suit as the price of long-staple cotton skyrocketed, reproducing a paternalistic farmworker landscape that was populated largely by families. Provision of housing was important, because cotton picking was a family endeavor (U.S. Congress 1926). According to E. D. Tetreau (1939), 1 square mile of irrigated farmland required 10.6 families. At the peak of cotton production in 1920, and assuming that the average Mexican family consisted of two parents and eight children, the approximate size of the farm-labor force in the Salt River Valley approached 30,000 people (Solliday 1993, 97). The result was a dispersed network of farm-labor camps throughout the valley (see Figure 1).

Cotton camps often formed at the intersection of major roads (Hill 2007). Vehicles were essential to the cotton camps: They ferried workers to the fields and transported picked cotton to the numerous gins built in the late 1910s (Christy 1920). According to Solliday (1993, 98), the Southwest Cotton Company standardized the form and location of cotton camps by building Mexican villages at strategic points on its property. Dwellings were in the native style of houses with ramadas shaded by vines. Permanent Mexican workers and their families resided in these houses, whereas others who only worked seasonally stayed in tents and camped outside. Cotton-camp conditions reflected the finances of individual operators (McWilliams 1942; Hill 2007). Lacking the capital of larger, corporate owners, small growers—the bulk of growers in the Salt River Valley—chose to neglect the needs of their laborers, consigning them to shelters hastily erected on a property. To maintain economies of scale, costs had to remain low and one way of assuring that they did was to exploit labor. The quick cash that cotton farmers could make in a season, combined with the fluidity of the farm-labor market, led small growers to view proper camps as an added expense that cut into their profits. Basic services were extremely limited: no electricity, water from tanks or directly out of nearby irrigation canals, and only common privies as toilets. Camps were fertile ground for any number of diseases, leading the Arizona State Board of Health to declare that operating “under such conditions should be prohibited by law in the interest of public health and common humanity” (Urtuzuastegui 1972, 10). The typical cotton camp was a landscape of simple canvas tents often erected on sun-baked mud flats, although some did offer barrack housing (Urtuzuastegui 1972).

Workers who could not secure accommodation in the grower camps typically found shelter in informal camps (McWilliams 1942, 81), many of which were located along a major roadway or intersection, where canals provided adequate water (see Figure 1). Conditions were much more primitive in such camps. Cotton pickers built shacks, erected tents, or lived out of their cars; the most unfortunate of them sought shelter under railroad trestles and bridges (Kotlanger 1983). The typical informal camp in the Salt River Valley contained an average of fifty families; one camp outside Phoenix contained more than 3,704 families, 42 percent of them Mexican (McWilliams 1942, 87). Growers who did not provide shelter hired labor from the informal camps (Toepel 1971; Urtuzuastegui 1972). Pickers preferred informal camps built by entrepreneurs because families were free from the high prices charged by growers’ field commissaries. At informal camps private businesses sprang up toprovide a variety of services, including such as gas stations, grocery stores, and bars. Such services also gave Mexicans an opportunity to earn extra money from side jobs. Informal camps were the genesis of the predominantly Mexican farm towns of Agua Fria and Marinette (Toepel 1971; Duran 2006).

Corporate growers like Goodyear were well financed and, therefore, provided shelters for their entire workforce, both Mexican and Anglo. Litchfield Park, as one example, constructed camps with well-managed living facilities that included stores, churches, and schools (Arreola and others 2008). Mexicans were nonetheless segregated from Anglos, in wood frame or adobe homes. These early segregated suburbs were later incorporated or annexed by cities. Even though the post–World War II era dramatically expanded suburban sprawl that razed many urban colonias, the last cotton camps in Litchfield Park did not close until 1986.

## THE MEXICAN LEGACY IN THE SALT RIVER VALLEY

The history of Mexican suburbanization in the Salt River Valley began well before the city of Phoenix incorporated (1881) and Arizona became a state (1912). Mexican labor was crucial to the early decentralized agricultural economy and helped to expand this labor-intensive economy far beyond the floodplain of the Salt River. The result was the founding of numerous Mexican colonias scattered throughout the urban fringe of the valley, linked by a network of canals, rail lines, and roads. Originally, most colonias formed outside city limits and thus lacked city services, had no ethnic/racial deed restrictions, and no minimum construction standards. In time, land speculators created segregated subdivisions that attracted Mexicans to peripheral settlements. The end of urban colonias in the Salt River Valley came in 1945 with the establishment of Victory Acres in Tempe. Thereafter, municipalities began to regulate unplanned residential subdivisions.

holdings into a premier suburban development (Lucio 2007). Architects sought to build a master village plan that paid homage to romanticized suburbs and did not incorporate its Mexican heritage.

As Mary Sies put it, “we are sometimes guilty of using a post–World War II suburban stereotype to describe the residential suburbs of earlier eras” (2001, 317). Urban colonias in the Salt River Valley are a case in point. These suburban settlements began in the late nineteenth century and continued through the mid-twentieth century. New urban historians are breaking down the normative view that pre–World War II suburbs are defined strictly in terms of political entities. In the Salt River Valley urban colonias mark a distinct chapter in suburban formation, one tied to industrial decentralization and the need for a large labor pool to support economies of scale. Pre–World War II urban colonias proliferated on the urban fringe where an absence of deed restrictions permitted real estate promotions to target Mexicans, allowing them to become homeowners. Many settlements based on real estate promotions were in peripheral zones. Company-sponsored housing forged suburban communities through the formation of cotton camps. The era of cotton camps lasted fifty years and spanned the pre– and post–World War II suburbanization period. Although most of the early settlements no longer exist,their legacy in the early suburbanization process should not be overlooked, for they played a major role in establishing peripheral settlement patterns that persist to this day in the Phoenix metropolitan area.

The persistence of the Salt River Valley’s boomtown narrative continues the neglect of the Mexican roots of early settlement in the region (Oberle and Arreola 2008). In the northwestern portion of the valley, for example, recent commercial development is paving over fields once worked by Mexican labor, yet the name attached to one of these new mixed-use spaces—Zanjero—harks back to the era of the first colonias where most irrigators (*zanjeros*) were Mexican (Figure 9). In the Salt River Valley, landscapes have come full circle over nearly a century. Yesterday, agriculture helped create the first suburban settlements, and Mexicans helped build those places. Today, suburban development transforms those fields, and ethnic Mexicans, along with others, live, work, dine, shop, and play in the new spaces. The historical geography of urban colonias sheds light on this contemporary transformation, alerting us to the deep roots and place-making significance of valley’s Mexican communities.

In just over a century the Salt River Valley has seen a dramatic shift in its demographic makeup. In 1870 Mexicans constituted roughly half of the population of the city of Phoenix, and the city projects that it will be predominantly Hispanic by 2010 (Arizona Republic 2003). Placing this recent demographic shift within a historic and geographical framework is essential to explaining how Mexicans have played, and continue to play, a critical role in the urbanization process of the fifth-largest city in the United States.

## NOTES

1. “Salt River Valley” refers to the drainage area encompassing the lower Salt and Verde Rivers in central Arizona (Haschenburger 1988, 3). The area is largely defined historically by the Salt River Project (SRP) lands south of the Arizona Canal and west of the Eastern Canal and includes the communities of Scottsdale, Glendale, Phoenix, Tempe, Mesa, and Chandler (Comeaux 1981, Map 6.7). The Gila River to the south and the Agua Fria River to the west are technically on the outer margins of the SRP, although irrigated agriculture was practiced in those drainages as well (Gober 2005, 45). In contemporary Phoenix, the “Valley” is a term used by the leading daily newspaper, the *Arizona Republic*, to define its local and state section, thus the term is understood by residents to be the near equivalent of metropolitan Phoenix.
2. The term “Anglo” is used colloquially in the U.S. Southwest to refer to white Americans or non-Hispanic/Latino persons. In its original usage it may have referred to Anglo-Saxons or anyone descended from early English or Northern European colonists (Meier and Rivera 1981, 17), but today it refers chiefly to persons of European descent—other than Mexicans—in the Southwest (Grebler, Moore, Guzman 1970, 16; Camarillo 1979, 1).
