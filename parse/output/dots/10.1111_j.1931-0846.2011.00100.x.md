AD HOC REGIONALISM IN RURAL DEVELOPMENT*

MAX LU

**ABSTRACT.** Government units such as counties and cities have long been the basis for economic development, service delivery, and problem solving in rural America, but they are increasingly inappropriate, because many of them are too small to reach the critical mass and because rural issues often transcend arbitrarily set political boundaries. To overcome the resultant adverse effects, many rural communities have been rethinking their development strategies and actively forging regional partnerships. Ad hoc regionalism is a politically feasible and effective way for rural communities to collaborate in these initiatives. The exact issues that spark such collaboration may vary from place to place, but they are all grassroots, voluntary regional governance frameworks or innovations. They allow rural communities to pool their resources and reach the critical mass necessary to tackle a variety of regional issues and take advantage of new economic development opportunities without replacing or threatening existing government units. The two case studies discussed in this article—the western Kansas Rural Economic Development Alliance and the San Juan Forum in the Four Corners region of the southwestern United States—show that ad hoc regionalism offers several advantages over rural communities working independently but that challenges in implementing it exist. *Keywords: ad hoc regionalism, economic development, Four Corners region, rural America, U.S. Southwest, western Kansas.*

Regionalism has a long and storied history in the economic development practices of the United States. As early as in 1935 the National Resources Committee advocated regions as crucial elements in dealing with social and economic problems and recommended the creation of multistate organizations that would apply regional development plans that followed guidelines provided by the national government. The Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA), launched in 1933, and the Appalachian Regional Commission (ARC), created in 1965, are perhaps the two best-known examples of large-scale regional development programs from the early and mid-twentieth century (Isserman and Rephann 1995; Boyce 2004). Both projects were a response to severe economic and social problems in their respective region, and in both cases “the nation’s fiscal resources and best minds were committed to the task of effecting qualitative change in the economic bases of relatively poor regions” (Pudup 1993, 177). Although the TVA later became mainly a public power company, the ARC is an example of the successful use of a regional approach to promote rural development (Isserman and Rephann 1995). Many other regional planning initiatives, including eight regional commissions modeled after the ARC, were created during the 1970s (Isserman 2002). In the 1980s, however, regional planning fell out of political favor. Economic development became the responsibility of local governments, especially cities and counties.

Instead of the earlier large-scale, top-down plans, the regional initiatives that are being promoted now are often grassroots, collaborative efforts, whereby neighboring communities voluntarily form a regional alliance based on the common issues or challenges they face, usually with some links to multicounty metropolitan organizations such as sewage districts and park districts. Some scholars have called this practice “ad hoc regionalism” (Porter and Wallis 2002; Hamin and Marcucci 2008). Ad hoc regional organizations have existed in large urban areas in the United States for quite some time, but now they are emerging across rural America too. Although the exact number of such organizations is difficult to determine, examples are legion.¹

The purpose of this article is to analyze the impetus for ad hoc regionalism in rural areas and to examine its characteristics and advantages in rural economic development and problem solving. Regionalism may connote different things to different people. It is not the same as what has generally been characterized as “new regionalism,” although the emergence of the former in rural America appears to have coincided with the debate on the latter (Lovering 1999, 2001; Tomaney and Ward 2000; Webb and Collis 2000; MacLeod 2001). New regionalism, especially in the European context, is usually predicated on the view that the relentless process of globalization and the shift from Fordism to post-Fordism have resulted in a rescaling or reterritorialization of political economic activity. The role of the nation-state is considered weakened due to its declining capability for carrying out Keynesian macroeconomic management—redistributive policies and strategic placement of public investments, for example. Instead, a region is now the most obvious site for economic activities and also the territorial sphere most suited to the interaction of political, social, and economic processes (Storper 1995; Keating 1996, 1997; Tomaney and Ward 2000; MacLeod 2001; Scott and Storper 2003; Brenner 2004; Hudson 2007). New regionalism tends to be about city regions, so much so that John Harrison insists that the proper term for the phenomenon should be “new city regionalism” (2007). Ad hoc regionalism in rural areas, on the other hand, often stems more from practical considerations than from a new understanding of global political economic dynamics (McDaniel 2003). It is a grassroots effort to help rural areas overcome the adverse effects of small populations and limited fiscal resources and reach the critical mass necessary for both tackling various problems that often transcend political boundaries and implementing new economic development strategies.

In this article I first make a case for thinking regionally and, more specifically, for adopting ad hoc regionalism by describing the changing socioeconomic realities and challenges in rural America. I then discuss the characteristics of ad hoc regionalism through two case studies—the western Kansas Rural Economic Development Alliance (WKREDA) and the San Juan Forum (SJF) in the Four Corners region of the southwestern United States. Both the WKREDA and the SJF embody the ad hoc regional approach in their organizational structures and activities, but they have fared differently. The former has been playing an important role in economicdevelopment in western Kansas since its inception in 1994, whereas the latter—located where Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico, and Utah join each other at right angles—ceased to function in 2005 following the departure of a key player. Their experiences provide valuable lessons about the challenges of implementing ad hoc regionalism.

## CHANGING RURAL REALITIES AND THE NEED TO THINK AND ACT REGIONALLY

Rural communities are adopting ad hoc regionalism in response to both changing socioeconomic realities and the challenges they face in dealing with a host of regional issues and in implementing new economic development strategies. Ad hoc regionalism appears to be an effective and politically feasible way to overcome the limitations of current political configurations and bring rural communities together to work toward maintaining or even enhancing their competitive advantages in the global economy and their quality of life.

Rural areas in the United States have been undergoing rapid social, economic, and demographic changes and face a number of challenges to their future well-being (Browne and Swanson 1995; Johnson 2001; Ward and Brown 2009). The remarkable success of U.S. agriculture in the second half of the twentieth century resulted in ever-expanding farms but ever-fewer farmworkers. Many parts of rural America have lost population since the early twentieth century (Albrecht 1993; Rathge and Highman 1998; Johnson and Rathge 2006). Along with a shrinking population, rural places have lost their political influence, their functional organizations have faded away, and their social fabric has worn thin (Jacobs 2005). The eroding tax base makes it increasingly difficult to provide essential services such as education and health care (McDaniel 2003). In areas that are experiencing population growth, residents face a different set of challenges. A larger population may expand the tax base and stimulate local economic activities, but it can also raise housing prices, increase traffic, and strain local infrastructure. The influx of new residents, particularly immigrants, often results in conflicts between long-term residents and newcomers (Kelsey 1998).

Rural areas are generally characterized by wide-open space and small, dispersed populations. Low land costs, lack of traffic congestion, and lack of pollution are important assets that traditionally attracted manufacturing industries to rural areas, but low population density—the hallmark of being rural—is a liability in modern economic development (Isserman 2001; Thompson 2007). It works against the development of agglomeration economies. Firms may find it difficult to reach the necessary customer threshold for their products and services, or a limited labor pool may inhibit business activities. In order to attract nonfarm businesses and create jobs, many local governments resort to incentive packages such as tax abatement and infrastructure upgrading, but they often find themselves competing with each other and therefore susceptible to exploitation by large corporations (Barlett and Steele 1998).The situation is compounded by globalization of the world economy (Thompson 2007; Longworth 2008). Rural areas now must compete with the rest of the world and are affected by shifts in the global market. Many rural communities in the United States, such as those supported by mining, by farming, by timber industry, or by low-wage and low-skill manufacturing, have been losing their historic job bases. Recruiting manufacturing industries to rural America is becoming increasingly costly. In addition, environmental change and variability, as well as changes in such natural resources as groundwater, on which agriculture and other extractive industries depend, are also adding uncertainty to rural economies and communities.

The conventional approach to rural economic development planning and problem solving relies on government units such as cities and counties. Government units are convenient to use, and data for them are readily available, but they are much less appropriate today than when they were established some 150 years ago. County boundaries were delineated before the automobile era and ensured that residents could reach their county seat and return home in one day's time traveling by horse-drawn buggies or on foot (Shelley and others 1996). Many counties are just too small to have the critical mass of facilities, resources, and people to solve problems and support economic development (Pezzini 2001). Advances in transportation and communication technologies and population loss have made the county even less viable as a unit for economic planning, problem solving, and service delivery. Rural issues are rarely confined to arbitrarily set political boundaries. Tackling them usually requires adjacent counties and communities to work together and use resources that are not available to any one jurisdiction. Intergovernmental cooperation is therefore critical if problem-solving measures are to succeed.

In the conventional political configuration, few effective mechanisms exist for local governments to collaborate. That statistical data for government units are readily available but difficult to obtain for regions that may be appropriate for dealing with various issues in rural economic development further discourages regional thinking and intergovernmental cooperation (Vigier, Driscoll, and Lee-Chuvala 2006). When issues such as infrastructure expansion, environmental and resource management, and tourism development transcend political boundaries they are often dealt with inadequately or left unaddressed.

The need to solve problems is only one reason for adopting regional thinking in rural America, however. While posing challenges, the ongoing socioeconomic and technological changes are also creating opportunities for rural areas to build new economic engines, improve service provision, and maintain and even increase economic viability and quality of life. For example, recent advances in transportation and communication technologies have greatly reduced the restrictive effects of distance. The physical locations of many knowledge-intensive operations, among them accounting, health care, and computer programming, are all but irrelevant. Scale and agglomeration relate not so much to production at a specific location as to the scope and efficiency of the linkages. For firms can now reach both internal and external economies of scale through global networking instead of by pursuing anintegrated, big-push strategy locally (Ward and Hite 1998). Current economic development initiatives at the community level tend to be piecemeal. Local community leaders usually focus on their own community and have relatively limited knowledge about activities in neighboring communities. Such measures may solve short-term problems, but they are unlikely to lead to long-term prosperity.

AD HOC REGIONALISM

Given the limitations of the current configurations of local governments, a logical
reaction is to call for government consolidation and merging. Indeed, scholars and
politicians have frequently made such calls, often based on the potential benefits of
improving efficiency and reducing the cost of service provision (Self 1978; Burger
and Combs 2009; Steineger 2009a, 2009b). Existing political boundaries, particu-
larly county boundaries, however, seem well entrenched in people’s mind. Ballot
initiatives for government consolidation and merging have consistently failed to
pass (Porter and Wallis 2002). For example, scholars and politicians in Kansas have
made several proposals to pare the state’s 105 counties down to 36, 25, or even 13
counties (Self 1978; Kurtz 2009; Steineger 2009a, 2009b). Few people would deny
that Kansas has too many counties, but little support for consolidation exists. So far
only two cases of city-county consolidation have occurred, one between Kansas
City (Kansas) and Wyandotte County and the other between the city of Tribune
and Greeley County in western Kansas. There is no prospect of county consolida-
tion in the near future. Rural areas need alternative, politically feasible models of
regional governance that overcome the weaknesses of the current governing struc-
ture and encourage interjurisdictional collaboration and partnering among com-
munities, the private sector, and government.

An effective and feasible form of regional governance is ad hoc regionalism. It
constitutes voluntary, often grassroots initiatives by interested stakeholders whoseek collaborative, incentive-based measures rather than regulatory solutions to regional challenges. The main feature of ad hoc regionalism is its use of flexibly defined ad hoc regions. Regions are segments of the earth's surface differentiated by various human and physical characteristics; they are intellectual constructs and, as such, do not exist until people recognize them (Wishart 2004). What sets ad hoc regions apart from other types of regions is that local residents create them based on common issues or conditions that need addressing. The boundaries of such regions depend loosely on the perceived areal extent of the issues or challenges, so they often transcend established political boundaries. The key concern is to use the concept to foster regional partnerships and to achieve the critical mass needed to respond to and solve practical regional problems as well as to take advantage of new opportunities (Porter and Wallis 2002). The scale of ad hoc regions may vary substantially, from a portion of a county, to several counties in a state, or even to several states.

Ad hoc regional organizations have existed in urban areas of the United States for decades. Urban planners have long felt the limitations of existing jurisdictional structures in dealing with urban problems such as sprawl, traffic congestion, environmental degradation, and regional disparity, as well as in responding to economic opportunities. Many metropolitan areas have developed an amorphous collection of governance experiments involving interest groups from multiple sectors in loose-knit, collaborative relationships, including councils of governments, regional planning councils, and metropolitan planning organizations (Porter and Wallis 2002; Vigier, Driscoll, and Lee-Chuvala 2006). Decision makers in the public and private sectors as well as in academia recognize their important role in dealing with regional issues (Porter and Wallis 2002). The need for more flexible regional governance has arisen equally if not more urgently in rural areas where political fragmentation, a proclivity for independence, and small populations often lead to political impasse.

Since roughly the early 1990s, development practitioners, advocates, and academics have realized the value of ad hoc regional governance in rural areas, and many regional organizations have emerged across rural America (Porter and Wallis 2002; McDaniel 2003; Fluharty 2004; Hamin and Marcucci 2008). As an indicator of this growing interest, the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy—a nonprofit organization based in Cambridge, Massachusetts—convened a forum in April 2001 to explore issues related to ad hoc regionalism in both urban and rural areas (Porter and Wallis 2002). In 2002 the Center for the Study of Rural America at the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City held a conference on “The New Power of Regions” in dealing with rural problems. A dozen policy experts and 200 or so participants concluded that rural policy needs to promote more regional partnering among rural entrepreneurs, communities, and governments (Drabenstott and Sheaff 2002). They singled out self-defined regions, where unique economic activities were occurring—for example, biopharming in Iowa and broadband development in the Four Corners region of the southwestern United States—as good examples of regionallyfocused activities (Drabenstott and Sheaff 2002; Isserman 2002; Mortensen 2002). The concept of self-defined regions is essentially the same as ad hoc regions discussed in this article. Increasing use of partnerships among public, private, and voluntary sectors is also one feature of the “new rural paradigm” (Ward and Brown 2009).

In 2008, writing about the driving forces behind ad hoc rural regionalism, Hamin and Marcucci divide rural America into three types based on Terry Marsden's 1998 work on rural territories. The first type is “paternalistic countryside,” which refers to profitable agricultural areas such as Iowa, “where large holders and agribusiness prevail in relatively stable landscapes” (Hamin and Marcucci 2008, 470). Remote, isolated rural areas with less viable global-scale agriculture and an often poor, elderly, or Native American population form the second type. Marsden asserted that in those areas government subsidies are critical to the survival of farming and much of the rest of the rural economy and that employment concerns and the welfare of the rural community will dominate local politics (1998). Instead of calling them “clientelistic countryside”—Marsden's term—Hamin and Marcucci prefer “marginal countryside,” with North Dakota being their example. Their third category is “contested countryside,” characterized by relative proximity to metropolitan centers or universities, natural amenity-based recreation, and rapid population growth. Contestation in those areas often arises between competing uses of natural resources and between long-term residents and newcomers.

Hamin and Marcucci assert that ad hoc regionalist efforts are most likely to occur in the contested countryside. This point is debatable. Although intracommunity social conflicts and challenges posed by a rapidly growing population in contested rural areas provide strong motivation for ad hoc regionalism, they are by no means the only driving force. There are equally compelling reasons for regional thinking in the so-called marginal countryside such as the Great Plains and the Four Corners region. Indeed, a number of ad hoc regional organizations have emerged in those areas over the past few decades. My two case studies are good examples, but others exist (McDaniel 2003). Instead of dealing with the pressures caused by rapid population growth and contested resource uses, ad hoc regional organizations in “marginal” areas try to cope with the threats of both population and economic decline or the very process of “marginalization.” Their organizers see the advantages of regional collaboration and partnering in solving regional problems, developing new economic engines, and maintaining a sense of community. The driving forces for ad hoc regional initiatives may differ from case to case, but two common features are that they are all grassroots efforts and that they are usually problem based; that is to say, ad hoc.

## AD HOC REGIONALISM IN ACTION: TWO CASE STUDIES

Two recent examples of ad hoc regionalism in rural America are the WKREDA and the SJF. Both cases are from the so-called marginal countryside, where the need for such regional partnerships is particularly great. Whereas the WKREDA involves mul-tiple counties in a single state—Kansas—the SJF encompassed parts of four states. The two organizations followed different trajectories after their inception, however. By all accounts, the WKREDA is having a very positive impact on economic and community development in its region. The SJF, on the other hand, ceased functioning after a very promising start due to the departure of a key player. Their experiences may offer useful lessons to other rural communities that have implemented or are thinking about implementing similar types of regional corporate governance.

For my case studies I used a number of methods, including field visits, online research, and interviews with key informants to learn about these organizations’ purposes, triumphs, and failures. The fieldwork on the WKREDA and western Kansas took place in four trips during the first half of the 2000s in conjunction with another research project. I first heard about the SJF at the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City’s meeting on the power of regions in 2002 and followed it afterwards. To see the region firsthand, I toured the Four Corners region by automobile in March 2009.

Two important sources of information on both the WKREDA and the SJF are their Web sites.² The WKREDA site contains the organization's bylaws, strategic plans, and fairly complete minutes of the group's annual and committee meetings, so it is particularly informative. The SJF page, linked from the Fort Lewis College Web site, is much briefer, but the report of a Four Corners regional study commissioned by the organization and available on the Web site provides useful background information on the region and its constituent communities (Graves 2003). Other media reports and formal publications on these two organizations appear to be limited. The only two published documents I found are both about the SJF (Anesi, Eppich, and Taylor 2002; CAD 2002). I read all these documents very carefully and many of them multiple times.

To better understand the impetuses for and practices of the two regional organizations, I interviewed ten people for the WKREDA case—all except one from western Kansas—and five for the SJF example between October 2008 and July 2009. Eleven of the interviews were by telephone, three by e-mail, and one in person. The interviewees include two former presidents of the WKREDA, the executive director of the SJF, county economic development practitioners, private-sector employees, and a scholar familiar with the WKREDA. I selected some interviewees because of their involvement in the regional organizations and others to represent different parts of the regions concerned. A number of additional people turned down requests for interviews, usually for lack of knowledge, but they often recommended others, some of whom I interviewed. All interviewees answered five open-ended questions: What issues prompted the regional thinking? What can the WKREDA/SJF do that individual counties/communities cannot? What are the WKREDA's/SJF's major accomplishments so far? How do you deal with the competition among local governments? What lessons have you learned from this regional experience? The discussion below draws information from all of the sources I have described.THE WESTERN KANSAS RURAL ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT ALLIANCE

Like other parts of the Great Plains, western Kansas is a sparsely populated, some-
what isolated agricultural region. It faces a number of challenges to its economic
well-being. First, most of the incorporated places in this region are small farm com-
munities, with Garden City (population 26,658 in 2010), Dodge City (population
27,340), Hays (20,510), and Liberal (20,525) the only four cities with more than 20,000
people (IPSR 2011). Community leaders and residents have long believed that their
small population size and relatively long distance from the state capital—Topeka,
located in the more densely populated eastern part of the state—put them at a dis-
advantage in economic development. They feel that state lawmakers do not always
pay adequate attention to their concerns. Second, this region has experienced vary-
ing trajectories of demographic change in recent decades due to the uneven devel-
opment of both irrigated agriculture and value-added industries (White 1994, 1998;
Williams and Bloomquist 1996). That irrigation plays an important role in sustain-
ing rural population in western Kansas itself raises concern about the long-term
economic sustainability of many communities there. The saturated thickness of the
Ogallala Aquifer that underlies the region and the related usable lifetime of ground-
water vary considerably from place to place (KGS 2000). In some areas conversion to
dryland farming has already been under way. The “planned depletion” of the aqui-
fer has profound implications for western Kansas and, indeed, other parts of the
High Plains region. The future of many communities may well hinge on their abil-
ity to adjust to groundwater depletion and to diversify local economies.

In May 1994 more than eighty-five community leaders and economic develop-
ment practitioners in western Kansas met to discuss their economic problems and
what they needed to do to solve them. They realized that they faced some common
issues and that solving them would require them to pool their resources and work
together. The idea of forming a regional alliance was born at that meeting, and the
WKREDA came into being shortly thereafter. The alliance's objectives are to promote
the exchange of ideas and information among its members, to provide resources to
assist in local and regional development, and, ultimately, to revitalize the region
through cooperative, community, and rural development activities (WKREDA 2010).
The organization formed four committees—business development, government
affairs, education, and public relations—that work independently but report their
activities to the group at its quarterly business meetings.

The WKREDA embodies the ad hoc regional approach in its activities and orga-
nizational structure. It is a grassroots, nonprofit organization initiated by the local
people who share similar concerns about the region's future and wanted to find
solutions in a cooperative way. Participation is voluntary, and membership is open
to economic development entities, government and nongovernmental agencies, and
interested individuals. Flexibility in regional definition is another important fea-
ture of ad hoc regionalism. The WKREDA adjusted its boundaries over time. Initially,
it comprised forty-six counties, but by the end of 2007 it had expanded to fifty-

1). The common concerns about their small populations—hence lack of political clout in the state legislature—and economic decline brought them together and have given meaning to the region.

Promoting intercommunity collaboration and partnership in rural areas requires trust and understanding among the communities. Rural residents generally take pride in their independence, but now they need to learn to work together. In a place with wide-open space, sparsely distributed inhabitants, and long-running high school rivalries, that has not been an easy task. The WKREDA organizers built trust and understanding among alliance's constituents by taking appropriate measures on its organizational structure and business operations. Recognizing the differences between the northern and southern parts of western Kansas, the founders made sure to elect an equal number of representatives from the two subregions to the Board of Directors. That proved very helpful in lessening the intrinsic distrust among neighboring communities.

To encourage information sharing and networking among participating communities, the WKREDA maintains a Web site and a member listserv, publishes a newsletter, and holds quarterly business meetings as well as an annual meeting. It alsoemphasizes networking with state government agencies and other trade organizations that serve similar purposes, such as the Kansas Cavalry (the state's business recruiting organization), the Western Kansas Manufacturing Association (a group of small manufacturers in the same region), and the western Kansas Dairy Coalition (a preexisting dairy trade group). In addition, the WKREDA communicates regularly with the state lawmakers about issues concerning western Kansas. However, to maintain its independence the alliance decided from its inception that it would not accept government funding—one of the reasons, my interviewees claimed, for its success. Membership dues and the pay-to-play fees fund the organization. All of the board members are employees of local governments and utility companies in the region and receive no compensation for their services.

To promote business opportunities in western Kansas, WKREDA delegates have attended many national and international trade shows, such as the World Dairy Expo in Madison, Wisconsin, and Trends 2000, a trade show in California specifically organized for those businesses seeking to relocate outside the state. The alliance requires that anyone on a recruitment mission represent the entire WKREDA region instead of individual communities. Business leads are passed on to all member communities, which are then free to follow up and market their specific communities. Promoting western Kansas as a region may be another factor that has helped to sustain the regional effort. The approach ensures a relatively equal distribution of benefits—or at least opportunities—and is also sensitive to the viability of individual communities (Stabler 1996, 1999). Competition among communities still exists, but rivalry seems to be quickly set aside when the region's well-being is at stake. As one interviewee commented, the “What's in it for me?” mind-set is being replaced by the “What's good for us?” mentality. As an example of this change, when a company contacted Ellis County about building greenhouses there, the county economic development director actually suggested that the company go to a town 23 miles north, in another county, because it had unused greenhouses—which the company did. Another example of this supportive mentality is that when a business is selling out in one place, community leaders are informing their counterparts elsewhere about the availability of equipment that may be too expensive to buy new.

As an ad hoc regional organization the WKREDA provides a number of advantages over individual counties or cities. Every interviewee pointed out its value as a networking conduit for rural communities to share information about new development initiatives and to seek suggestions for dealing with various problems from other communities. The WKREDA's financial resources allow it to send members to conferences, trade shows, and workshops, which individual communities, with their usually small budgets, may not be able to. Finally, working together as a region not only gives western Kansas one voice and much more political clout on regional issues in the state legislature but also opens other doors to small rural communities. For example, they now have access to more funding sources for economic development projects than they would if they were working independently.A major area in which the WKREDA has worked since its inception is the recruitment of large dairy farms as the region's new engine of growth. The decision to focus on dairying was made based on western Kansas's long tradition of animal economy and favorable geographical conditions for dairying—open space, a semiarid climate, groundwater resources, and so forth—as well as on the challenges that dairies were facing in more densely populated parts of the United States. To that end the WKREDA convinced the western Kansas Dairy Coalition to join it and work as a single unit. It has attended dairy trade shows and organized its own annual dairy conferences—later called the “Dairy University”—which have provided investors and dairy farmers elsewhere opportunities to see western Kansas while learning about changes and opportunities in the industry. By early 2009, twenty-one dairy farms had moved to western Kansas with the assistance of the WKREDA, so that the total number of large dairy farms in the region increased to twenty-five (see Figure 1). The region's dairy-herd size had almost doubled since the early 1990s to approximately 90,000 head of dairy cows, making western Kansas an emerging dairying area (Harrington, Lu, and Kromm 2010). Here again the “What's good for us” mind-set comes into play. Not every county can accommodate large dairies because of the land and water resources needed, but people still support this initiative because they believe they can benefit, for example, by providing supplies and veterinary services. In fact, western Kansas is hoping to build an entire industry from dairy cows, veterinarians, supply businesses, milk and cheese processing, and eventually pharmaceutical and nutraceutical uses for parts of milk (protein, fat, sugars, and so forth). In the meantime, the WKREDA is looking beyond dairy. In 2009 it launched a “Western Kansas Jobs” campaign that aims in part “to recruit individuals from outside the area looking for new career or seeking a change in lifestyle, or just looking to relocate” (Depperschmidt 2009).³

The WKREDA's work may have a profound impact on the future well-being of western Kansas. One of the characteristics about globalization is that it has a highly uneven regional effect, for some regions are likely to be privileged and others bypassed. By forming a regional alliance and nurturing a regional identity, communities in western Kansas are essentially “renegotiating” a place in the globalizing economy and preventing global economic forces from playing them off against one another.

THE SAN JUAN FORUM

One major impetus for ad hoc regionalism is the set of disadvantages associated with political fragmentation, such as lack of a critical mass of population and businesses and difficulty in interjurisdictional collaboration. The disadvantages often undermine rural economic development and problem solving. Issues that occur along jurisdictional boundaries present a particular challenge because the affected population may be too small in either jurisdiction to attain the political influence needed. Perhaps no site typifies this situation more than the Four Corners region. Its experience offers valuable lessons about the challenges of running regional organizations.The four state corners have much in common geographically and economically. Linked by the San Juan River, which meanders through the area, all four corners are arid, sparsely populated, geographically isolated due to their long distances from the interstate highway system and from their respective state capitals as well as other major cities. However, political boundaries—literally lines in the sand—and regional rivalries have long hampered communities’ efforts to work together to tackle their common challenges in enhancing economic viability and quality of life. The large number of governments that exist in this area compound the situation. In addition to the four state governments, there are counties, which usually have different sets of regulations, and within counties are town or city councils. The Native American tribal nations in the area, including the Southern Ute, the Jicarilla Apache,the Ute Mountain Ute, and the Navajo, are considered sovereign nations within the United States, all with their own governments, judicial systems, and law enforcement under the authority of the federal government. In addition, federal agencies such as the Bureau of Land Management, the Forest Service, and the National Park Service play important roles in local affairs because the federal government owns about 43.5 percent of the area's land. Because these corner areas all lack political clout, some people have referred to them as the "stepchildren" of their respective states (Anesi, Eppich, and Taylor 2002).

Many people have long realized the need for intergovernmental cooperation in the Four Corners region, and the push has come both from the federal government and the grassroots levels. As an example of the former, the federal government established the Four Corners Regional Planning Commission in 1967 to promote economic revitalization through interstate cooperation. The commission was short-lived because President Richard M. Nixon abolished it in 1974. Moreover, several grassroots regional initiatives have been created. The SJF, formed in January 1991, represents the most recent attempt. The purpose of this nonprofit corporation was to promote "regional information exchange, regional unity and identity, and collective efficiency in the use and development of regional resources" (Anesi, Eppich, and Taylor 2002, 92). The forum focused on breaking down institutional boundaries and barriers to promote partnerships among local governments, federal agencies, and the private sector in an area covering approximately 12,000 square miles. Its organizational structure encouraged "adhocracy" or "a flexible, open system and public-private balance" (p. 93). A Board of Directors governs the forum. David Eppich, then an assistant to the president of Fort Lewis College, played a pivotal role in the SJF. He not only believed firmly that, for sparsely populated places like the Four Corners, regionalism is the way to go but also possessed the vision and leadership to bring the communities together to make it happen. He served as the executive director of the SJF from its inception.

Like the WKREDA, the SJF emphasized the need to facilitate regional networking and collaboration and to pool the resources of communities and existing organizations to address regional issues, particularly those that could not be dealt with adequately by individual communities or organizations. The founders considered communication between different entities "vital for a successful future for all citizens in the region" (Anesi, Eppich, and Taylor 2002, 94; CAD 2002).

The forum focused on bringing various stakeholders together into a common course of action, to nurture the Four Corners as a regional entity, and to achieve economies of scale (Figure 2). As in other parts of the United States, local governments in the Four Corners region compiled economic and demographic data separately. The multitude of entities involved and formats used for data collection made interjurisdictional comparison and regional decision making virtually impossible. The SJF helped to develop a regional database and community snapshot to facilitate cooperative decision making (Graves 2003). The region's two institutions of higher learning—Fort Lewis College, a four-year liberal arts college in Durango, Colorado;and San Juan College, a two-year community college in Farmington, New Mexico—
provided nonpartisan support in education, research, and leadership development
that was important to the regional cooperative endeavors (Anesi, Eppich, and Tay-
lor 2002). Fort Lewis College also housed the SJF.

The forum emphasized regional economic development strategies that would crosscut state, tribal, and county lines and unify the efforts of local governments and other groups. After an extended period of deliberation, the forum identified the regional issues to engage in, among them telecommunications, transportation, value-added and sustainable agriculture, tourism, and health care. The forum, however, served essentially as a regionalism advocate. It did not try to raise funds or secure grants. Existing individuals or agencies carried out specific action strategies (Anesi, Eppich, and Taylor 2002). For example, one of the forum's major accomplishments involved the World Wide Web: It generated interest and rallied the players to create a broadband Internet network for the Four Corners in order to improve the region's telecommunication infrastructure and generate new opportunities for economic development. Another nonprofit regional organization—the San Juan Economic Development Service's Regional Telecommunications Committee—actually coordinated the effort, sought private investment, and found contractors to construct the line. The network that links Albuquerque in New Mexico and Grand Junction in Colorado was completed in November 2000.

The SJF organizers clearly understood the challenges the Four Corners region faced in economic development and had many good ideas. Unfortunately, one area in which they failed to do enough, according to Eppich, was to obtain the broadest possible public support for the regional efforts; in other words, the SJF did not develop a broad-based constituency. Some of my interviewees admitted that the SJF was perceived more or less as Eppich's brainchild. Despite its achievements in the first one and one-half decades of its existence, it stopped functioning shortly after Eppich moved to an administrative position at San Juan College in Farmington, New Mexico, in the spring of 2005.

GOING REGIONAL: LESSONS LEARNED

Economic and demographic changes in rural America both pose challenges and create new opportunities for economic development. In this article I have argued that, in order to tackle various issues effectively and to take advantage of the new opportunities, rural communities need to overcome the limitations of the current political configurations and work together. Ad hoc regionalism provides a politically feasible and effective way for rural communities to collaborate and form partnerships (Porter and Wallis 2002; McDaniel 2003; Fluharty 2004; Hamin and Marcucci 2008). The regional organizations that emerge from this process can also serve as conduits for information sharing and networking, in the process reducing transaction costs for the whole region. Because of the greater political and economic clout it affords a region, ad hoc regionalism may be a way for rural areas, particularly isolated, sparsely populated, and agriculture-dependent areas such as the GreatPlains and the Four Corners region, to mitigate the pernicious effects of economic globalization and, in a way, to “renegotiate” a place in the globalizing economy and prevent the global economic forces from playing rural areas off against one another (Longworth 2008).

Despite its benefits, ad hoc regionalism does not occur easily. Beverly Cigler identified nine preconditions for the emergence of multi community collaborative organizations in small, rural communities, including fiscal stress or perceived stress caused by some disaster occurrence, a political constituency, supportive capacity building, and visible advantages of cooperation for participating governments (1999). Jack Stabler also argued that, to sustain the regional effort, an organization must establish an appropriate governing structure, ensure a relatively equal distribution of benefits, and be sensitive to individual communities' viability (1996). Their points are borne out in the two case studies I presented in this article.

In both western Kansas and the Four Corners region residents perceive rural depopulation and economic decline as a disaster in progress, so the impetus for ad hoc regionalist practices is strong. Differences in the implementation of regionalism, however, led to different outcomes. Between the two, the WKREDA seems to have had a more enduring organizational structure, with a representative Board of Directors and its president serving a one-year term. All of the WKREDA's board members are volunteers who hold other jobs and do not receive compensation for what they do for the group. Promoting the regional alliance is, as one interviewee put it, “just a labor of love.” The SJF also had a board, but unlike the WKREDA, it also had an executive director and a full-time, paid employee to run the organization. Securing funds for the position became a challenge.

A major challenge for regionalism is that “region” is “a scale of economic organization and change that lacks a compelling political category” (Hicks 1982, 260). It takes a concerted effort by the communities involved to set their rivalries aside and collaborate for their common good. The vision and strong leadership of a few key personnel is often crucial in this process, particularly in its early stages, but organizational governance must move beyond these key players. Regionalism cannot survive and, indeed, may be resisted if the citizens do not buy into the idea and see its benefits (Hall and Stern 2009). From the outset, the WKREDA organizers made substantial efforts to eliminate distrust among communities and to bring people together. They decided that the traditionally divided northern and southern parts of the region should be represented equally on the organization's Board of Directors. Their decision helped to dispel the misgivings that some communities, particularly small ones, had about their roles in the alliance and to build mutual trust among communities. The group's decision to market western Kansas as a region instead of individual communities in its business-recruitment activities further lessened distrust. The SJF, on the other hand, failed to change the perception that it was one person's brainchild and to garner support from its constituents, who were scattered in four states.

Furthermore, to justify their investments in time and money, members of a regional organization must see benefits. Here too the WKREDA may have been moresuccessful than the SJF. The WKREDA actively provides informational and financial assistance to its constituents in their economic development endeavors. It also pools resources in business recruitment, with impressive successes in attracting large dairies to move to western Kansas. Residents of the region appear to appreciate the fact that, with a relatively small investment, their communities can benefit from a social and educational/training structure, and they have supported it. The SJF faced a much more complex political situation and did not do enough to nurture a supportive constituency. It acted mainly as an advocate of regional thinking, with no attempts to recruit businesses to the region. The SJF had some successes, especially in its effort to bring together the right players for the creation of a broadband Internet network, but its benefits were more intangible.

Challenges notwithstanding, the experiences of the WKREDA and other successful examples of rural ad hoc regionalism show that the regional form of governance can work effectively in dealing with various rural issues and taking advantage of new economic opportunities despite the problem Hicks pointed out in 1982: the absence of both legal borders and a political constituency. Implemented appropriately, such grassroots, informal, flexible organizations can complement the existing political structure and play an important role in rural economic development.
